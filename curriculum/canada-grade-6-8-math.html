Canada Grade 6–8 Math Curriculum Guide

Canadian students study within provincial and territorial education systems. There is no single national mathematics curriculum for every school. Learnora provides international Grade 6–8 practice and this guide helps families identify the right local curriculum context.

How school curriculum works in Canada

Responsibility for education rests with Canada's ten provinces and three territories. Each jurisdiction sets its own curriculum, grade expectations and implementation details. Grade labels may look similar across the country, but topic sequencing, terminology and assessment can differ.

Ontario mathematics

Ontario publishes an elementary mathematics curriculum for Grades 1–8, with learning across number, algebra, data, spatial sense, financial literacy and mathematical processes. For Ontario-specific guidance, see Learnora's Ontario mathematics guide and the official provincial Grade 6, Grade 7 and Grade 8 expectations.

Other provinces and territories

Students in British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Quebec, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut should consult their own education authority. Do not assume that an Ontario curriculum page or another province's grade sequence applies locally.

What most provinces have in common

Although each province and territory writes its own curriculum, the middle-school mathematics taught across Canada shares a recognisable shape. Most provinces organise learning into strands such as Number; Patterns and Relations (or Algebra); Shape and Space (or Geometry and Measurement); and Statistics and Probability. Students are expected not only to compute, but to communicate their thinking, connect ideas, use mental mathematics and estimation, solve problems, use reasoning and use technology appropriately. These processes appear in provincial documents under different names, but the expectation is the same everywhere: an answer on its own is not enough — the learner should be able to explain how they got there.

Another shared feature is that number work deepens gradually. Grade 6 consolidates fractions, decimals and percentages and introduces ratios and integers. Grade 7 extends arithmetic to all rational numbers, including negatives, and makes proportional reasoning central. Grade 8 introduces exponents, square roots, linear relations and the Pythagorean relationship. Because the sequence is similar even when the wording differs, a Learnora lesson matched to the topic — rather than to a province's exact grade label — is usually the right starting point, and the teacher's plan decides the depth.

Ontario — Grades 6–8

Ontario publishes an elementary mathematics curriculum for Grades 1–8, organised into the strands Number, Algebra, Data, Spatial Sense, Financial Literacy and Social-Emotional Learning Skills in Mathematics and the Mathematical Processes. It includes coding expectations alongside algebra, and financial literacy is a strand in its own right, so budgeting, interest and unit prices are taught as mathematics, not only as life skills.

For Ontario-specific guidance, see Learnora's Ontario mathematics guide. For practice, use fractions, percentages and rational numbers for the Number strand; two-step equations and linear functions for Algebra; data and graphs and statistics for Data; angles and right triangles for Spatial Sense; and budgeting, savings and interest and unit prices for Financial Literacy.

British Columbia — Grades 6–8

British Columbia's curriculum is built around Big Ideas, curricular competencies and content, with an emphasis on reasoning, communicating and connecting mathematics to real situations. Number, patterns, algebra, geometry, measurement and data appear in every grade, and students are expected to use concrete materials, pictures and symbols flexibly rather than jumping straight to procedures.

Match by topic: equivalent fractions and comparing fractions for number sense; ratios and rates for proportional thinking; algebraic expressions and equations and inequalities for patterns and algebra; perimeter and area and surface area and volume for geometry and measurement; and data and graphs for data. Check the BC curriculum site and the learner's school plan for the current grade-level expectations.

Alberta and the Prairies — Grades 6–8

Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba have historically shared a common framework for mathematics, organised into Number; Patterns and Relations; Shape and Space; and Statistics and Probability, with mathematical processes — communication, connections, mental mathematics and estimation, problem solving, reasoning, technology and visualization — running through all of them. Alberta has been updating its curriculum in recent years, so the exact wording and sequencing for a given grade should always be checked against the current provincial programme of studies.

For practice, use factors, multiples, HCF and LCM and integers for Number; order of operations and simplifying expressions for Patterns and Relations; angles and scale drawings and transformations and symmetry for Shape and Space; and statistics and probability and scatter plots for Statistics and Probability.

Quebec — Grades 6–8 (Cycle 3 and Secondary I–II)

Quebec's system is structured differently from the rest of Canada: elementary school runs through Grade 6, organised in cycles, and secondary school begins in Secondary I. The Progression of Learning sets out the knowledge students are expected to acquire in mathematics, covering arithmetic, algebra, geometry, measurement, statistics and probability. Much of the official documentation is in French, and the language of instruction in most Quebec schools is French, so Learnora's English lessons are best used as supplementary practice for families who want English-language explanations of the same ideas — for example, an English-speaking family in Quebec, or a learner studying in English.

Match by topic: multiplying and dividing fractions and multiplying and dividing decimals for arithmetic; one-step equations and linear equations for algebra; volume of 3D shapes and metric conversions for measurement; and probability models for chance. Always follow the teacher's French terminology in class; Learnora is extra practice, not a replacement for the school's programme.

Atlantic Canada — Grades 6–8

New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador each publish their own curricula, but the Atlantic provinces have a history of working from a shared mathematics framework with the same four strands as the western provinces: Number; Patterns and Relations; Shape and Space; and Statistics and Probability. The emphasis is on number sense, mental mathematics, estimation and problem solving before formal procedures.

For practice, use decimals and decimal operations for Number; ratios and proportional relationships for Patterns and Relations; perimeter and area and angle relationships for Shape and Space; and data and graphs and probability for Statistics and Probability. The territories — Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut — generally follow the curriculum of a partner province, so check which one applies locally.

Grade by grade: what to practise on Learnora

Grade 6 — fractions, decimals, ratios, first algebra, geometry

Grade 6 is the year number sense becomes flexible. Students divide fractions by fractions, work fluently with multi-digit decimals, and learn that a ratio compares two quantities while a rate compares quantities with different units. They write and evaluate expressions, solve one-step equations and begin plotting points in the coordinate plane.

Start with understanding fractions, equivalent fractions and mixed numbers, then understanding decimals and understanding percentages. Ratios are covered in introduction to ratios and rates and proportions. For algebra, try order of operations, algebraic expressions and solving one-step equations. Geometry practice is in perimeter and area, volume of 3D shapes and angles and triangles, and data in data and graphs.

Grade 7 — proportional reasoning, rational numbers, equations

Grade 7 turns Grade 6 foundations into tools. Proportional relationships become the central idea: students decide whether two quantities are proportional, find unit rates, and solve percent problems including tax, tips and discounts. They add, subtract, multiply and divide rational numbers — including negative fractions and decimals — and solve multi-step equations and inequalities.

Work through ratios, rates and proportional relationships and fractions, decimals and percentages in problem solving. Rational numbers and operations and adding and subtracting integers cover signed-number arithmetic. For algebra, use two-step equations, equations and inequalities and simplifying algebraic expressions. Geometry is in angles and scale drawings, area, surface area and volume and transformations and symmetry; data and chance are in statistics, probability and data interpretation and coordinate geometry.

Grade 8 — exponents, linear relations, geometry, data

Grade 8 is where algebra becomes a system. Students work with irrational numbers and square roots, use integer exponents and scientific notation, and solve linear equations — including equations with variables on both sides — and pairs of equations. Functions appear as a new idea: a rule that assigns exactly one output to each input, shown as a table, a graph or an equation.

Begin with exponents, powers and scientific notation and square roots and irrational numbers. Then linear equations and inequalities, linear functions and graphs and systems of linear equations. Geometry practice is in angle relationships and parallel lines, the Pythagorean Theorem, transformations, congruence and similarity and volume of cylinders, cones and spheres. For data, use scatter plots and data relationships and probability models and compound events.

A simple weekly practice routine

Short, regular practice beats occasional long sessions. A workable routine is three sessions of about 25 minutes a week: one session on the topic being studied at school right now, one session revisiting a topic from a few weeks ago, and one session on a weak spot found by a quiz. In each session, read the explanation, follow the worked examples without skipping steps, then try the practice questions before looking at the answers. If a question is wrong, the useful work is finding the exact step where the reasoning went off track — not just copying the correct answer. This routine works in every province because it follows the learner's own school sequence instead of imposing a different one.

Mistakes worth watching for

A few errors appear again and again across Grades 6–8, and naming them in advance helps learners catch themselves. The most common is treating a fraction bar as two separate numbers instead of one value — so 1/2 + 1/3 becomes 2/5. Another is forgetting that a negative number gets smaller as its size grows: −8 is less than −3. In algebra, the classic slip is doing an operation to only one side of an equation. And with percentages, learners often confuse "increased by 20%" with "20% of the original" — the first is a new total, the second is only the change. When a learner makes one of these mistakes, ask them to explain their step out loud; the error usually becomes obvious to them halfway through the sentence.

Study tips for families

Ask which unit the learner is studying at school. Read the matching explanation, complete a worked example together, then let the learner attempt the questions independently. Check the answer and the method, not only the final number. If a term or method differs from class, follow the teacher's approach and use Learnora as extra practice.
