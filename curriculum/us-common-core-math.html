US Common Core Mathematics: Grades 6–8

Use this guide to navigate Learnora's supplementary middle-school maths practice alongside the Common Core State Standards for Mathematics. The standards describe mathematical content and practices; they are not a single national lesson plan or a replacement for local school requirements.

What students study across Grades 6–8

  • Grade 6: ratios and rates, fractions and decimals, expressions and equations, geometry, and statistics.
  • Grade 7: proportional relationships, rational-number operations, expressions and equations, geometry, probability and statistics.
  • Grade 8: linear equations and functions, systems, transformations, the Pythagorean Theorem, exponents and bivariate data.

Practise related topics on Learnora

  • Fractions, decimals, percentages and ratios.
  • Grade 7 ratios and proportional relationships, two-step equations and statistics and probability.
  • Grade 8 linear equations, Pythagorean Theorem, linear functions and exponents.

How the Common Core is organised

The standards are grouped by grade, and inside each grade by broad areas called domains. A domain such as "Ratios and Proportional Relationships" contains several individual standards, and each standard describes one specific piece of understanding or skill. The standards say what students should be able to do; they do not prescribe a textbook, a daily lesson plan or the order in which a school teaches the topics. That is why two schools can both follow the Common Core and still teach fractions in different months.

Three ideas run through the whole middle-school sequence. First, number work grows outward: whole numbers and fractions in Grade 6, rational numbers in Grade 7, and irrational numbers in Grade 8. Second, algebra grows from expressions and one-step equations to systems of equations and functions. Third, geometry and data keep returning with harder questions each year — an angle fact learned in Grade 7 becomes a tool for proving things about triangles in Grade 8. A learner who sees these threads will find each new topic less surprising.

The eight Standards for Mathematical Practice

Beside the content standards, the Common Core lists eight practices that describe how good mathematicians work. They apply at every grade, and they are worth teaching explicitly rather than hoping they appear on their own:

  1. Make sense of problems and persevere. Read the question twice, restate it in your own words, and keep going when the first attempt fails.
  2. Reason abstractly and quantitatively. Move between the numbers and what they stand for — 3/4 is a calculation, but it is also three quarters of a pizza or of a budget.
  3. Construct arguments and critique reasoning. Explain why an answer is right, and spot the flaw in a wrong method instead of only marking it wrong.
  4. Model with mathematics. Turn a real situation — a discount, a journey, a recipe — into numbers, a diagram or an equation, then check the answer against the situation.
  5. Use tools strategically. Choose the right tool for the job: mental maths, a sketch, a table, a calculator or a graph.
  6. Attend to precision. Use the right words, the right units and the right level of accuracy, and label every answer.
  7. Look for structure. Notice that 3x + 3y is 3(x + y), or that a ratio table repeats the same multiplication — structure turns hard problems into easy ones.
  8. Look for regularity in repeated reasoning. When the same calculation keeps appearing, find the shortcut and say why it always works.

Learnora lessons support these habits directly: worked examples show the reasoning, not just the answer; practice questions ask learners to explain a step; and quizzes include error-spotting questions, where the job is to find and fix a mistake in someone else's working.

Grade by grade: what to practise on Learnora

Grade 6 — ratios, the number system, expressions, geometry, statistics

Grade 6 is the year number sense becomes flexible. Students divide fractions by fractions, work fluently with multi-digit decimals, and learn that a ratio compares two quantities while a rate compares quantities with different units. They write and evaluate expressions with whole-number exponents, solve one-step equations, and begin plotting points in the coordinate plane.

Start with understanding fractions and equivalent fractions, then adding and subtracting fractions and multiplying and dividing fractions. For decimals, work through understanding decimals, adding and subtracting decimals and multiplying and dividing decimals. Ratios are covered in introduction to ratios and rates and proportions, and percentages in understanding percentages and percentage increase and decrease. For algebra, try order of operations, algebraic expressions and solving one-step equations. Geometry practice is in perimeter and area, volume of 3D shapes, metric units and conversions and angles and triangles, and data in data and graphs.

Grade 7 — proportional relationships, rational numbers, expressions, geometry, probability

Grade 7 turns the Grade 6 foundations into tools. Proportional relationships become the central idea: students decide whether two quantities are proportional, find unit rates, and solve percent problems including tax, tips and discounts. They add, subtract, multiply and divide rational numbers — including negative fractions and decimals — and solve multi-step equations and inequalities.

Work through ratios, rates and proportional relationships and fractions, decimals and percentages in problem solving for the percent work. Rational numbers and operations and adding and subtracting integers cover signed-number arithmetic. For algebra, use two-step equations, equations and inequalities and simplifying algebraic expressions. Geometry is in angles and scale drawings, area, surface area and volume and transformations and symmetry; data and chance are in statistics, probability and data interpretation and coordinate geometry.

Grade 8 — the number system, expressions and equations, functions, geometry, statistics

Grade 8 is where algebra becomes a system. Students work with irrational numbers and square roots, use integer exponents and scientific notation, and solve linear equations — including equations with variables on both sides — and pairs of equations. Functions appear as a new idea: a rule that assigns exactly one output to each input, shown as a table, a graph or an equation.

Begin with exponents, powers and scientific notation and square roots and irrational numbers. Then linear equations and inequalities, linear functions and graphs and systems of linear equations. Geometry practice is in angle relationships and parallel lines, the Pythagorean theorem and right triangles, transformations, congruence and similarity and volume of cylinders, cones and spheres. For data, use scatter plots and data relationships and probability models and compound events.

A simple weekly practice routine

Short, regular practice beats occasional long sessions. A workable routine is three sessions of about 25 minutes a week: one session on the topic the learner is studying at school right now, one session revisiting a topic from a few weeks ago, and one session on a weak spot found by a quiz. In each session, read the explanation, follow the worked examples without skipping steps, then try the practice questions before looking at the answers. If a question is wrong, the useful work is finding the exact step where the reasoning went off track — not just copying the correct answer.

Mistakes worth watching for

A few errors appear again and again across Grades 6–8, and naming them in advance helps learners catch themselves. The most common is treating a fraction bar as two separate numbers instead of one value — so 1/2 + 1/3 becomes 2/5. Another is forgetting that a negative number gets smaller as its size grows: −8 is less than −3. In algebra, the classic slip is doing an operation to only one side of an equation. And with percentages, learners often confuse "increased by 20%" with "20% of the original" — the first is a new total, the second is only the change. When a learner makes one of these mistakes, ask them to explain their step out loud; the error usually becomes obvious to them halfway through the sentence.

Mathematical practices matter too

Do not study a topic only as a list of procedures. Encourage learners to make sense of problems, explain their reasoning, model real situations, use tools strategically, attend to precision, identify structure and look for repeated reasoning. Ask “why does this work?” as well as “what is the answer?”

How to check local fit
