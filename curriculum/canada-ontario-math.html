Ontario Mathematics: Grades 6–8

Canada does not have one single national school curriculum: provinces and territories set their own education programmes. This guide focuses specifically on Ontario and its Mathematics (2020) curriculum for Grades 1–8.

Ontario's mathematics framework

Ontario's current elementary mathematics policy says that Grades 1–8 programmes are based on the Mathematics (2020) expectations. The framework includes mathematical processes and learning across number, algebra, data, spatial sense and financial literacy. Expectations and examples vary by grade, so check the grade-specific official pages.

Grade by grade: what Ontario expects

Ontario's expectations are written grade by grade across six areas. The first area — social-emotional learning skills and mathematical processes — runs through everything else. The other five areas are Number, Algebra, Data, Spatial Sense and Financial Literacy. A topic is not "covered" by reading about it once; the expectation is that learners can use it, explain it and apply it to a new problem.

Grade 6 — whole numbers, fractions, patterns, measurement

Grade 6 builds confident whole-number and fraction work: reading, representing and ordering fractions and decimals, adding and subtracting fractions with different denominators, and using ratios and rates in simple contexts. Algebra begins with patterns — describing a pattern rule, extending it and representing it with a variable. Spatial Sense covers angles, triangles and the area and volume of simple shapes, and Data introduces reading and making graphs and describing data with measures such as the mean and median.

Useful Learnora practice: understanding fractions, equivalent fractions, comparing and ordering fractions, adding and subtracting fractions, understanding decimals, adding and subtracting decimals, understanding percentages, introduction to ratios, algebraic expressions, solving one-step equations, order of operations, perimeter and area of 2D shapes, volume of 3D shapes, metric units and conversions, angles and triangles, data and graphs and factors, multiples, HCF and LCM.

Grade 7 — rational numbers, proportional thinking, equations, geometry

Grade 7 extends number work to integers and rational numbers, including adding and subtracting with negative values, and deepens proportional reasoning: unit rates, percent problems and scale. Algebra grows to two-step equations and inequalities, and learners begin to graph relationships. Spatial Sense adds surface area and volume of prisms, angle relationships and scale drawings, while Data adds sampling, probability and interpreting graphs critically — including spotting misleading graphs.

Useful Learnora practice: adding and subtracting integers, rational numbers and operations, ratios, rates and proportional relationships, fractions, decimals and percentages in problem solving, two-step equations, equations and inequalities, simplifying algebraic expressions, angles and scale drawings, area, surface area and volume, transformations and symmetry, coordinate geometry and statistics, probability and data interpretation.

Grade 8 — exponents, linear relations, Pythagoras, data relationships

Grade 8 is the bridge to high school. Number work adds powers, square roots and irrational numbers, and scientific notation for very large and very small numbers. Algebra becomes linear relations: solving linear equations, working with systems, and reading slope and intercepts from graphs. Spatial Sense adds the Pythagorean theorem, transformations with congruence and similarity, and the volume of cylinders, cones and spheres. Data work moves to scatter plots, correlation and probability models for combined events.

Useful Learnora practice: exponents, powers and scientific notation, square roots and irrational numbers, linear equations and inequalities, linear functions and graphs, systems of linear equations, the Pythagorean theorem and right triangles, angle relationships and parallel lines, transformations, congruence and similarity, volume of cylinders, cones and spheres, scatter plots and data relationships and probability models and compound events.

Financial literacy: a strand of its own

Ontario is unusual in giving financial literacy its own strand from Grade 1, and it grows steadily through Grades 6–8. Learners compare unit prices, plan a simple budget, understand interest on savings, and weigh the cost of borrowing. The point is not to memorise definitions but to make better decisions with real numbers: is the larger pack actually cheaper per gram? If you save a fixed amount each month, how much interest do you earn in a year? What does a sale price really save you?

Learnora's financial literacy lessons practise exactly these questions: budgeting (planning income and spending), savings and interest (how interest grows savings over time) and unit prices (comparing the true cost of different pack sizes). The budget calculator tool lets learners test their own numbers. These lessons are supplementary practice for the strand, not a full financial advice course — and they deliberately avoid trading, investing or speculation, which are not part of the Grades 6–8 curriculum.

Mathematical processes: how Ontario expects students to think

Ontario's curriculum names seven mathematical processes, and teachers assess them alongside the content. Problem solving means choosing a strategy rather than waiting to be told one. Reasoning and proving means explaining why an answer must be right. Reflecting means checking your own work and asking whether the answer makes sense. Selecting tools and computational strategies means deciding when to calculate mentally, when to draw a diagram and when to use a calculator. Connecting means seeing that fractions, decimals and percentages are the same idea in different clothes. Representing means showing the same idea as a number, a picture, a table and a graph. Communicating means writing the reasoning so someone else can follow it. A learner who can do the calculation but cannot explain it has learned only half the expectation — so when you practise together, ask "how do you know?" as often as "what is the answer?"

A weekly practice routine

Three short sessions a week work better than one long one. Use the first session for the topic being taught at school right now, the second to revisit a topic from two or three weeks ago, and the third for a weak spot a quiz has revealed. In every session, read the explanation, follow the worked examples step by step, then attempt the practice questions before checking the answers. End each session by writing down one sentence: what did I learn, and what is still shaky? That sentence is the plan for the next session, and over a term it becomes a record of real progress.

When your child gets stuck

Getting stuck is normal and useful — it shows exactly where the understanding stops. The most helpful response is to go one step back, not to push forward. If two-step equations are failing, check one-step equations. If fraction division is failing, check what a fraction means. Ask the learner to explain the problem in their own words, and to point at the exact step where they stop knowing what to do. That step is the lesson to revisit. Avoid the temptation to simply show the answer: a copied answer feels like progress for a day and vanishes by the test. And remember that the official Ontario expectations page for the exact grade is the authority — if a Learnora lesson and the school disagree about sequence or emphasis, follow the school.

Ontario's six areas of study

The Ontario Mathematics (2020) curriculum is organised around six areas. The first area focuses on social-emotional learning skills and mathematical processes; the remaining areas cover Number, Algebra, Data, Spatial Sense and Financial Literacy. The expectations are described by grade, so a Grade 6 activity should be checked against the Grade 6 page rather than assumed to fit Grade 7 or Grade 8.

Number and algebra

Number work develops flexible understanding of fractions, decimals, percentages and operations. Algebra helps learners recognise patterns, represent relationships and solve problems with variables. Related Learnora practice includes decimal place value, percentages, algebraic expressions and linear equations.

Data, spatial sense and financial literacy

Data work supports interpreting and communicating information. Spatial Sense connects geometry, measurement and visual reasoning. Financial Literacy helps learners apply mathematics to money decisions and everyday contexts. Learnora has related data and graphs, area and volume and geometry lessons; financial literacy coverage is still developing and should not be considered complete.

Canada is not one curriculum

Education is a provincial and territorial responsibility. Ontario's Mathematics (2020) policy is not automatically the curriculum for a learner in Alberta, British Columbia, Quebec or another province or territory. For learners outside Ontario, use the local ministry or education department's current programme and treat these Learnora links only as general supplementary practice.

How families can use the guide

Choose the learner's exact grade, open the official Ontario expectations and compare the topic before assigning practice. Ask learners to explain their method, show working and check answers with a second method. A Learnora quiz can help identify a topic to revisit, but it does not replace classroom assessment or the complete Ontario curriculum.

Scope: This page is Ontario-specific, not a Canada-wide curriculum claim. Families in other provinces and territories should consult their own education authority. Learnora is supplementary and is not an Ontario Ministry resource.
