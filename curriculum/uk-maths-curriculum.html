UK Maths Curriculum Guide: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

The UK does not use one identical school curriculum across all four nations. This guide explains the main differences in mathematics terminology and helps learners find related Learnora practice without treating international grade labels as exact equivalents.

England: Key Stage 3

In England, Key Stage 3 covers Years 7–9. The mathematics programme of study includes number, algebra, ratio and proportion, geometry and measures, probability, and statistics. Use Learnora's England KS3 guide for topic links, while checking the learner's school sequence.

Scotland: Curriculum for Excellence

Scotland uses Curriculum for Excellence. The Broad General Education runs from early years to the end of S3 and includes five curriculum levels: Early, First, Second, Third and Fourth. Numeracy and mathematics is one of the curriculum areas. Scottish stage and level labels should not be mapped directly to England's year groups or Learnora's Grade 6–8 labels.

Wales: Curriculum for Wales

Wales organises learning through Areas of Learning and Experience, including Mathematics and Numeracy. The official guidance describes learning progression and supports schools in designing their own curriculum. Use the descriptions of learning and your school's curriculum plan to choose relevant practice; do not assume a direct year-by-year match with England.

Northern Ireland: Key Stage 3

In Northern Ireland, Key Stage 3 is part of post-primary education and covers Years 8, 9 and 10, typically ages 11–14. CCEA provides curriculum and assessment guidance for this system. These year labels differ from England's KS3 Years 7–9.

How the four systems differ

Education is devolved in the UK, so each nation runs its own school system and its own curriculum. England and Northern Ireland both use key stages, but their year labels differ. Scotland uses Curriculum for Excellence with levels rather than key stages. Wales uses the Curriculum for Wales, built around Areas of Learning and Experience and progression steps rather than year-by-year programmes of study. The maths itself is largely the same — fractions, algebra, geometry and statistics appear everywhere — but the order, the depth, the terminology and the assessments are not identical. That is why this guide treats the four systems separately instead of pretending one map fits all four.

One practical consequence is that age and year labels do not line up neatly. A learner aged 12 might be in Year 7 in England, S1 in Scotland, Year 8 in Wales or Year 8 in Northern Ireland. Learnora's Grade 6–8 labels are international, not UK year labels, so the right way to use this guide is to match the topic being studied at school, not the grade number. If the class is working on linear equations, use the linear equations lesson whatever the year label says.

England — Key Stage 3, Years 7–9

England's national curriculum sets a programme of study for Key Stage 3 mathematics covering number, algebra, ratio and proportion, geometry and measures, probability, and statistics. Students move from fluency with fractions, decimals and percentages to formal algebra: simplifying expressions, solving linear equations, working with sequences and graphs. Geometry covers angles, properties of shapes, transformations and the Pythagorean relationship, while statistics covers averages, charts and probability.

Use Learnora's England KS3 guide for the full topic-by-topic map. For the core topics, start with fractions, decimals and percentages, then ratios and proportional relationships and two-step equations. For geometry, use angles and triangles, angle relationships and the Pythagorean Theorem; for data, data and graphs and statistics and probability.

Scotland — Curriculum for Excellence, Broad General Education to S3

Scotland's Curriculum for Excellence organises learning into five levels — Early, First, Second, Third and Fourth — and the Broad General Education runs from the early years to the end of S3. Numeracy and mathematics is one of the eight curriculum areas, and it is taught alongside numeracy across learning, which means number skills are expected to be used in other subjects too. Learners in the Third and Fourth levels, roughly the middle-school years, work on number processes, algebra, geometry, data analysis and chance, with a strong emphasis on applying mathematics to real-life contexts and explaining reasoning.

Scottish levels should not be mapped directly to England's year groups or to Learnora's Grade 6–8 labels. Instead, match by topic: integers and rational numbers for number processes; algebraic expressions, two-step equations and linear functions for algebra; angles and scale drawings and area and volume for geometry; and scatter plots and probability models for data and chance. In Scotland, learners are also expected to talk about their thinking, so after each practice question it helps to ask "how do you know that?" rather than only checking the answer.

Wales — Curriculum for Wales, Mathematics and Numeracy

Wales organises learning through six Areas of Learning and Experience, one of which is Mathematics and Numeracy. Instead of prescribing topics year by year, the Curriculum for Wales describes progression: learners develop number, algebra, geometry, measures, statistics and probability in increasing depth, guided by descriptions of learning at each progression step. Schools design their own curriculum within that framework, so two Welsh schools can teach the same ideas in a different order.

Because sequencing varies, the practical approach is to find the topic on the school's plan and match it to a Learnora lesson: fractions and decimals for number; ratios and proportional relationships for ratio; one-step equations and linear equations for algebra; perimeter and area and volume for measures; and statistics and probability for data. Financial literacy is part of the Welsh framework too, so budgeting and savings and interest make useful real-life practice.

Northern Ireland — Key Stage 3, Years 8–10

In Northern Ireland, Key Stage 3 is part of post-primary education and covers Years 8, 9 and 10, typically ages 11–14. CCEA provides curriculum and assessment guidance for this system. The mathematics content is familiar — number, algebra, measures, shape and space, and handling data — but the year labels differ from England's KS3 Years 7–9, so an English Year 8 topic may appear in a Northern Ireland Year 9 classroom.

Match by topic, not by year: fractions, percentages and ratios for number; algebraic expressions, simplifying expressions and systems of equations for algebra; angles, transformations and congruence and similarity for shape and space; and data and graphs, scatter plots and probability for handling data. Check the learner's school plan and CCEA guidance before treating any lesson as a direct match for an assessed topic.

Terminology to watch for

British and international maths vocabulary differ in small ways that can confuse a learner who meets both. In the UK, the gradient of a line is what many international resources call the slope; scientific notation is usually called standard form; a system of equations is often called simultaneous equations; and "brackets" is used where some resources say "parentheses". A "sequence" question may ask for the nth term, meaning a formula for the term in position n. None of these change the mathematics, but a learner who has only met one word for an idea can freeze on a question that uses the other. When that happens, translate the word first, then solve the problem — the difficulty was vocabulary, not maths.

A simple weekly practice routine

Short, regular practice beats occasional long sessions. A workable routine is three sessions of about 25 minutes a week: one session on the topic being studied at school right now, one session revisiting a topic from a few weeks ago, and one session on a weak spot found by a quiz. In each session, read the explanation, follow the worked examples without skipping steps, then try the practice questions before looking at the answers. If a question is wrong, the useful work is finding the exact step where the reasoning went off track — not just copying the correct answer. This routine works in all four UK systems because it follows the learner's own school sequence instead of imposing a different one.

Mistakes worth watching for

A few errors appear again and again across the middle-school years, and naming them in advance helps learners catch themselves. The most common is treating a fraction bar as two separate numbers instead of one value — so 1/2 + 1/3 becomes 2/5. Another is forgetting that a negative number gets smaller as its size grows: −8 is less than −3. In algebra, the classic slip is doing an operation to only one side of an equation, or forgetting to multiply every term inside brackets. With percentages, learners often confuse "increased by 20%" with "20% of the original" — the first is a new total, the second is only the change. In geometry, a frequent error is using the wrong unit: giving an area in centimetres instead of square centimetres. When a learner makes one of these mistakes, ask them to explain their step out loud; the error usually becomes obvious to them halfway through the sentence.

How to use Learnora across the UK

Learnora organises its core maths lessons by international Grades 6–8. These are supplementary topic resources, not an official UK-wide mapping. Choose the lesson that matches the topic currently being studied, compare it with the appropriate national curriculum and ask the learner's teacher about the expected depth and terminology. Topics include fractions, decimals, percentages, ratios, algebra, geometry, statistics and probability.
