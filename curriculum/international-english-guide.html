International curriculum overview · English

English Across the USA, England, Ontario and Australia

A starting point for families and learners using English resources in middle school. Compare selected official frameworks, then choose extra reading, writing, vocabulary and grammar practice.

Important: Learnora is an independent supplementary resource, not a school, government curriculum provider or examination board. This is an orientation map, not a standards crosswalk or guarantee that a lesson meets every local expectation. School sequences and adopted frameworks can differ.

What strong middle-school English study includes

  • Reading: understand literary and informational texts, identify central ideas, analyse structure and support interpretations with evidence.
  • Writing: plan, draft, organise, revise and edit informative, narrative and argumentative texts for a purpose and audience.
  • Language: use grammar, sentence structure, punctuation, spelling and vocabulary precisely.
  • Speaking and listening: explain ideas, listen actively, ask useful questions and present reasoning.
  • Literature and media: explore how language, form, viewpoint and context shape meaning.

A practical study pathway

  1. Sentence foundations: review parts of speech, sentence types, clauses and subject–verb agreement.
  2. Conventions: practise verb tenses, punctuation, capitalisation and vocabulary in context.
  3. Active reading: identify main ideas, annotate details, make careful inferences and cite textual evidence.
  4. Purposeful writing: create a focused paragraph with a topic sentence, relevant evidence, transitions and a conclusion.
  5. Review and edit: read work aloud and check sentence boundaries, clarity and accuracy.

United States — Common Core ELA, Grades 6–8

Common Core English Language Arts for Grades 6–8 is organised into Reading, Writing, Speaking and Listening, and Language. Reading covers literature and informational text: students identify central ideas, analyse how a text is structured, and support interpretations with evidence from the text. Writing covers three text types — argument, informative/explanatory and narrative — and expects students to plan, draft, revise and edit. The Language strand covers grammar, vocabulary and conventions, including how word choice and sentence structure affect meaning.

For practice, use reading comprehension and text structure and author's purpose for reading; paragraph writing and argument writing for writing; and parts of speech, sentence types and structure, verb tenses and agreement, punctuation and capitalization and vocabulary skills for language. Remember that Common Core is adopted and implemented by individual states, so check the school's state standards and district guidance before treating any lesson as a required sequence.

England — Key Stage 3 English, Years 7–9

England's national curriculum for Key Stage 3 English covers reading, writing, grammar and vocabulary, and spoken English. Reading includes a wide range of fiction and non-fiction, including pre-1914 literature, contemporary texts and texts from other cultures; students are expected to analyse language, structure and the writer's choices, and to make comparisons between texts. Writing includes writing for a range of purposes and audiences, with attention to grammar, vocabulary and the whole text, and spoken English includes discussion, presentation and drama.

For practice, use reading comprehension to build evidence-based analysis, text structure and author's purpose for comparing how texts are built, and vocabulary skills for word choice. For writing, work through paragraph writing and argument writing, and use sentence types and structure and punctuation and capitalization to polish accuracy. KS3 spans three years, so match the lesson to the topic on the school's plan, not to a grade number.

Canada — Ontario Language, Grades 6–8

Ontario's 2023 Language curriculum has four connected strands: Literacy Connections and Applications; Foundations of Language; Comprehension: Understanding and Responding to Texts; and Composition: Expressing Ideas and Creating Texts. The strands are meant to be taught together: grammar and vocabulary are learned in the service of reading and writing, not as isolated drills. The curriculum also includes digital and media literacy, and expects students to think critically about the texts they encounter, including online.

For practice, connect parts of speech and verb tenses and agreement to the Foundations of Language strand; reading comprehension and text structure to Comprehension; and paragraph writing and argument writing to Composition. Other provinces and territories publish their own language curricula, so families outside Ontario should check the local programme first.

Australia — English, Years 7–8

Australian Curriculum Version 9 organises English through three interrelated strands: Language, Literature and Literacy. Language covers how English works — text structure, grammar, vocabulary and spelling. Literature covers responding to, interpreting and creating literary texts, including texts by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors. Literacy covers using language to listen, read, view, speak, write and create texts for different purposes and audiences, including multimodal and digital texts.

For practice, use parts of speech, sentence types and structure and verb tenses and agreement for the Language strand; reading comprehension and text structure and author's purpose for Literature and Literacy; and paragraph writing, argument writing and vocabulary skills for creating texts. State and territory authorities determine implementation, so check the learner's school plan for the current sequence.

Reading: what to practise

Good reading practice in the middle-school years is active, not passive. Before reading, a learner should know what they are looking for — a central idea, an argument, a character's change. While reading, they should annotate: underline a claim, note a piece of evidence, mark a word they do not know. After reading, they should be able to say the main idea in one sentence, point to two details that support it, and explain one inference — something the text suggests without stating directly. Learnora's reading comprehension lesson practises exactly this, and text structure and author's purpose helps learners see how a text is built so they can read it more efficiently. A common mistake is treating a detail as the main idea: if a sentence is interesting but the text would still make sense without it, it is a detail, not the main idea.

Writing: what to practise

Strong middle-school writing starts before the first sentence. A learner should plan: what is the point, who is the audience, what evidence supports it? Then draft a paragraph with a topic sentence, relevant evidence, transitions and a concluding sentence — the pattern taught in paragraph writing. Argument writing adds a claim, reasons and evidence, and answers the strongest objection, as taught in argument writing. The most useful habit is revision: read the draft aloud and listen for sentences that ramble, words that repeat, or evidence that does not actually support the claim. Editing comes last — check punctuation and capitalization, verb tenses and agreement and sentence boundaries. A paragraph that is clear and honest beats a long paragraph that wanders.

A simple weekly practice routine

Three short sessions a week, about 25 minutes each, work well. One session for reading: read a passage, annotate it, and answer questions with evidence. One session for writing: plan and draft one paragraph, or revise a paragraph written earlier in the week. One session for language: work through a grammar or vocabulary lesson, such as parts of speech or vocabulary skills, and use the new words in a sentence of your own. Reading aloud is a surprisingly good editing tool — awkward sentences are easy to hear and hard to see. Keep a notebook of new words with an example sentence for each; a word you have used three times is a word you own.

Mistakes worth watching for

A few errors appear again and again in middle-school English. Run-on sentences are the most common: two complete thoughts joined with only a comma, or with nothing at all. Next is subject–verb disagreement, especially when words come between the subject and the verb — "The box of chocolates is on the table", not "are". Verb tense slips are common in narratives: a story that starts in the past tense and drifts into the present without a reason. In reading, the classic error is answering from memory or opinion instead of from the text — always check the passage again before writing an answer. And in vocabulary, learners often use a impressive-sounding word in the wrong context; a plain word used correctly is always better than a fancy word used wrongly.
