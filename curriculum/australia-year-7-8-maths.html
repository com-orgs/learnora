Australian Curriculum Mathematics: Years 7–8

Australia's national curriculum describes mathematics by year level from Foundation to Year 10. This guide focuses on Years 7 and 8 and points learners to related Learnora Grade 6–8 supplementary practice. Grade and year labels should not be treated as exact equivalents.

Mathematics strands

The Australian Curriculum: Mathematics organises learning through six connected strands: Number, Algebra, Measurement, Space, Statistics and Probability. Learning builds across year levels and develops fluency, reasoning, problem solving and the ability to interpret mathematical situations.

Six connected strands

Australian Curriculum Version 9 presents mathematics through six interrelated strands: Number, Algebra, Measurement, Space, Statistics and Probability. They are connected, so a good learning activity may use number skills while exploring measurement, geometry or data in a real context.

Number and algebra

Year 7 and Year 8 learners extend number skills and use algebra to describe patterns, relationships and unknown values. Related Learnora practice includes integers, fractions, ratios and linear equations.

Measurement and space

Measurement involves choosing and using units, calculating with measures and solving practical problems. Space develops visualisation, geometric properties and spatial reasoning. Statistics and probability

Statistics supports collecting, representing and interpreting data. Probability helps learners describe and compare the likelihood of events. Related resources include statistics and probability, scatter plots and probability models.

How the Australian Curriculum is organised

The Australian Curriculum: Mathematics (Version 9) describes learning by year level, from Foundation to Year 10. For each year it lists content descriptions — the specific knowledge and skills students are expected to learn — and achievement standards, which describe the quality of learning students should typically demonstrate by the end of the year. The curriculum is organised into six strands: Number, Algebra, Measurement, Space, Statistics and Probability. The strands are not separate subjects to be studied in isolation; a single investigation, such as planning a school garden or analysing sports results, can draw on number, measurement, space and data at the same time.

Version 9 also emphasises four proficiencies that run through every strand: understanding, fluency, problem-solving and reasoning. Understanding means knowing why a method works, not only how to carry it out. Fluency means choosing and using procedures accurately and efficiently. Problem-solving means applying mathematics to unfamiliar situations. Reasoning means explaining, justifying and generalising. A learner who can calculate an answer but cannot explain why the method works has only part of the picture, and Learnora lessons are written to build both halves: worked examples show the reasoning step by step, and practice questions ask learners to explain and check, not only to compute.

The four proficiencies in practice

  • Understanding: connect a new idea to what is already known — for example, seeing that dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal, rather than memorising a rule.
  • Fluency: recall number facts, use standard procedures and choose efficient strategies, such as using a unit rate instead of repeated addition.
  • Problem-solving: work through multi-step problems, try more than one approach, and check whether an answer is reasonable in context.
  • Reasoning: explain a solution in words, give a counterexample when a claim is false, and generalise a pattern — for instance, describing the nth term of a sequence rather than only listing the next few terms.

When a learner is stuck, it helps to ask which proficiency is missing. If the method is known but the answer is wrong, the issue is usually fluency or checking. If the learner does not know where to start, the issue is usually understanding or problem-solving, and re-reading the worked example slowly — covering the solution and predicting each next step — is more useful than doing ten more similar questions.

Year by year: what to practise on Learnora

Year 7 — integers, fractions, algebra, measurement, data

Year 7 builds on primary-school number work and introduces signed numbers, algebraic thinking and more formal geometry. Students compare and order integers, add and subtract them, and use them to describe position and change. They work with fractions, decimals and percentages in context — including percentage increase and decrease — and begin to use ratios and rates.

Start with integers and the number line and adding and subtracting integers, then understanding fractions, adding and subtracting fractions and multiplying and dividing fractions. For percentages, use understanding percentages and percentage increase and decrease; for ratios, introduction to ratios and rates and proportions. Algebra begins with algebraic expressions and one-step equations, and continues with two-step equations. For measurement and geometry, try metric units and conversions, perimeter and area and angles and triangles; for data, data and graphs and statistics and probability.

Year 8 — rational numbers, linear relationships, geometry, probability

Year 8 extends number work to all rational numbers, including negative fractions and decimals, and makes algebra central: students simplify expressions, solve linear equations, and describe relationships with graphs. Geometry adds congruence, transformations and the Pythagorean relationship, while statistics and probability ask students to compare data sets and model chance events.

Work through rational numbers and operations and fractions, decimals and percentages in problem solving. For algebra, use simplifying algebraic expressions, equations and inequalities, linear equations and inequalities and linear functions and graphs. Exponents and roots are covered in exponents, powers and scientific notation and square roots and irrational numbers. Geometry practice is in angles and scale drawings, angle relationships and parallel lines, the Pythagorean Theorem and transformations, congruence and similarity. For measurement, try area, surface area and volume and volume of cylinders, cones and spheres; for data and chance, scatter plots and probability models.

A simple weekly practice routine

Short, regular practice works better than occasional long sessions. A workable routine is three sessions of about 25 minutes a week: one session on the topic being studied at school right now, one session revisiting a topic from a few weeks ago, and one session on a weak spot found by a quiz. In each session, read the explanation, follow the worked examples without skipping steps, then try the practice questions before looking at the answers. If a question is wrong, the useful work is finding the exact step where the reasoning went off track — not just copying the correct answer. Spaced revisiting matters because the Australian Curriculum revisits ideas at greater depth each year; a fraction idea learned in Year 7 returns as rational-number work in Year 8, and a learner who has kept it fresh will recognise it immediately.

Mistakes worth watching for

A few errors appear again and again in Years 7 and 8, and naming them in advance helps learners catch themselves. The most common is treating a fraction bar as two separate numbers instead of one value — so 1/2 + 1/3 becomes 2/5. Another is forgetting that a negative number gets smaller as its size grows: −8 is less than −3. In algebra, the classic slip is doing an operation to only one side of an equation, or forgetting to distribute a multiplier across every term inside brackets. With percentages, learners often confuse "increased by 20%" with "20% of the original" — the first is a new total, the second is only the change. In geometry, a frequent error is using the wrong unit: giving an area in centimetres instead of square centimetres, or a volume in square units. When a learner makes one of these mistakes, ask them to explain their step out loud; the error usually becomes obvious to them halfway through the sentence.

How to check local fit

The Australian Curriculum sets national expectations by year level, but each state and territory manages implementation, and schools choose their own sequencing and teaching programmes. Always check the current Year 7 or Year 8 content descriptions on the official Australian Curriculum site and the learner's school plan before treating a Learnora lesson as a direct match for a classroom topic. The official curriculum is the authority; Learnora is a supplementary practice resource that explains the same ideas in a different way and gives extra questions to work through.
