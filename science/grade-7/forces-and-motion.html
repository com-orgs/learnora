Forces and Motion

Forces can change how objects move or change their shape. By describing forces and measuring motion, we can explain everyday events such as a bicycle slowing down or a ball falling.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Describe a force as a push or pull and identify its direction.
  • Distinguish balanced forces from unbalanced forces.
  • Explain how friction, air resistance and gravity affect motion.
  • Calculate average speed using distance divided by time.
  • Use appropriate units when describing distance, time, speed and force.

1. What is a force?

A force is a push or pull. It has a size (magnitude) and a direction, and it is measured in newtons (N). A resultant force can change an object's velocity: it can start an object moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down or change its direction. A force can also change an object's shape.

Examples: A foot pushes a football; a hand pulls a drawer; Earth attracts objects through gravity.

Forces are of two kinds. Contact forces need touching: friction, tension in a rope, air resistance and the push of a hand. Non-contact forces act at a distance: gravity, magnetism and static electricity. A magnet can pull a paper clip without touching it, and Earth pulls a falling apple without any contact at all.

Scientists draw forces as arrows. The arrow points in the direction of the force, and a longer arrow means a bigger force. Two arrows of the same length pointing in opposite directions show balanced forces; if one arrow is longer, the forces are unbalanced and the object will change its motion.

2. Balanced and unbalanced forces

When forces on an object are balanced, their combined effect (the resultant or net force) is zero. The object's motion does not change: it may remain at rest or continue at constant velocity in a straight line.

When forces are unbalanced, the resultant force is not zero, so the object's velocity changes. It may speed up, slow down or change direction.

Example: A book resting on a table has gravity pulling down and a support force from the table pushing up. When these forces balance, the book stays at rest.

To find the resultant force, add forces that point the same way and subtract forces that point opposite ways. A push of 150 N forwards and friction of 90 N backwards give a resultant of 150 − 90 = 60 N forwards. In a tug-of-war, if both teams pull with 500 N, the resultant is 0 N and the rope does not move. If one team pulls with 600 N and the other with 500 N, the resultant is 100 N towards the stronger team, and the rope moves that way.

A cyclist riding at a steady speed in a straight line has balanced forces: the driving force from pedalling is equal to the friction and air resistance holding the cyclist back. A rocket taking off has unbalanced forces: the thrust from its engines is much larger than its weight, so it accelerates upwards.

3. Friction, air resistance and gravity

Friction acts between surfaces in contact and usually opposes their relative motion. It can help us walk, but it also causes moving objects to slow down and can produce heat.

Air resistance is a type of drag that acts against an object moving through air. Gravity attracts objects with mass towards one another; near Earth's surface it pulls objects towards Earth's centre.

Friction is not always a nuisance. It lets your shoes grip the ground so you can walk, and it lets brakes slow a bicycle. But friction also wears out shoe soles and engine parts, and it turns useful energy into heat — rub your hands together quickly and you can feel that heat. Engineers reduce unwanted friction with smooth surfaces and lubricants such as oil.

Air resistance grows as speed increases, which is why fast cars and aeroplanes are streamlined — shaped so that air flows past them easily. A parachute does the opposite: it is wide and flat so that air resistance is large and the fall is slowed. Gravity always pulls towards the centre of the Earth, which is why a dropped ball falls straight down, not sideways.

4. Describe motion with speed

Average speed tells how much distance is travelled per unit of time:

Average speed = total distance ÷ total time

Common units include metres per second (m/s) and kilometres per hour (km/h). Use matching units: kilometres divided by hours gives km/h.

Speed tells how fast something moves. Velocity also includes direction.

A distance–time graph draws a journey as a line. A flat, horizontal line means the object has stopped. A straight sloping line means steady speed, and a steeper line means a faster speed. A curved line means the speed is changing. If a line on the graph is flat for 10 minutes, the traveller was resting for 10 minutes.

To change between speed units, remember that 1 km = 1,000 m and 1 hour = 3,600 s. So 36 km/h means 36,000 m ÷ 3,600 s = 10 m/s. A car at 72 km/h is travelling at 20 m/s. Always convert the units first, then divide distance by time — mixing kilometres with seconds gives a meaningless answer.

5. Mass and weight are different

Mass measures how much matter an object contains and is measured in kilograms (kg). Weight is the force of gravity acting on that mass and is measured in newtons (N).

Near Earth's surface, gravitational field strength is about 9.8 N/kg (often rounded to 10 N/kg in school calculations). Weight can be estimated using weight = mass × gravitational field strength.

Do not use kilograms as a unit of weight. Kilograms measure mass; newtons measure weight as a force.

Your mass does not change when you travel, but your weight does, because weight depends on gravity. On the Moon, gravitational field strength is only about 1.6 N/kg. A person with a mass of 60 kg weighs about 60 × 10 = 600 N on Earth, but only about 60 × 1.6 = 96 N on the Moon — while their mass stays exactly 60 kg. Astronauts feel light on the Moon for this reason, even though the amount of matter in their bodies has not changed.

In school work, g is often rounded to 10 N/kg because it makes the arithmetic easy, but the more accurate value near Earth's surface is 9.8 N/kg. A 10 kg object really weighs 10 × 9.8 = 98 N; using 10 N/kg gives 100 N, which is close enough for most school calculations. State which value you used so your answer can be checked.

6. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Saying mass and weight are the same thing. Mass (in kilograms) is how much matter an object contains; weight (in newtons) is the force of gravity on that mass. Your mass is the same on the Moon; your weight is not.

Mistake 2: Using kilograms as the unit of weight. “My weight is 50 kg.” is wrong in science. Correct: “My mass is 50 kg, so my weight is about 500 N on Earth.”

Mistake 3: Thinking a moving object must have an unbalanced force. A cyclist moving at a steady speed in a straight line has balanced forces. An unbalanced force is needed only to change the motion.

Mistake 4: Thinking friction is always bad. Without friction you could not walk, and brakes could not slow a bicycle. Friction is useful for grip and braking, even though it also causes wear and heat.

Mistake 5: Mixing units in speed = distance ÷ time. Dividing kilometres by seconds, or metres by hours, gives a meaningless number. Convert the units to match first: km with hours, or m with seconds.

Mistake 6: Saying air resistance speeds things up. Air resistance always opposes motion. Streamlining reduces it so things can go faster; a parachute increases it so things fall more slowly.

Worked Examples

  1. A cyclist travels 600 metres in 120 seconds. Average speed = 600 ÷ 120 = 5 m/s.
  2. A 2 kg object has a weight of about 2 × 10 = 20 N near Earth's surface when g is rounded to 10 N/kg.
  3. A stationary book has balanced vertical forces: its weight downward is balanced by the table's support force upward.
  4. Resultant force: a push of 150 N forwards and friction of 90 N backwards act on a crate. The forces oppose each other, so subtract: 150 − 90 = 60 N forwards. The resultant is 60 N forwards, so the crate speeds up in that direction.
  5. Average speed: a swimmer covers 200 m in 50 s. Average speed = 200 ÷ 50 = 4 m/s.
  6. Average speed: a car travels 180 km in 2 hours. Average speed = 180 ÷ 2 = 90 km/h.
  7. Weight: a person with a mass of 60 kg has a weight of about 60 × 10 = 600 N on Earth (g rounded to 10 N/kg).
  8. Unit conversion: a train travels at 72 km/h. In metres per second that is 72,000 m ÷ 3,600 s = 20 m/s. Check: 20 × 3,600 = 72,000, so the conversion is correct.

  8. A bus travels 240 km in 4 hours. Find its average speed.
  9. Estimate the weight of a 3 kg bag on Earth using 10 N/kg.
  10. A cyclist moves at a steady speed in a straight line. Are the forces balanced or unbalanced? Explain.
  11. Convert 36 km/h to m/s.

Show practice answers and explanations
  1. A push or a pull.
  2. Zero newtons; the forces are balanced.
  3. 300 ÷ 60 = 5 m/s.
  4. Friction.
  5. Newton (N).
  6. 5 × 10 = 50 N.
  7. 200 − 80 = 120 N forwards. The forces oppose each other, so subtract.
  8. 240 ÷ 4 = 60 km/h.
  9. 3 × 10 = 30 N.
  10. Balanced. Steady speed in a straight line means the resultant force is zero.
  11. 36 km/h = 36,000 m ÷ 3,600 s = 10 m/s.
  12. Gravity (or magnetism, or static electricity).

Lesson Summary

  • A force is a push or pull that can change motion or shape.
  • Balanced forces do not change motion; unbalanced forces cause acceleration.
  • Friction, air resistance and gravity influence how objects move.
  • Average speed equals total distance divided by total time; mass and weight are different.
  • The resultant force is found by adding forces in the same direction and subtracting forces in opposite directions.
  • Mass stays the same everywhere; weight changes with the strength of gravity.
