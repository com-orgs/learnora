Forces and Motion

Forces can change how objects move or change their shape. By describing forces and measuring motion, we can explain everyday events such as a bicycle slowing down or a ball falling.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Describe a force as a push or pull and identify its direction.
  • Distinguish balanced forces from unbalanced forces.
  • Explain how friction, air resistance and gravity affect motion.
  • Calculate average speed using distance divided by time.
  • Use appropriate units when describing distance, time, speed and force.

1. What is a force?

A force is a push or pull. It has a size (magnitude) and a direction, and it is measured in newtons (N). A force can start or stop motion, change speed or direction, or change an object's shape.

Examples: A foot pushes a football; a hand pulls a drawer; Earth attracts objects through gravity.

2. Balanced and unbalanced forces

When forces on an object are balanced, their combined effect (the resultant or net force) is zero. The object's motion does not change: it may remain at rest or continue at constant velocity in a straight line.

When forces are unbalanced, the resultant force is not zero, so the object's velocity changes. It may speed up, slow down or change direction.

Example: A book resting on a table has gravity pulling down and a support force from the table pushing up. When these forces balance, the book stays at rest.

3. Friction, air resistance and gravity

Friction acts between surfaces in contact and usually opposes their relative motion. It can help us walk, but it also causes moving objects to slow down and can produce heat.

Air resistance is a type of drag that acts against an object moving through air. Gravity attracts objects with mass towards one another; near Earth's surface it pulls objects towards Earth's centre.

4. Describe motion with speed

Average speed tells how much distance is travelled per unit of time:

Average speed = total distance ÷ total time

Common units include metres per second (m/s) and kilometres per hour (km/h). Use matching units: kilometres divided by hours gives km/h.

Speed tells how fast something moves. Velocity also includes direction.

5. Mass and weight are different

Mass measures how much matter an object contains and is measured in kilograms (kg). Weight is the force of gravity acting on that mass and is measured in newtons (N).

Near Earth's surface, gravitational field strength is about 9.8 N/kg (often rounded to 10 N/kg in school calculations). Weight can be estimated using weight = mass × gravitational field strength.

Do not use kilograms as a unit of weight. Kilograms measure mass; newtons measure weight as a force.

Worked Examples

  1. A cyclist travels 600 metres in 120 seconds. Average speed = 600 ÷ 120 = 5 m/s.
  2. A 2 kg object has a weight of about 2 × 10 = 20 N near Earth's surface when g is rounded to 10 N/kg.
  3. A stationary book has balanced vertical forces: its weight downward is balanced by the table's support force upward.

Independent Practice

Try each question before revealing the answers.

  1. A force is best described as what?
  2. Two equal opposite forces act on a stationary box. What is the resultant force?
  3. A runner travels 300 m in 60 s. Find average speed.
  4. Name one force that usually opposes motion between two surfaces.
  5. What is the SI unit of force?
  6. Estimate the weight of a 5 kg object on Earth using 10 N/kg.
Show practice answers and explanations
  1. A push or a pull.
  2. Zero newtons; the forces are balanced.
  3. 300 ÷ 60 = 5 m/s.
  4. Friction.
  5. Newton (N).
  6. 5 × 10 = 50 N.
