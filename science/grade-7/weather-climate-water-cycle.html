Weather, Climate and the Water Cycle

Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions. Climate describes patterns over much longer periods. Water continually moves between the atmosphere, land and bodies of water through the water cycle.

Learning time: approximately 30–35 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Distinguish weather observations from climate patterns.
  • Describe evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection.
  • Explain the role of the Sun and gravity in the water cycle.
  • Recognise runoff, infiltration and transpiration as pathways of water movement.
  • Use evidence carefully when describing weather and climate.

1. Weather and climate are different

Weather describes atmospheric conditions at a particular place and time, such as temperature, cloud cover, wind and precipitation. Climate describes long-term patterns and averages, often assessed across decades. “It rained heavily this afternoon” is weather; “the region usually has a dry season” describes climate. One cold day cannot establish or disprove a climate pattern.

2. Evaporation and transpiration

Evaporation occurs when liquid water gains enough energy for molecules to enter the air as water vapour. The Sun is the main energy source for the water cycle. Water evaporates from oceans, lakes, rivers and soil. Plants release water vapour through transpiration. Evaporation can happen below boiling point.

3. Condensation and precipitation

As moist air cools, water vapour may condense into tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals. Clouds form when many droplets or crystals gather. When water particles grow large enough, they may fall as precipitation—rain, snow, sleet or hail. Clouds are made mainly of tiny droplets and/or ice crystals, not invisible water vapour.

4. Runoff, infiltration and collection

After precipitation reaches the ground, water may flow over land as runoff, soak into soil through infiltration, be taken up by plants or collect in rivers, lakes and oceans. Some infiltrated water moves underground and can recharge groundwater. Paved areas often produce more runoff than permeable soil, depending on slope and drainage.

5. Water-cycle models and evidence

The water cycle has no single starting point. Water moves through different pathways and may spend very different amounts of time in the atmosphere, ocean, soil, ice or living things. A useful model shows transfers between reservoirs and identifies processes. Do not treat one short weather event as the climate of a region.

Worked Example

Sunlight warms water in a lake. Some liquid water evaporates and rises as vapour. Higher in the atmosphere, cooling can cause condensation into droplets that form clouds. Water may later fall as rain. Some rain runs into streams, while some infiltrates soil and may move towards groundwater.

Independent Practice

Use the lesson ideas to explain each situation before checking the suggested responses.

  1. Classify these statements as weather or climate: “A storm passed this afternoon” and “The region usually has a dry summer over many years.” Explain the time-scale clue.
  2. Describe how water can move from a lake into cloud droplets and then return to land. Use the terms evaporation, condensation and precipitation.
  3. After heavy rain, why might a paved playground produce more surface runoff than a plant-covered soil area? Include the term infiltration.
Show suggested responses
  1. The storm is weather because it describes a short-term event. The usual dry summer is climate because it describes a long-term pattern.
  2. Sunlight provides energy for evaporation; water vapour cools and condenses into droplets or ice crystals; precipitation returns water to the surface.
  3. Pavement is less permeable, so less water infiltrates the soil and more may flow over the surface as runoff.
