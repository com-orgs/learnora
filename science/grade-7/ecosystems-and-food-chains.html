Ecosystems and Food Chains

An ecosystem includes living organisms and the non-living parts of their environment. Organisms depend on one another for food, shelter and suitable conditions.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Distinguish organisms, populations, communities and ecosystems.
  • Identify biotic and abiotic parts of an environment.
  • Explain the roles of producers, consumers and decomposers.
  • Read food chains and food webs and show the direction of energy transfer.
  • Describe how changes in one population can affect other organisms.

1. What is an ecosystem?

An organism is an individual living thing. A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in an area. A community includes populations of different species. An ecosystem includes the community and the non-living environment with which it interacts.

2. Biotic and abiotic factors

Biotic factors are living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria. Abiotic factors are non-living conditions, such as light, water, temperature, air, soil and minerals.

Example: In a pond, fish and algae are biotic factors; water temperature, sunlight and dissolved oxygen are abiotic factors.

3. Producers, consumers and decomposers

  • Producers, such as green plants and algae, make their own food, usually using light energy in photosynthesis.
  • Consumers obtain energy by eating other organisms. Herbivores eat plants; carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat both.
  • Decomposers, including many fungi and bacteria, break down dead material and waste, returning nutrients to the environment.

4. Food chains and food webs

A food chain shows one pathway of feeding relationships. Arrows point in the direction that energy is transferred: from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it.

Example: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. The grass is the producer; the grasshopper is a primary consumer; the frog is a secondary consumer.

A food web combines many connected food chains. Most organisms have more than one food source and may be eaten by more than one predator.

5. Energy and ecosystem change

Energy enters many ecosystems as sunlight, passes through food chains and is eventually transferred to the surroundings, often as heat. Less energy is available at higher feeding levels, so food chains usually contain only a few levels.

If one population changes, other populations may be affected. For example, a fall in grass may reduce food for grasshoppers and then for animals that eat grasshoppers. Real ecosystems are complex, so effects can spread through several food-web links.

Do not draw food-chain arrows from predator to prey. The arrow shows the direction of energy transfer, from food to eater.

Worked Examples

  1. In grass → rabbit → fox, grass is the producer, rabbit is the primary consumer and fox is the secondary consumer.
  2. In a pond, algae are biotic; sunlight and water temperature are abiotic.
  3. If rabbit numbers fall, foxes may have less food, while plants eaten by rabbits may increase. The actual result depends on the wider food web.

Lesson Summary

  • An ecosystem includes organisms and the non-living conditions around them.
  • Producers, consumers and decomposers have different roles in energy flow and nutrient cycling.
  • Food chains show one pathway; food webs connect many pathways.
  • A change in one population or habitat can affect other parts of the ecosystem.
