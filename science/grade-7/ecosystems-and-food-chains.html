Ecosystems and Food Chains

An ecosystem includes living organisms and the non-living parts of their environment. Organisms depend on one another for food, shelter and suitable conditions.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Distinguish organisms, populations, communities and ecosystems.
  • Identify biotic and abiotic parts of an environment.
  • Explain the roles of producers, consumers and decomposers.
  • Read food chains and food webs and show the direction of energy transfer.
  • Describe how changes in one population can affect other organisms.

1. What is an ecosystem?

An organism is an individual living thing. A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in an area. A community includes populations of different species. An ecosystem includes the community and the non-living environment with which it interacts.

A habitat is the place where an organism lives — a pond, a forest floor, a desert or even the bark of a single tree. The habitat provides food, shelter and a place to reproduce. The levels fit inside one another like boxes: one organism, then a population of the same species, then a community of many populations, then the ecosystem, which adds the non-living surroundings. Very large regions with a similar climate and similar communities, such as a desert or a rainforest, are called biomes.

Ecosystem boundaries are not always sharp. A pond and the meadow beside it share birds, insects and water, so where one ecosystem ends and the next begins is often a judgement, not a fence. What matters is the idea: living things and their non-living surroundings form one connected system.

2. Biotic and abiotic factors

Biotic factors are living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria. Abiotic factors are non-living conditions, such as light, water, temperature, air, soil and minerals.

Example: In a pond, fish and algae are biotic factors; water temperature, sunlight and dissolved oxygen are abiotic factors.

Other abiotic factors include the acidity of the soil or water (pH), the saltiness of the water, wind, and the amount of oxygen dissolved in water. Abiotic factors limit where organisms can live: most green plants need light to photosynthesise, and fish need enough dissolved oxygen in the water to breathe. If an abiotic factor changes — for example, if a pond warms up and holds less oxygen — the organisms living there are affected too.

Biotic factors also interact. Organisms compete for food, water, light and space, and they depend on one another in other ways: bees pollinate flowers, and predators keep prey populations in check. An ecosystem is therefore shaped by both its living and its non-living parts working together.

3. Producers, consumers and decomposers

  • Producers, such as green plants and algae, make their own food, usually using light energy in photosynthesis.
  • Consumers obtain energy by eating other organisms. Herbivores eat plants; carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat both.
  • Decomposers, including many fungi and bacteria, break down dead material and waste, returning nutrients to the environment.

Feeding levels have names. Producers are the first trophic level. Primary consumers (herbivores) are the second, secondary consumers the third, and so on. In “grass → grasshopper → frog → snake”, grass is level 1, the grasshopper is level 2, the frog is level 3 and the snake is level 4. Decomposers do not sit at one level: they feed on dead material from every level.

Producers make food by photosynthesis: using light energy, a green plant turns carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. The glucose stores chemical energy that every other organism in the food chain depends on. Detritivores such as earthworms and woodlice eat dead leaves and droppings and break them into smaller pieces, helping decomposers finish the job and return nutrients such as nitrates to the soil, where plants can use them again.

4. Food chains and food webs

A food chain shows one pathway of feeding relationships. Arrows point in the direction that energy is transferred: from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it.

Example: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. The grass is the producer; the grasshopper is a primary consumer; the frog is a secondary consumer.

A food web combines many connected food chains. Most organisms have more than one food source and may be eaten by more than one predator.

To read a food web, pick one producer and follow one chain at a time. In a garden web, lettuce is eaten by a snail and by a slug; the snail is eaten by a thrush; the thrush is eaten by a hawk. The same thrush may also eat the slug, and the same hawk may also eat a mouse. Because chains share organisms, removing one organism can affect several chains at once.

Food chains are usually short — four or five links at most — because energy is lost at every step. Only about a tenth of the energy at one level reaches the next; the rest is used for movement and life processes or transferred to the surroundings as heat. A pyramid of numbers shows the same idea: for example, 1,000 grass plants might feed 100 rabbits, which feed 10 foxes. There are fewer organisms at the top because there is less energy to support them. (A pyramid is not always pyramid-shaped: one large oak tree can feed thousands of caterpillars.)

5. Energy and ecosystem change

Energy enters many ecosystems as sunlight, passes through food chains and is eventually transferred to the surroundings, often as heat. Less energy is available at higher feeding levels, so food chains usually contain only a few levels.

If one population changes, other populations may be affected. For example, a fall in grass may reduce food for grasshoppers and then for animals that eat grasshoppers. Real ecosystems are complex, so effects can spread through several food-web links.

Do not draw food-chain arrows from predator to prey. The arrow shows the direction of energy transfer, from food to eater.

Competition happens because resources are limited. Plants compete for light, water, space and minerals from the soil; animals compete for food, water, mates and territory. Competition is one reason why two species that need exactly the same resources rarely live side by side for long.

Interdependence goes beyond eating. Many flowering plants depend on insects such as bees for pollination: the insects carry pollen between flowers so the plants can make fruits and seeds. Many of the foods people eat depend on insect pollinators, so a fall in bee numbers can reduce crop harvests. Humans also change ecosystems directly — cutting down forests removes habitats and food, and pollution can poison the water and soil that organisms depend on.

6. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Drawing food-chain arrows backwards. Arrows show the direction energy travels, from the food to the eater. In “grass → rabbit”, the arrow points from grass to rabbit, never the other way.

Mistake 2: Calling a consumer a producer. Only producers make their own food. A rabbit eats plants, so it is a consumer, not a producer.

Mistake 3: Putting decomposers only at the end of the chain. Decomposers and detritivores act on dead material from every level, not just the top predator.

Mistake 4: Confusing a population with a community. A population is one species in an area; a community is all the populations of different species living together.

Mistake 5: Saying energy is recycled. Energy flows through a food chain and is lost as heat at each step. Nutrients are recycled by decomposers; energy is not.

Mistake 6: Saying a food web is the same as a food chain. A chain is one pathway; a web is many linked chains, and it is the more realistic picture.

Worked Examples

  1. In grass → rabbit → fox, grass is the producer, rabbit is the primary consumer and fox is the secondary consumer.
  2. In a pond, algae are biotic; sunlight and water temperature are abiotic.
  3. If rabbit numbers fall, foxes may have less food, while plants eaten by rabbits may increase. The actual result depends on the wider food web.
  4. Label the trophic levels in “algae → snail → thrush → hawk”. Algae are the producer (level 1), the snail is the primary consumer (level 2), the thrush is the secondary consumer (level 3) and the hawk is the tertiary consumer (level 4).
  5. Pyramid of numbers: 1,000 grass plants feed 100 rabbits, which feed 10 foxes. The numbers fall at each level because less energy is available higher up the chain.
  6. Energy transfer: if grass holds 10,000 kJ of energy, about a tenth — 1,000 kJ — reaches the rabbits, and about a tenth of that — 100 kJ — reaches the foxes. The rest is used up or lost as heat.
  7. Sort the factors in a desert: a cactus and a lizard are biotic; sand, temperature and sunlight are abiotic.
  8. Predict the effect: if frog numbers fall, grasshoppers may increase (nothing is eating them) and snakes may decrease (they have less food). The wider food web decides the final result.

Lesson Summary

  • An ecosystem includes organisms and the non-living conditions around them.
  • Producers, consumers and decomposers have different roles in energy flow and nutrient cycling.
  • Food chains show one pathway; food webs connect many pathways.
  • A change in one population or habitat can affect other parts of the ecosystem.
  • Only about a tenth of the energy at one feeding level reaches the next, so food chains are usually short.
  • Energy flows through a food chain and is lost as heat; nutrients are recycled by decomposers.
