Mixtures and Separation Techniques

Many materials around us are mixtures. A mixture contains two or more substances together, but the substances are not chemically joined into a new substance. Scientists use physical methods to separate mixtures and recover useful materials.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Beginner level

What You Will Learn

  • Distinguish pure substances, mixtures and solutions.
  • Describe solutes, solvents and soluble substances.
  • Choose suitable methods to separate different mixtures.
  • Explain filtration, evaporation, crystallisation, distillation, sieving and chromatography.
  • Use particle size, solubility and boiling point to explain separation.

1. Pure substances and mixtures

A pure substance contains only one type of substance and has a fixed composition. An element contains one type of atom; a compound contains two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio.

A mixture contains two or more substances physically combined. The substances keep their own chemical identities and can often be separated by physical methods. Mixtures may have different compositions.

Examples: Distilled water is close to a pure substance. Air is a mixture of gases. Seawater is a mixture of water and dissolved salts. Soil contains minerals, organic matter, air and water.

2. Solutions, solutes and solvents

A solution is a uniform mixture. The solute is the substance that dissolves; the solvent is the substance that does the dissolving. In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.

A substance that dissolves in a particular solvent is described as soluble in that solvent. A substance that does not dissolve appreciably is insoluble. Dissolving does not mean that a substance disappears; its particles spread through the solvent.

Important: Filtering salt water does not remove dissolved salt. The dissolved particles are small enough to pass through the filter paper with the water.

4. Filtration and sieving

Sieving uses a mesh with holes of a chosen size. Smaller particles pass through while larger particles remain behind. It is useful when the components are solids with noticeably different particle sizes.

Filtration uses a porous barrier such as filter paper. The liquid and dissolved substances pass through as the filtrate; the insoluble solid left on the filter is the residue.

Example: To separate sand from water, pour the mixture through filter paper in a funnel. Sand remains as residue and water passes through as filtrate.

5. Evaporation, crystallisation and distillation

Evaporation can recover a dissolved solid by allowing the solvent to escape as vapour. For example, water can evaporate from salt water, leaving salt behind. The water is not collected in this process.

Crystallisation is used to obtain crystals of a dissolved solid. A solution is concentrated, then cooled or left to evaporate slowly so crystals form. The crystals can be separated and dried.

Distillation includes evaporation followed by condensation. In simple distillation, a solution is heated so the solvent evaporates; the vapour is cooled in a condenser and collected as a liquid. Dissolved salt remains in the original container.

Do not heat a solution to dryness if the aim is to collect the solvent. Use an appropriate distillation setup and follow laboratory safety instructions.

6. Paper chromatography

Chromatography separates substances because different components move at different rates through a stationary material while being carried by a moving solvent. In paper chromatography, the paper is the stationary phase and the solvent is the mobile phase.

  1. Draw a pencil baseline near the bottom of the paper.
  2. Place a small spot of the sample on the baseline and allow it to dry.
  3. Stand the paper in a little solvent, keeping the sample spot above the solvent level.
  4. Allow the solvent to travel up the paper, then remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top.
  5. Mark the solvent front and compare the positions of the separated spots.
Example: A black ink may separate into several coloured spots, showing that it contains more than one dye.

7. Worked examples

  1. Sand and water: filtration is suitable because sand is insoluble and its particles are trapped by filter paper.
  2. Salt and water, salt wanted: evaporation or crystallisation can recover the salt.
  3. Salt and water, water wanted: simple distillation collects water vapour after it condenses.
  4. Iron filings and sand: a magnet can attract the iron filings while the sand remains.
  5. Different dyes in ink: paper chromatography may separate the dyes because they travel at different rates.

Key Takeaways

  • Mixtures contain substances physically combined, not chemically bonded into a single new substance.
  • A solution contains a solute dissolved in a solvent.
  • Filtration separates insoluble solids from liquids; it does not remove dissolved substances.
  • Evaporation, crystallisation and distillation are useful for solutions.
  • Chromatography separates components that move at different rates through a medium.
