Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Thermal energy moves from warmer places to cooler places. It can travel through direct contact, through the movement of fluids, or by electromagnetic waves. These processes are called conduction, convection and radiation.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Beginner level

What You Will Learn

  • Explain that thermal energy moves from higher temperature to lower temperature.
  • Describe conduction, convection and radiation.
  • Identify everyday examples of each heat-transfer method.
  • Explain how insulation reduces unwanted energy transfer.
  • Distinguish temperature from thermal energy in simple situations.

1. Heat and temperature

In everyday language, people often say that heat moves from one object to another. In science, thermal energy is transferred because of a temperature difference. The net transfer is from a warmer region to a cooler region until they reach the same temperature, if no other energy transfer changes the system.

Temperature tells us how hot or cold something is and is related to the average kinetic energy of its particles. Thermal energy depends on more than temperature: the amount and type of material also matter.

Example: A warm mug transfers energy to cooler air and to a cool table touching it. The energy transfer continues while there is a temperature difference.

2. Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material or between objects in direct contact, without the material as a whole flowing from place to place. Particles transfer energy through collisions and interactions. In metals, mobile electrons also help transfer energy quickly.

  • Metals such as copper and aluminium are generally good thermal conductors.
  • Materials such as wood, plastic, wool and trapped air are generally poor conductors and can act as insulators.
  • Conduction can occur in solids, liquids and gases, but it is usually most effective in solids.
Example: A metal spoon left in hot soup becomes warm along its handle because energy is conducted through the metal.

3. Convection

Convection is the transfer of thermal energy by the movement of a fluid. Fluids are liquids and gases. When part of a fluid is heated, it often expands and becomes less dense than the cooler fluid around it. The warmer, less-dense fluid rises while cooler, denser fluid moves down to take its place. This circulation can form a convection current.

Example: In a pot of water heated from below, warmer water tends to rise and cooler water sinks, helping spread energy through the water.

Convection happens in fluids, not in a rigid solid. Wind and large-scale movements in the atmosphere and oceans involve convection processes, although real weather systems are more complex.

4. Radiation

Thermal radiation is energy transferred by electromagnetic waves, mainly infrared for objects at everyday temperatures. Radiation does not need particles or a material medium, so it can travel through a vacuum.

Example: Energy from the Sun travels through space and warms Earth's surface. You can also feel radiant energy from a fire without touching it.

Dark, matt surfaces are generally good absorbers and emitters of thermal radiation. Shiny, light-coloured surfaces are generally poorer absorbers and emitters and reflect more radiation.

5. Compare the three methods

MethodHow energy is transferredEveryday example
ConductionThrough contact and particle interactions within a materialA metal pan handle becoming hot
ConvectionBy movement of a liquid or gasCirculating water in a heated pot
RadiationBy electromagnetic waves; no medium requiredEnergy from sunlight warming your skin
Remember: More than one method can transfer energy in the same situation. A campfire warms you by radiation, while moving warm air can transfer energy by convection.

6. Insulation and reducing heat transfer

Insulation slows the transfer of thermal energy. A good insulator may trap pockets of air, reduce fluid movement, or reflect thermal radiation.

  • A woollen jumper traps air close to the body, reducing heat loss by conduction and convection.
  • Double-glazed windows trap a layer of air or gas between panes, reducing conduction and convection.
  • A shiny emergency blanket reflects some infrared radiation back towards the body.
  • A vacuum flask uses a vacuum between its walls to reduce conduction and convection; reflective surfaces reduce radiation.

Insulation does not create heat. It slows energy transfer between places at different temperatures.

7. Practice Exercises

Try these questions before revealing the suggested answers.

  1. Which method transfers thermal energy through direct contact in a metal spoon?
  2. Why does warm water tend to rise in a convection current?
  3. Which method allows energy from the Sun to travel through space?
  4. Why can trapped air help insulate a house?
  5. Can conduction, convection and radiation all happen around a warm fire? Give one example of each.
Show practice answers
  1. Conduction.
  2. When warmed, the water expands and usually becomes less dense than the cooler surrounding water, so it rises.
  3. Radiation, carried by electromagnetic waves.
  4. Air is a poor conductor, and trapping it in small spaces limits bulk movement that could cause convection.
  5. Yes. Radiation travels from the flames; warm air can transfer energy by convection; a metal object touching a hot part can transfer energy by conduction.

Key Takeaways

  • Thermal energy is transferred overall from warmer regions to cooler regions.
  • Conduction transfers energy through contact and interactions within matter.
  • Convection transfers energy through the movement of liquids and gases.
  • Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and can travel through a vacuum.
  • Insulation slows energy transfer; it does not create heat.
