States of Matter and the Particle Model

Everything around you is made of matter. Matter can exist as a solid, liquid or gas. The particle model helps explain why these states have different properties and how heating or cooling can change one state into another.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Beginner level

What You Will Learn

  • Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in solids, liquids and gases.
  • Compare the shape, volume and compressibility of each state.
  • Explain melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation.
  • Describe how heating and cooling affect particle movement.
  • Recognise that changes of state are physical changes.

1. What is matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Air, water, rocks, plants and your desk are all examples of matter. Matter is made of tiny particles. Depending on the substance and its conditions, these particles can be arranged and move in different ways.

Think about water: ice is solid water, liquid water flows, and water vapour is a gas. The substance is still water, but its state and particle behaviour are different.

2. The particle model

The particle model is a scientific model used to explain the behaviour of matter. It represents matter as tiny particles that are always moving. The drawings in textbooks are simplified models; particles are not necessarily little solid balls.

Solids

  • Particles are close together in an orderly arrangement in many solids.
  • They vibrate around fixed positions rather than moving freely from place to place.
  • A solid usually keeps its own shape and volume.
  • Most solids are difficult to compress because there is little empty space between particles.

Liquids

  • Particles are close together but are not fixed in one position.
  • They can move and slide past one another.
  • A liquid has a fixed volume but takes the shape of its container.
  • Liquids are not easily compressed.

Gases

  • Particles are much farther apart than in solids and liquids.
  • They move rapidly and randomly in all directions.
  • A gas spreads out to fill its container, so it has no fixed shape or volume.
  • Gases can usually be compressed because there is space between their particles.

3. Heating and particle movement

When a substance is heated, energy is transferred to it. Its particles generally gain kinetic energy and move more energetically. When a substance is cooled, its particles generally lose kinetic energy and move less energetically.

Example: When an ice cube is warmed, its particles vibrate more strongly. At its melting point, the solid structure breaks down and the ice becomes liquid water.

Heating does not mean that particles themselves get bigger. It changes their energy and movement; the spacing between particles may also change.

4. Changes of state

ChangeWhat happensExample
MeltingSolid → liquidIce becomes water
FreezingLiquid → solidWater becomes ice
EvaporationLiquid → gas at the surfaceA puddle slowly dries
BoilingLiquid → gas throughout the liquidWater boils in a kettle
CondensationGas → liquidWater droplets form on a cool glass

Evaporation can happen below the boiling point and takes place at the liquid's surface. Boiling happens throughout the liquid at its boiling point for a given pressure.

Important: During a change of state of a pure substance at constant pressure, the temperature stays constant while energy is used to change the arrangement and separation of particles. This is why a thermometer may stop rising for a while as ice melts or water boils.

5. Physical changes

Melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation are physical changes. The substance itself does not become a different substance. For example, liquid water and ice are both H₂O.

A physical change may be reversible. Water can freeze into ice and the ice can melt back into water. By contrast, burning paper creates new substances and is a chemical change.

6. Everyday examples

  • Wet clothes drying: liquid water evaporates from the fabric into the air.
  • Water droplets on a cold bottle: water vapour in the surrounding air condenses on the cool surface.
  • Ice cream melting: a solid mixture warms and becomes softer or liquid.
  • Steam above boiling water: water vapour is present; the visible white cloud is mainly tiny liquid droplets formed as vapour cools and condenses.
The visible white cloud above a kettle is not the same as invisible water vapour. It consists mostly of tiny droplets of liquid water suspended in air.

Key Takeaways

  • Solids keep their shape and volume; liquids keep volume but take the container's shape; gases spread to fill the container.
  • Particles are always moving, but their arrangement and freedom of movement differ by state.
  • Heating generally increases particle kinetic energy; cooling generally decreases it.
  • Melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation are changes of state.
  • Changes of state are physical changes because the substance remains the same.
