Electric Circuits: Current, Voltage and Resistance

Electric circuits transfer energy from a source to components such as lamps and motors. Learn what current, potential difference and resistance mean, and how series and parallel circuits behave.

Study time: 25–30 minutes · Includes: circuit examples, comparison tables, independent practice, five-question quiz and printable worksheet.

What You Will Learn

  • Identify the parts of a simple electrical circuit.
  • Explain why a circuit needs a complete conducting path.
  • Describe current, potential difference (voltage) and resistance.
  • Compare series and parallel circuits.
  • Use the relationship between voltage, current and resistance in simple calculations.
  • Choose the correct way to connect an ammeter and voltmeter and follow safe low-voltage practices.

1. What makes a circuit work?

A simple circuit is a complete path through which electric charge can move. A basic circuit may contain a cell or battery, connecting wires, a switch and a component such as a lamp.

ComponentRole
Cell or batteryProvides a potential difference that transfers energy to charges in the circuit.
Connecting wiresProvide a conducting path for charge to move.
SwitchOpens or closes the circuit path.
Lamp or motorTransfers electrical energy into light, heat or movement.
ResistorProvides resistance and can limit current in a circuit.

If a switch is open or a wire is disconnected, the path is broken and a simple circuit will not operate as intended. A closed circuit has a complete conducting loop.

Worked Example A: a lamp does not light

A battery and lamp are connected, but the switch is open. The circuit path is incomplete, so charge cannot flow continuously around the loop. Closing the switch completes the path and allows current to flow.

2. Electric current

Current describes the rate at which electric charge flows past a point in a circuit. It is measured in amperes (A), often called amps. One ampere means one coulomb of charge passes a point each second.

Current = charge ÷ time   (I = Q ÷ t)

In a simple series circuit, the current is the same at every point in the loop. In a parallel circuit, current divides between branches and the branch currents add to the total current entering the junction.

Worked Example B: calculate current

If 12 coulombs of charge pass a point in 4 seconds:

I = Q ÷ t = 12 C ÷ 4 s = 3 A

The current is 3 amperes.

3. Potential difference (voltage)

Potential difference, commonly called voltage, describes the energy transferred per unit of charge between two points. It is measured in volts (V). A cell or battery provides a potential difference that can transfer energy to components.

For a component, a potential difference of 1 volt means 1 joule of energy is transferred for each coulomb of charge passing through it.

Worked Example C: energy transferred

A component has a potential difference of 6 V. For each coulomb of charge, it transfers 6 joules of energy. If 2 C pass through it, the energy transferred is 6 J/C × 2 C = 12 J.

4. Resistance

Resistance describes how much a component opposes electric current. It is measured in ohms (Ω). For a fixed potential difference, a larger resistance means a smaller current.

For many simple resistors and metal wires under steady conditions, voltage, current and resistance are related by Ohm's law:

V = I × R

Where V is potential difference in volts, I is current in amperes and R is resistance in ohms. This relationship applies to an ohmic conductor when its physical conditions, especially temperature, remain constant.

Worked Example D: use Ohm's law

A 6 V supply is connected across a 3 Ω resistor. The current is:

I = V ÷ R = 6 V ÷ 3 Ω = 2 A

The current is 2 amperes.

5. Series circuits

In a series circuit, components are connected along one path, one after another.

  • The same current passes through every component in the single loop.
  • The supply potential difference is shared between components; the individual voltage drops add to the supply voltage.
  • If one component or connection breaks, the single path is interrupted and all components stop working.
  • For simple resistors connected in series, total resistance is the sum of their resistances.
Worked Example E: series resistance

Two resistors of 2 Ω and 4 Ω are connected in series. Their total resistance is 2 Ω + 4 Ω = 6 Ω.

6. Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit, components are connected on separate branches across the same two connection points.

  • Each branch has the same potential difference as the supply across those two points.
  • Current divides between branches. The total current is the sum of the branch currents.
  • If one branch is disconnected, other complete branches can continue to work.

Household lighting is generally arranged in parallel so appliances can operate independently and receive the supply voltage. Household mains electricity is dangerous and must never be used for classroom experiments.

7. Measuring current and voltage

InstrumentWhat it measuresHow it is connected
AmmeterCurrent, in amperes (A)In series with the component or branch being measured.
VoltmeterPotential difference, in volts (V)In parallel across the component or two points being compared.
Worked Example F: measure a lamp

To measure the current through a lamp, place the ammeter in the same series path as the lamp. To measure the voltage across the lamp, connect the voltmeter across the lamp's two terminals.

8. Electrical safety

  • Use only approved low-voltage classroom cells or battery packs for learning activities.
  • Never experiment with wall sockets, mains wiring or damaged electrical equipment.
  • Do not connect the two terminals of a battery directly with a wire; a short circuit can cause a large current and overheating.
  • Switch off the supply before changing circuit connections.
  • Follow teacher instructions and use suitable equipment.

  1. Explain why a simple circuit needs a complete conducting loop.
  2. Calculate the current when 15 C of charge pass a point in 5 seconds.
  3. A 12 V supply is connected across a 4 Ω resistor. Calculate the current using Ohm's law.
  4. State two differences between a series circuit and a parallel circuit.
  5. Explain how to connect an ammeter and a voltmeter to measure a lamp's current and potential difference.

11. Printable Worksheet

  1. Draw or describe a simple circuit containing a cell, switch, lamp and connecting wires. Explain what happens when the switch is open.
  2. Calculate the current when 20 C of charge pass a point in 4 seconds.
  3. Calculate the current through a 5 Ω resistor connected to a 10 V supply.
  4. Compare current and potential difference, including their units.
  5. Give two differences between series and parallel circuits.
  6. Explain why an ammeter is connected in series and a voltmeter in parallel.

Suggested Answer Features

  1. A complete loop connects both battery terminals through the switch and lamp. An open switch breaks the path, so the lamp does not light.
  2. I = Q ÷ t = 20 C ÷ 4 s = 5 A.
  3. I = V ÷ R = 10 V ÷ 5 Ω = 2 A.
  4. Current is the rate of charge flow and is measured in amperes; potential difference is energy transferred per charge and is measured in volts.
  5. Series circuits have one path and the same current through each component; parallel circuits have branches, the voltage across each branch is the same, and branch currents add.
  6. An ammeter measures the current through a path so it is placed in series; a voltmeter compares two points so it is placed across the component in parallel.

12. Lesson Summary

  • A circuit needs a complete conducting path for continuous current.
  • Current is the rate of charge flow, measured in amperes.
  • Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge, measured in volts.
  • Resistance opposes current and is measured in ohms; V = I × R for ohmic conductors at constant conditions.
  • Series circuits have one path; parallel circuits have separate branches.
  • Use ammeters in series, voltmeters in parallel and only safe low-voltage classroom equipment.
