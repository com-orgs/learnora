Earth and Space Science · Grade 8

Earth, Moon and Space Patterns

Explain familiar patterns in the sky by exploring Earth's rotation and orbit, the Moon's changing appearance and the geometry of eclipses.

Study time: 20–25 minutes · Includes: worked examples, practice, quiz and printable worksheet.

What You Will Learn

  • Explain day and night using Earth's rotation.
  • Describe Earth's orbit and axial tilt as key causes of the seasons.
  • Explain why the Moon appears to change shape during its phases.
  • Compare solar and lunar eclipses.
  • Use simple models to explain the positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon.

1. Earth's rotation: day and night

Earth rotates, or spins, around an imaginary axis that runs through the North and South Poles. One rotation takes about 24 hours. The side facing the Sun experiences daylight; the side facing away experiences night.

The Sun appears to move across the sky during the day because Earth rotates. The Sun is not travelling around Earth each day.

Worked Example 1: When your location rotates from the night side of Earth towards the side facing the Sun, sunrise occurs. About half a rotation later, your location turns away from the Sun and sunset occurs.

2. Earth's orbit and the seasons

Earth travels around the Sun in an orbit that takes about one year. Earth's axis is tilted by about 23.5° relative to the perpendicular to its orbital plane. As Earth orbits the Sun, this tilt causes each hemisphere to receive different angles and durations of sunlight at different times of the year.

When a hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, it generally has longer days and more direct sunlight, producing summer. When it is tilted away, it has shorter days and less direct sunlight, producing winter. The seasons are not caused mainly by Earth being closer to or farther from the Sun.

3. The Moon and its phases

The Moon does not make its own visible light; it reflects sunlight. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different portions of its sunlit half. These changing appearances are called phases.

The main phase sequence is new Moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full Moon, waning gibbous, last (third) quarter and waning crescent. A complete cycle takes about 29.5 days.

Waxing means the visible lit portion is increasing; waning means it is decreasing. The phases are not usually caused by Earth's shadow.

4. Solar and lunar eclipses

An eclipse occurs when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up closely enough for one body to cast a shadow on another.

  • Solar eclipse: the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun, and the Moon's shadow falls on part of Earth. It can happen only near new Moon.
  • Lunar eclipse: Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. It can happen only near full Moon.

Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted slightly compared with Earth's orbital plane, so the three bodies usually do not line up exactly.

Safety: Never look directly at the Sun, including during a partial eclipse. View a solar eclipse only with certified eclipse viewers that meet the relevant safety standard or by using a safe indirect projection method under expert guidance. Ordinary sunglasses are not safe for direct Sun viewing.

5. Modelling the Sun–Earth–Moon system

A model can help explain patterns, but it may not show true sizes or distances. Use a bright lamp as the Sun, a large ball as Earth and a smaller ball as the Moon. Keep the model in a dim room and never use the real Sun as a viewing target.

To model Moon phases, keep the lamp fixed and move the Moon ball around the Earth ball. The fraction of its illuminated side visible from Earth changes with position. To model a lunar eclipse, place Earth between the lamp and Moon so Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

Worked Example 2: If the Moon is positioned between the lamp (Sun) and Earth in a straight line, the model represents a solar eclipse. If Earth is between the lamp and Moon, it represents a lunar eclipse.

6. Independent Practice

  1. Explain why different places on Earth experience day and night at different times.
  2. Give two reasons a hemisphere has warmer summer days than winter days.
  3. Why do Moon phases occur even though the Moon is always approximately half illuminated by the Sun?
  4. Which alignment produces a lunar eclipse?
  5. Explain why eclipses do not happen every new Moon and full Moon.

7. Test Your Understanding

Choose one answer for each question, then check your feedback.

1. What causes day and night?

2. What is the main reason for Earth's seasons?

3. Why does the Moon have phases?

4. Which alignment produces a lunar eclipse?

5. Why is it unsafe to look directly at a solar eclipse?

8. Printable Worksheet

  1. Draw Earth with its axis and label the side experiencing day and the side experiencing night.
  2. Explain how Earth's tilt changes the amount of sunlight received by each hemisphere during the year.
  3. Put the eight main Moon phases in order, starting with new Moon.
  4. Draw and label the alignment for a solar eclipse and a lunar eclipse.
  5. Write two rules for viewing a solar eclipse safely.

Suggested Answer Features

  1. Earth rotates; the side facing the Sun has day and the side facing away has night.
  2. Tilt changes sunlight angle and daylight duration; the hemisphere tilted towards the Sun receives more direct and longer sunlight.
  3. New, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full, waning gibbous, last quarter, waning crescent.
  4. Solar: Moon between Sun and Earth. Lunar: Earth between Sun and Moon.
  5. Use certified eclipse viewers or a safe indirect projection; never look directly at the Sun or rely on ordinary sunglasses.

9. Key Takeaways

  • Earth's rotation causes the daily cycle of day and night.
  • Earth's axial tilt combined with its orbit causes the seasons.
  • Moon phases show changing views of the Moon's sunlit half.
  • Solar and lunar eclipses require particular Sun–Earth–Moon alignments.
  • Never view the Sun directly without certified solar viewing protection.
