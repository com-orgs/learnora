Waves, Sound and Light

Discover how waves transfer energy, how sound travels through matter, and how light reflects and changes direction.

Study time: 20–25 minutes · Includes: diagrams described in words, worked examples, practice, quiz and printable worksheet.

What You Will Learn

  • Describe a wave as a way energy is transferred without an overall transfer of matter.
  • Compare transverse and longitudinal waves.
  • Identify amplitude, wavelength, frequency and wave speed.
  • Explain that sound is produced by vibrations and needs a medium.
  • Describe reflection and refraction of light.

1. What is a wave?

A wave is a travelling disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another. In many waves, particles of the medium vibrate around their usual positions; the medium does not travel along with the wave as a whole.

Water ripples, sound and light are all examples of waves, but they behave differently. Sound is a mechanical wave and needs a material medium. Light is an electromagnetic wave and can travel through a vacuum.

2. Transverse and longitudinal waves

In a transverse wave, the vibrations are perpendicular to the direction the wave travels. A wave travelling along a rope is a useful model: the rope moves up and down while the wave moves along it.

In a longitudinal wave, the vibrations are parallel to the direction the wave travels. Sound in air is longitudinal: air particles vibrate back and forth, creating compressions and rarefactions.

3. Wave properties

  • Amplitude: maximum displacement from the rest position. For many waves, greater amplitude means more energy is carried.
  • Wavelength (λ): distance between matching points on consecutive waves, such as crest to crest.
  • Frequency (f): number of complete waves passing a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz).
  • Wave speed (v): distance travelled by the wave each second, measured in metres per second (m/s).

The relationship is v = f × λ. Use frequency in hertz and wavelength in metres to calculate speed in metres per second.

Worked Example 1: A wave has a frequency of 5 Hz and a wavelength of 2 m. Its speed is v = 5 × 2 = 10 m/s.

4. Sound and vibrations

Sound is produced when an object vibrates. The vibration makes nearby particles in a medium vibrate, passing the disturbance along. Sound can travel through gases, liquids and solids, but it cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles to pass on the vibrations.

In the same medium, a higher-frequency sound is heard as a higher pitch. Greater amplitude is generally heard as a louder sound. Loudness and pitch describe different properties.

Sound usually travels faster in solids than in gases because particles in solids are closely spaced and interact strongly. The exact speed depends on the material and its conditions.

5. Light: reflection and refraction

Light is an electromagnetic transverse wave. It can travel through a vacuum. When light hits a surface, some may be absorbed, transmitted or reflected.

Reflection is the bouncing of light from a surface. For a plane mirror, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; both angles are measured from the normal, an imaginary line at 90° to the surface.

Refraction is a change in direction when light enters a different medium at an angle because its speed changes. Light entering glass from air bends towards the normal; leaving glass for air, it bends away from the normal. If it enters along the normal, its speed changes but its direction does not.

Worked Example 2: A ray strikes a plane mirror at 35° to the normal. The reflected ray also makes an angle of 35° to the normal, on the other side of the normal.

6. The electromagnetic spectrum

Electromagnetic waves transfer energy and can travel through a vacuum. In order of increasing frequency, the main groups are radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. In a vacuum, they all travel at the same speed: approximately 300,000,000 m/s.

Higher-frequency electromagnetic waves have more energy per photon. Some, such as ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, can damage living tissue, so they must be used with appropriate safety precautions.

  2. Describe the particle motion in a longitudinal sound wave.

  4. Explain why astronauts cannot hear each other directly across the vacuum outside a spacecraft.

Suggested Answer Features

  1. Amplitude is measured from rest position to crest or trough; wavelength is between matching points.
  2. Particles vibrate back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels.
  3. v = f × λ = 8 × 0.5 = 4 m/s.
  4. Sound needs a material medium; a vacuum has no particles to transmit it.
  5. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; angles are measured from the normal.

10. Key Takeaways

  • Waves transfer energy; particles of a medium generally vibrate about fixed positions.
  • Transverse and longitudinal waves differ in the direction of particle vibration.
  • Wave speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength.
  • Sound is a mechanical wave and needs a medium; light is electromagnetic and can travel through a vacuum.
  • For a plane mirror, angle of incidence equals angle of reflection; refraction occurs when light changes speed between media.
