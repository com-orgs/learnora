Earth’s Resources, Sustainability and Human Impact

People depend on Earth's resources for food, water, energy, materials and shelter. Sustainable decisions consider environmental effects, human needs and how resources can remain available over time.

Learning time: approximately 30–35 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Distinguish renewable and non-renewable resources.
  • Explain how resource extraction and use can affect ecosystems.
  • Identify sources and impacts of air, water and land pollution.
  • Compare reduce, reuse, repair and recycle strategies.
  • Evaluate sustainability choices using evidence and trade-offs.

1. Renewable and non-renewable resources

Renewable resources are replenished by natural processes on a timescale relevant to human use when managed carefully. Examples include sunlight, wind and sustainably managed forests. Non-renewable resources, such as coal, oil, natural gas and many minerals, form much more slowly than people use them. Renewable does not mean impact-free.

2. How people change ecosystems

Land clearing, intensive extraction, habitat fragmentation, overfishing and invasive species can change ecosystems. Effects may include reduced biodiversity, disrupted food webs, soil erosion and altered water flows. Impacts depend on location, scale, methods and recovery time. Identify the activity, the environmental change and evidence for the effect.

3. Pollution and prevention

Air pollution can come from vehicle exhaust, industry and burning fuels. Water pollution can include excess nutrients, chemicals, oil and plastic waste. Land pollution can result from poorly managed waste and contamination. Prevention—such as treating wastewater and controlling emissions—often works better than cleaning up damage later.

4. Use resources carefully

The waste hierarchy encourages people to reduce unnecessary consumption first, then reuse and repair items where practical, and recycle suitable materials. Recycling can conserve raw materials, but it also uses energy and does not make unlimited consumption sustainable. Efficiency and conservation can reduce energy demand.

5. Evaluate a sustainability decision

Consider evidence and trade-offs: who benefits, who may be affected, what resources are required, what happens over time and how success will be measured. For a school rainwater tank, consider rainfall reliability, safe storage, maintenance, cost and which uses local rules permit. A solution can help without being perfect; explain its limits.

Worked Example

A school wants to reduce waste. Students first measure the amount and type of rubbish produced. They may then reduce single-use packaging, provide clearly labelled sorting bins and reuse suitable materials. After a trial, they compare new measurements with the starting data and check whether contamination has increased.

Explain the main idea in your own words before moving to the quiz.

Knowledge Check

Choose one answer for each question, then select Check answers. Read the feedback even when your answer is correct.

1. Which resource is generally non-renewable on a human timescale?

2. Why can a renewable resource need careful management?

3. Which action is highest in the waste hierarchy?

4. What is a useful first step in evaluating a school waste plan?

5. What is a trade-off?
