Human Body Systems: Digestion and Circulation

The digestive system breaks food into small molecules that the body can absorb. The circulatory system transports these useful substances, along with oxygen, to cells throughout the body.

Study time: 25–30 minutes · Includes: labelled process tables, worked examples, practice, five-question quiz and printable worksheet.

What You Will Learn

  • Describe the main organs of the digestive system and their roles.
  • Explain mechanical and chemical digestion in simple terms.
  • Describe how nutrients are absorbed in the small intestine.
  • Identify the main parts of the circulatory system and the direction blood travels.
  • Explain how digestion and circulation work together to supply body cells.

1. Why do humans need these systems?

Food contains nutrients that provide energy and materials for growth and repair. Most food molecules are too large to pass directly into the blood, so digestion breaks many of them into smaller, soluble molecules. The circulatory system then carries absorbed nutrients to cells.

Cells also need oxygen for aerobic respiration. Blood transports oxygen from the lungs to body tissues and carries carbon dioxide away to be removed by the lungs.

Remember the difference: digestion breaks food down; absorption moves small soluble nutrients through the gut wall; circulation transports substances around the body.

2. Follow food through the digestive system

Organ or structureMain role
MouthTeeth break food into smaller pieces. Saliva moistens food, and salivary amylase begins the digestion of starch.
Oesophagus (food pipe)Muscle contractions called peristalsis move food towards the stomach.
StomachMuscular walls churn food. Acid creates conditions for enzymes such as pepsin to begin protein digestion and helps kill many microbes.
Small intestineDigestion is completed with help from enzymes. Most absorption of digested nutrients takes place through the intestinal wall.
Large intestineAbsorbs water and some salts from the remaining material, helping form faeces.
Rectum and anusThe rectum stores faeces before they leave the body through the anus.

The liver makes bile, which is stored in the gallbladder and released into the small intestine. Bile is not an enzyme: it helps break large fat droplets into smaller droplets (emulsification), increasing the surface area for lipase to act.

Worked Example A: why chew food?

Chewing is mechanical digestion. It breaks food into smaller pieces and increases the surface area exposed to digestive juices. It does not by itself turn starch into sugar; enzymes in saliva begin that chemical process.

3. Mechanical and chemical digestion

Mechanical digestion physically breaks food into smaller pieces without changing the food molecules themselves. Chewing and stomach churning are examples.

Chemical digestion uses enzymes to break large food molecules into smaller molecules that can be absorbed. Enzymes work best under suitable conditions, including an appropriate temperature and pH.

Enzyme groupActs onSmaller products
AmylaseStarchSmaller carbohydrates, including maltose
Proteases (such as pepsin)ProteinsShorter peptides and eventually amino acids
LipaseFats (lipids)Fatty acids and glycerol (simplified school-level description)
Worked Example B: choose the enzyme

A meal contains bread, which is rich in starch. Amylase helps digest the starch into smaller carbohydrates. Protease is not the enzyme that breaks down starch.

4. How are nutrients absorbed?

The small intestine has many tiny finger-like projections called villi. Each villus has a thin surface and a good blood supply, helping digested nutrients enter the body efficiently.

  • Glucose and amino acids pass into blood capillaries in the villi and are carried towards the liver and then around the body.
  • Many products of fat digestion enter tiny lymph vessels called lacteals before eventually joining the bloodstream.
  • The large intestine absorbs water and some salts from the material that remains.

Absorption is different from digestion: digestion makes small molecules, while absorption moves them across the gut wall.

5. The circulatory system

The circulatory system includes the heart, blood and blood vessels. The heart is a muscular pump that keeps blood moving through two connected circuits.

Blood vesselDirection and structureMain function
ArteriesCarry blood away from the heart; usually have thick, elastic walls.Carry blood under relatively high pressure from the heart to organs.
VeinsCarry blood towards the heart; many contain valves.Return blood to the heart at lower pressure.
CapillariesMicroscopic vessels with very thin walls.Allow exchange of gases, nutrients and waste substances between blood and tissues.
Important exception: arteries are defined by carrying blood away from the heart, not by always carrying oxygen-rich blood. The pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood from the heart to the lungs; pulmonary veins return oxygen-rich blood to the heart.

6. The heart and two blood circuits

The human heart has four chambers: the right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle. Valves help prevent blood from flowing backwards.

  1. Pulmonary circuit: oxygen-poor blood returns from the body to the right side of the heart. The right ventricle pumps it to the lungs, where carbon dioxide leaves the blood and oxygen enters it.
  2. Systemic circuit: oxygen-rich blood returns from the lungs to the left side of the heart. The left ventricle pumps it to the rest of the body.
Worked Example C: trace the route

Blood returning from the body enters the right atrium, moves to the right ventricle and is pumped to the lungs. After picking up oxygen, it returns to the left atrium, moves to the left ventricle and is pumped around the body.

7. What is blood made of?

ComponentRole
PlasmaThe liquid part of blood; transports dissolved substances such as nutrients, hormones, carbon dioxide and waste products.
Red blood cellsContain haemoglobin, which carries most oxygen in the blood.
White blood cellsHelp defend the body against pathogens and infection.
PlateletsHelp blood clot and reduce blood loss when a blood vessel is damaged.

8. How the systems work together

After a meal, digested nutrients such as glucose and amino acids are absorbed through the small intestine into the blood. The circulatory system transports them to body cells. Oxygen from the lungs is also carried by the blood. Cells use nutrients and oxygen in respiration to release energy for their activities.

Worked Example D: after eating

After eating a sandwich, starch digestion begins in the mouth and continues in the small intestine. Glucose produced by digestion is absorbed into the blood. The blood carries it to cells, where it can be used in respiration.

11. Printable Worksheet

  1. Put these structures in the correct order for food: stomach, mouth, small intestine, oesophagus.
  2. State one function of bile and explain why bile is not an enzyme.
  3. Explain how villi help absorption in the small intestine.
  4. Complete: arteries carry blood ______ the heart; veins carry blood ______ the heart.
  5. Name the four main components of blood and give one role for each.
  6. Explain in three or four sentences how a nutrient from food can reach a muscle cell.

Suggested Answer Features

  1. Mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine.
  2. Bile emulsifies fats into smaller droplets, increasing surface area for lipase; bile is not an enzyme because it does not chemically break fat molecules.
  3. Villi provide a large surface area, thin walls and a good blood supply for efficient absorption.
  4. Away from; towards.
  5. Plasma transports dissolved substances; red cells carry most oxygen; white cells defend against pathogens; platelets help clotting.
  6. Food is digested into small soluble nutrients, absorbed through the small intestine, transported in blood and delivered to cells.

12. Lesson Summary

  • Digestion breaks food into small molecules; mechanical and chemical processes both help.
  • The small intestine is the main site where digested nutrients are absorbed.
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return it and capillaries allow exchange with tissues.
  • Blood contains plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.
  • The digestive system supplies nutrients and the circulatory system transports them to cells.
