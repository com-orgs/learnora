Free printable practice · Grades 6–8

International English and Science Practice Workbook

Twenty original middle-school questions to practise reading, writing, language conventions and scientific reasoning. Each subject section has a worked answer key.

How to use: Try each question without notes, show your working or reasoning, then open the answer key. Use the print control to print the questions and explanations.

Scope: Original supplementary practice, not official examination papers or a complete standards assessment. Grade and year expectations vary by location; use the current local curriculum and teacher guidance.

English practice

Reading comprehension · vocabulary · grammar · punctuation · writing

1. Context clues. “After walking for hours over the steep, rocky path, the hikers felt weary.” What does weary most nearly mean? Explain the clue.
2. Inference. “Amir checked the bus timetable twice, moved closer to the shelter and zipped his coat when the wind grew stronger.” What can you infer? Give two clues.
3. Main idea. A paragraph says community gardens provide fresh produce, give neighbours a shared project and create habitats for pollinators. Write its main idea in one sentence.
4. Subject–verb agreement. Choose and explain: “The collection of short stories (is / are) on the top shelf.”
5. Punctuation. Correct: “before the match we packed water snacks and a first aid kit”
6. Sentence structure. In “Although the rain stopped, the field remained wet,” identify the dependent and independent clauses.
7. Text evidence. Which better supports “The reading club became popular”: “The library was quiet” or “By the fourth week, the group had grown so much that they needed a larger room”? Explain.
8. Writing. Improve this weak topic sentence into a clear, focused claim: “Sports are good.”
9. Word parts. What does prefix inter- mean in international? Give one other word using it.
10. Figurative language. Identify the technique in “The classroom was a beehive of activity” and explain the comparison.
Show worked answers — English
  1. Tired or exhausted. Hours of walking on a steep, rocky path suggest the hikers have used much energy.
  2. Amir expects the bus and is preparing for colder weather. Checking the timetable and moving to the shelter suggest he is waiting; the wind explains zipping his coat. Other answers are acceptable when supported by clues.
  3. Community gardens benefit neighbourhoods in several ways. Produce, shared work and pollinator habitats are supporting details.
  4. Is. The subject is the singular noun “collection”; “of short stories” is a prepositional phrase.
  5. Before the match, we packed water, snacks and a first-aid kit. Capitalise the first word, add a comma after the introductory phrase and punctuate the list.
  6. Dependent: “Although the rain stopped.” Independent: “the field remained wet.”
  7. The second detail. It directly shows attendance grew enough to require a larger room.
  8. Example: “Team sports can help students build cooperation and communication skills.” A strong topic sentence makes a specific point that later sentences can support.
  9. Between or among. “Interconnected” uses the same prefix.
  10. Metaphor. The classroom is compared directly to a busy beehive to suggest simultaneous activity.

Science practice

Cells · ecosystems · forces · matter · heat · electricity · waves · inquiry

1. Cells. Which structure releases usable energy from food during cellular respiration: nucleus, cell membrane or mitochondrion? State its role.
2. Ecosystems. In grass → rabbit → fox, which organism is the producer? Where does most energy entering this food chain originally come from?
3. Forces. A trolley experiences a 5 N force east and a 3 N force west. Find the resultant force and direction.
4. Particle model. What happens to particles when liquid water evaporates? Does each water molecule become a different substance?
5. Heat transfer. How does energy from the Sun reach Earth through space? Name the transfer method.
6. Mixtures. A mixture contains sand and dissolved salt in water. Describe how to separate and collect the sand and salt.
7. Circuits. A circuit has a 6 V supply and a 3 Ω resistor. Using V = I × R, calculate the current.
8. Waves. A wave travels at 12 m/s and has a frequency of 3 Hz. Calculate its wavelength.
9. Earth and space. What causes the regular cycle of day and night on Earth?
10. Fair testing. A student tests whether light intensity affects plant growth. Name one variable to change, one outcome to measure and two factors to keep constant.
Show worked answers — Science
  1. Mitochondrion. Mitochondria are a main site of aerobic cellular respiration in many eukaryotic cells, transferring energy from food molecules into ATP.
  2. Grass is the producer. It makes organic food by photosynthesis; the energy entering the food chain originally comes from the Sun.
  3. 2 N east. Opposing forces subtract: 5 − 3 = 2 N, in the direction of the larger force.
  4. Some faster-moving surface particles escape into the gas state. They remain water molecules; evaporation is a physical change.
  5. Radiation. Electromagnetic waves travel through a vacuum; conduction and convection require matter.
  6. Filter, then evaporate. Filtration traps insoluble sand. Evaporating the water from the salt solution leaves salt crystals. Follow teacher-approved lab safety instructions.
  7. I = V ÷ R = 6 ÷ 3 = 2 A. The current is 2 amperes in this idealised circuit.
  8. Wavelength = speed ÷ frequency = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 m. The units reduce to metres.
  9. Earth's rotation on its axis. The side facing the Sun has daylight while the side turned away has night.
  10. Example: Change light intensity; measure plant height or leaf number after a fixed time; keep species, water, soil and temperature constant. Use repeats where possible.
