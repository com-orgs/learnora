AI Bias, Privacy and Safe Use

AI tools can affect what people see, learn and decide. Responsible use means understanding limitations, noticing unfairness and protecting personal information. Follow age requirements, school rules and guidance from a trusted adult.

Learning time: 15–20 minutes · Beginner level · Free study resource

What you will learn

  • Explain how incomplete data can produce unfair results.
  • Recognise sensitive personal information.
  • Identify safer ways to use AI for learning.
  • Know when to ask an adult or teacher for help.

1. Understand bias and fairness

AI bias means a system can produce systematically uneven or harmful results for some people or situations. One possible cause is training or test data that does not represent the people who will use the system. Bias can also come from design choices, how a system is tested, the setting where it is used and how people interpret its output. Bias does not always require someone to intend harm.

Worked scenario: a school reading helper

Imagine a school tests a reading-support tool using examples from only one class. Learners in other year groups, learners who use different varieties of English, or learners who use assistive technology may have different experiences. The small test sample does not prove the tool is biased, but it is a warning that the school should test a wider range of relevant examples before relying on it.

  1. Ask who was included in the training or test examples, if that information is available.
  2. Ask whose experiences may be missing.
  3. Try appropriate examples from different groups and situations.
  4. Have a teacher review important results rather than treating the system as the final decision-maker.

NIST describes several kinds of bias, including systemic, computational/statistical and human-cognitive bias. For a student, the practical lesson is to question whether a result is reliable and fair in its real context—not just whether it sounds confident.

2. Protect privacy

Personal information can identify or describe you or another person. Do not enter passwords, one-time codes, home addresses, private phone numbers, school records, financial details, private messages or identifying photographs into a public AI tool.

Safer example: Instead of uploading a class list with names and marks, ask a general question such as “How can I make a revision timetable for three subjects?” You can get help without sharing classmates’ details.
  • Share the minimum information needed; use fictional examples instead of real personal records.
  • Check the tool’s current age requirements and privacy information with a parent, guardian or teacher.
  • Check whether the service may store prompts or use them to improve its systems. Settings and terms differ between services and can change.
  • Do not assume that deleting a chat removes every copy or record held by a service.
  • If you are unsure, stop and ask a trusted adult before uploading or sharing anything.

UNICEF’s 2025 guidance on AI and children identifies children’s data and privacy as a core requirement for child-centred AI. Privacy is not only about keeping passwords secret; it is also about having a say in what information is collected and how it is used.

3. Use AI honestly and safely

AI may help explain a concept, suggest revision questions or help you plan a project when your teacher and the tool’s rules allow it. It can still make mistakes, leave out important context or produce unsuitable material.

A responsible study routine
  1. Ask for an explanation of a topic you are studying.
  2. Compare important facts with your textbook, teacher’s notes or a reliable source.
  3. Solve a practice question yourself before looking at a suggested solution.
  4. Follow your school’s rules and disclose AI assistance when required.
  5. Ask a teacher or trusted adult if an answer is confusing, upsetting or potentially harmful.
  • Do not use AI to bully, impersonate, threaten or embarrass someone.
  • Do not treat an AI chatbot as a trusted adult, counsellor or emergency service.
  • If a tool requests private details, produces frightening content or makes you feel unsafe, stop using it and tell a trusted adult.

UNESCO recommends a human-centred and age-appropriate approach to generative AI in education. Tool age rules vary; there is no single age rule that should be assumed to apply to every service or country. Always check the current service terms, local requirements and school guidance.

Common mistakes to avoid

Assuming one successful test proves an AI system is fair for everyone. Ask which people and situations were included.
Sharing real personal records because a chatbot asks for more detail. Use a fictional example instead.
Believing that a confident answer must be correct. Check important claims and ask a teacher when needed.
Assuming every AI service has the same minimum age or privacy settings. Read the current rules for the specific service.
Treating an AI companion as a real friend or trusted adult. Get help from a person you trust.

4. Practice with worked answers

Try each situation first. Then open the explanation and compare your reasoning.

Scenario 1: a biased recommendation

A school activity recommender was tested only with students from one year group. What should the school do before using it for all students?

Suggested response: Test it with relevant examples from other year groups and different learner needs, check who may be missing from the data, and ask teachers and students to review possible problems. The limited test is a reason to investigate, not proof by itself that the system is unfair.

Scenario 2: a private document

A chatbot offers to summarise a report that contains students’ names, grades and support needs. What is a safer approach?

Suggested response: Do not upload the identifiable report to a public tool. Ask a teacher or authorised adult what service is approved. If an approved tool is allowed, use only the minimum necessary information and remove identifying details where appropriate.

Scenario 3: an AI-generated answer

An AI gives you a science explanation that conflicts with your textbook. What should you do?

Suggested response: Do not copy either answer without checking. Compare the claim with your textbook and a reliable scientific source, then ask your teacher to help resolve the difference.

5. Independent practice

  1. Write five examples of information you should not share with a public chatbot.
  2. Describe one way a test dataset could leave out a group of learners. Explain how you would check for this.
  3. Write three steps you would take before using an AI-generated explanation in schoolwork.
  4. Explain why an AI chatbot should not replace a trusted adult when a learner feels unsafe.
Show suggested answer features
  1. Examples include passwords, one-time codes, home address, private phone number, school records, financial information and private messages.
  2. For example, a test set might contain examples from only one year group. Test with a wider range of relevant learners and situations, and review results with teachers.
  3. Check the claim against a textbook or reliable source; solve or explain it in your own words; follow school rules and disclose AI help when required.
  4. A chatbot cannot provide the same real-world care, safeguarding or emergency response as a responsible person. Stop and speak to a parent, guardian, teacher or another trusted adult.

Teacher / Parent Suggested Answer Key
  1. Check age requirements, school approval, privacy information, what data is collected or stored, and whether the information being shared identifies anyone. Use only the minimum necessary details.
  2. Ask which learners and situations were represented; ask who may be missing; request testing with a wider range of relevant examples; have a teacher review possible impacts. A narrow test does not alone prove bias.
  3. Protect personal information; verify important outputs; follow school rules and disclose assistance when required. Other valid safe-use rules may be accepted.
  4. Identify the exact claim, compare it with the textbook and a reliable source, note uncertainty, and ask a teacher to help resolve the conflict.
  5. Stop sharing information and tell a parent, guardian, teacher or another trusted adult. Do not continue because the tool asks again.

7. Lesson recap

  • AI systems can produce uneven results because of data, design, testing and human-use factors.
  • Ask who is represented, who may be missing and who could be harmed.
  • Keep passwords, private records and identifying details out of public AI tools.
  • Check the age rules, privacy information and school guidance for each service.
  • Verify important AI outputs and involve a trusted adult when something is unsafe or unclear.
  • Use AI honestly and follow school rules.

Sources and further reading

These primary guidance resources support the lesson’s explanations of fairness, privacy, safety and age-appropriate educational use. They are background references, not a claim that Learnora is endorsed by these organisations.
