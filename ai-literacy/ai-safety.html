AI Bias, Privacy and Safe Use

AI tools can affect what people see, learn and decide. Responsible use means understanding limitations, noticing unfairness and protecting personal information. Follow age requirements, school rules and guidance from a trusted adult.

Learning time: 35–40 minutes · Beginner level · Free study resource

What you will learn

  • Explain how biased or incomplete data can lead to unfair AI results — with real examples.
  • Recognise personal and sensitive information and know what never to share with a public AI tool.
  • Use AI tools safely: age rules, school rules, and checking before you share.
  • Use AI honestly for school work, including when to disclose that you used it.
  • Know what to do when an AI tool asks for something private or shows something worrying.
  • Follow a five-step safe-use routine every time you open an AI tool.

1. Bias and fairness: when data leaves people out

An AI system learns from the data it is given. If that data does not represent everyone the system will be used on, the system can work well for some people and badly for others. That is called bias: a systematic unfairness that comes from the data, the design, or the way the system is tested.

Consider a fictional example. A company builds a tool that reads job applications and picks the best ones. It is trained on applications from the past ten years, when most successful applicants were men. The tool learns the pattern "men are usually picked" and starts ranking women's applications lower — even when they are equally good. The tool was not programmed to be unfair. It learned an unfair pattern from unfair history. The fix is not to blame the tool: it is to fix the data, test the tool on different groups, and keep a person in charge of the final decision.

Fairness is not only about the training data. It is also about testing. A voice assistant tested only on adult voices will misunderstand children. A photo app tested only on light skin tones will do worse on dark skin tones. Good testing means checking the system on the different groups of people who will actually use it, and asking: "Who is missing? Who could be harmed if this goes wrong?"

Worked example 1 — the missing group. A school uses an AI tool to recommend books. It was trained on book choices from one school where most pupils read adventure stories. A new pupil who loves science books keeps getting adventure recommendations and never sees a science book. Step 1: ask whose choices were in the training data (mostly adventure readers). Step 2: ask who is missing (science readers). Step 3: the fix — add science readers' choices to the data and test the recommendations on them. Check: a system is only as fair as the people its data represents.
Worked example 2 — the unfair pattern. A fictional hiring tool rejects an application because the applicant went to a school the tool has rarely seen. Step 1: the tool learned "familiar school = good applicant" from past data. Step 2: that pattern punishes people from less common schools, which is unfair. Step 3: the right response is to remove the school name from what the tool sees, and let a person make the decision. Check: if a pattern would be unfair as a human rule, it is unfair as a machine pattern too.

2. Privacy: what never to share

Many AI tools are public services: what you type can be stored, reviewed by the company, and sometimes used to train future systems. That is why some information should never be typed into a public AI tool. The list is short and worth memorising: passwords and one-time codes; your home address; your phone number or private email; your school records or grades; financial details such as bank or card information; and photos or details that identify you or your family.

The rule is simple: share the minimum information needed to get help. If you need help with a maths problem, type the maths problem — not your name, your school, or a photo of your homework with your name on it. If a tool asks for personal details before it will help, stop and ask a parent, guardian or teacher. A good learning tool does not need your private life to explain fractions.

Also remember that "delete" does not always mean "gone". Some services keep copies of conversations for safety checks or training. Assume that anything you type into a public tool could be kept, and type accordingly. School accounts and tools your school has approved are different — your school has checked their privacy rules. Use those when your teacher tells you to.

Worked example 3 — minimum information. Sara wants help with a science question: "Why does the Moon look different shapes?" She could type: "Hi, I am Sara, I am 12, I go to Greenhill School, my email is sara@example.com, why does the Moon look different shapes?" Step 1: cross out everything the tool does not need — name, age, school, email. Step 2: what is left is the question itself: "Why does the Moon look different shapes?" Step 3: that is all the tool needs. Check: if the answer is good, it is good because of the question, not because of the personal details.
Worked example 4 — the tool that asks too much. A free AI homework app asks Ali to create an account with his full name, date of birth, school name and a photo before it will answer a question. Step 1: the app is asking for sensitive information it does not need. Step 2: Ali should stop, not fill in the form, and tell a parent or teacher. Step 3: a trusted adult can check whether the app is approved by the school or should be avoided. Check: a tool that demands personal details for a simple question is a warning sign, not a normal step.

3. Age rules and school rules

Many AI tools have age limits. Some require users to be 13 or older; some require 18, or a parent's permission below that age. These limits exist because the tools were not designed for young children, and because the law in many countries protects children's data more strictly than adults' data. UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education also recommends an age limit for independent use of these platforms, and a human-centred, age-appropriate approach to checking them.

Your school has rules too. Some schools allow AI tools for certain tasks and not for others. Some require you to say when you used one. The rules are not there to spoil the fun — they exist because the school is responsible for your learning and your safety. If you are unsure whether a tool is allowed for a task, ask your teacher before you use it. "I did not know" is not a good defence after the work is handed in.

UNICEF's Guidance on AI and Children (version 3.0, 2025) sets out ten requirements for child-centred AI, including safety, data protection, fairness, transparency and support for children's development. The guidance is written for governments and companies, but its message for learners is simple: AI systems should be designed to protect you, and you should be told clearly when you are talking to an AI rather than a person.

Worked example 5 — checking the rules first. Bilal, 12, wants to use a new AI chat app for a history project. Step 1: check the app's age rule — it says users must be 13 or older. Step 2: check the school's rule — his teacher has said AI tools may be used for finding starting points, but not for writing the essay. Step 3: Bilal is under the age limit, so he does not use the app. Instead, he asks his teacher for an approved tool, and uses it only to find starting points. Check: following both rules took two minutes and kept him safe and honest.

4. Using AI honestly for school work

AI can be a good study helper: it can explain a topic in simpler words, make practice questions, check your understanding, or give feedback on a draft. It becomes a problem when it replaces your thinking — when you hand in generated text as your own work, or when you copy an answer without understanding it. Your teacher is marking your understanding, not the tool's.

Three habits keep your use honest. First, use AI to learn, not to finish: ask it to explain, then write the answer yourself. Second, check what it gives you — the verify-ai lesson shows how. Third, disclose it when your school asks: a short note such as "I used an AI tool to find starting points, then checked each claim against the sources listed" is honest and takes ten seconds.

If a task says "no AI help", that means no AI help — including asking a chatbot to rephrase your work or to write your introduction. The rule is about the work being yours. If you are ever unsure, ask the teacher. Teachers would much rather answer the question beforehand than discover the problem afterwards.

Worked example 6 — help or cheating? A student asks an AI tool: "Explain photosynthesis in simple words." She reads the explanation, then writes her own paragraph from memory and checks it against her textbook. Step 1: she used the tool to learn. Step 2: the final words are hers. Step 3: she can explain the idea if asked. That is honest use. Compare: another student asks the tool to "write my paragraph on photosynthesis" and copies it in. Step 1: the words are not his. Step 2: he cannot explain them if asked. That is not honest use, even if the paragraph is correct.

5. When things go wrong

Sometimes an AI tool will do something worrying: it may ask for personal information, give instructions for something dangerous, produce an image of a real person that never happened, or write a message that pretends to be from someone you know. AI-generated images and messages can now look very real, and they are increasingly used in scams — for example, a message that sounds like a teacher or a relative asking for money or a password.

If anything like that happens, follow three steps. First, stop: do not reply, do not share anything, and do not forward the message. Second, save evidence if it is safe to do so — a screenshot can help an adult understand what happened. Third, tell a trusted adult — a parent, guardian or teacher — the same day. You will not be in trouble for reporting something; adults need to know so they can protect you and others.

The same applies to AI-generated pictures and videos of real people. Creating or sharing a fake image of a real person — even as a joke — can harm them seriously and can break school rules and the law. If you see one, do not share it, and tell an adult.

Worked example 7 — the message that is not from your teacher. A student receives a message that looks like it is from her teacher: "Please send your password so I can check your homework." Step 1: stop — a real teacher would never ask for a password by message. Step 2: do not reply or send anything. Step 3: tell the real teacher in person the next day, and show the message. Check: the message used the teacher's name and a friendly tone, but the request itself — a password — is the warning sign. Tone is easy to fake; the request is what matters.
Worked example 8 — the five-step safe-use routine. Before opening any AI tool, run through five steps: (1) Is this tool approved by my school or a trusted adult? (2) Am I old enough to use it? (3) What personal information will I share — can I share less? (4) What will I do with the answer — check it before using it? (5) If something goes wrong, who will I tell? Worked check: a student wants a maths explanation. (1) Yes, the school approved it. (2) Yes. (3) She will type only the maths question. (4) She will check the answer against her textbook. (5) Her teacher. Five steps, thirty seconds, and the tool is safe to use.

Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1: "The system is automatic, so it must be fair." Wrong: automatic systems learn from human data, including human unfairness. Correct: ask who is in the data, who is missing, and who could be harmed.
Mistake 2: "It is only a chatbot, so sharing is harmless." Wrong: what you type can be stored and used. Correct: share the minimum — the question, not your life.
Mistake 3: "The age limit is just a suggestion." Wrong: age limits protect your data and your safety. Correct: follow them, and ask an adult for an approved alternative.
Mistake 4: "If I rephrase the AI text, it becomes mine." Wrong: changing a few words does not make generated work your own. Correct: learn from it, then write your own answer from understanding.
Mistake 5: "A friendly tone means it is safe." Wrong: scams and unsafe requests often sound friendly and helpful. Correct: judge the request — what is it asking you to do or share? — not the tone.
Mistake 6: "Reporting a problem will get me in trouble." Wrong: adults need to know so they can help. Correct: tell a trusted adult the same day, and keep evidence if it is safe to do so.

2. List four things you should never type into a public AI tool.

8. A voice assistant misunderstands a pupil with a strong accent five times in a row. What is the likely cause, and what should the makers do?

9. Write the five-step safe-use routine in your own words.

10. A friend shows you an AI-generated photo of a real classmate in a situation that never happened. What should you do, and why?

11. A job tool trained on past hires rejects applicants from one town again and again. Explain the bias in two sentences.

12. Your school allows AI tools for finding starting points but not for writing essays. You used one to find starting points. What should you write when you hand in your work?

Practice answers

  2. Any four of: passwords or one-time codes; home address; phone number or private email; school records or grades; bank or card details; photos or details that identify you or your family.
  3. Tell them the tool does not need those details to answer a homework question, and that what they type can be stored. Suggest they ask a teacher for an approved tool instead.
  4. Do not use it. Ask a parent or teacher for an approved alternative that is suitable for your age.
  5. Yes. She used the tool to learn, and the final words and understanding are hers.
  6. No. Changing a few words does not make generated work your own, and she cannot explain it as her own understanding.
  7. 1. Stop — do not reply or send anything. 2. Save evidence if safe (a screenshot). 3. Tell a trusted adult the same day.
  8. The likely cause is that the training data did not include enough voices with that accent. The makers should add such voices to the data and test on them.
  9. Accept a correct version of: (1) approved tool? (2) old enough? (3) share the minimum? (4) check the answer? (5) who to tell if something goes wrong?

Lesson summary

  • Bias comes from data that leaves people out. Ask who is missing and who could be harmed.
  • Never share passwords, addresses, school records or financial details with a public AI tool — share the minimum.
  • Follow age limits and school rules. If you are unsure, ask a teacher first.
  • Use AI to learn, not to finish: explain it, check it, write your own answer, and disclose it when asked.
