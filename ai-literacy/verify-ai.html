How to Check AI Answers and Sources

AI tools can explain ideas, but they can also invent details, mix up sources or omit context. Treat a generated response as a starting point and verify important claims using reliable evidence.

Learning time: 15–20 minutes · Beginner level · Free study resource

What you will learn

  • Separate factual claims from opinions.
  • Trace claims to original trustworthy sources.
  • Check dates and whether evidence supports a claim.
  • Explain uncertainty and cite sources honestly.

1. Break an answer into claims

An AI response may contain several statements. Do not check a long paragraph as if it were one claim. Break it into smaller statements that can be checked separately.

  • Names: Is the person, organisation or place identified correctly?
  • Dates and numbers: Does the evidence show the same date, amount or measurement?
  • Scope: Does the statement apply to one school, one country, a certain age group or everyone?
  • Cause and effect: Does the evidence show that one thing caused another, or only that they happened together?
  • Quotes: Can you find the original wording and confirm who said it?
Worked example: split the claim

AI says: “A new rule began in 2024 and applies to every school in the world.” This contains at least two claims: (1) a rule began in 2024; (2) the rule applies to every school worldwide. A source might support the first but not the second. Check the date and the geographical/legal scope separately.

2. Inspect the source

Open the cited page itself. A title, search snippet or AI-generated citation is not enough. Ask who created the information, why it was created, what evidence it gives and whether it is suitable for the exact question.

  1. Author or organisation: Who is responsible for the page? Can you learn more about them from sources outside their own website?
  2. Purpose and audience: Is it informing, teaching, selling, entertaining or persuading? Who is it written for?
  3. Evidence: Does it show data, documents, quotations, methods or links that support the claim?
  4. Date and context: When was it published or updated? Does the information change quickly? Does the date and location match your question?
  5. Fit: Does the page support the exact sentence, or does it only discuss a similar topic?

A primary source is original or first-hand material for a particular question, such as the text of a law, an original research paper, a measurement record or an interview. A secondary source explains or analyses information from other sources. Either can be useful. A primary source is not automatically complete or unbiased; consider its context and purpose.

Choose a source that fits the claim:
  • For the exact wording of a current law, start with the official legal text for the relevant place.
  • For what a research study actually found, locate the original paper and check its methods and results.
  • For a beginner explanation, a recognised educational or scientific organisation may be easier to understand—but trace important claims back to the evidence when possible.

3. Record uncertainty

Use a clear status instead of forcing every claim into “true” or “false”. A source may support only part of a statement, be out of date or fail to answer the question.

  • Supported: suitable evidence directly supports the claim.
  • Contradicted: reliable evidence conflicts with the claim.
  • Partly supported: some details are supported but the full statement goes further.
  • Unverified: you could not find enough suitable evidence to decide.

If sources disagree, compare their dates, definitions, methods, locations and original evidence. Two websites repeating the same press release are not necessarily two independent confirmations. For important claims, look for independent sources that reach the evidence separately.

Worked example: an old school notice

This is a fictional example. An AI answer says: “The school library opens at 7:30 every day” and links to a notice from the previous school year. The current school information page says the library opens at 8:00 on weekdays and is closed on Sunday.

  1. Open the cited notice and check its date: it is from last year.
  2. Open the current official school information page.
  3. Compare the exact time and the word “every day”.
  4. Record the AI claim as outdated or contradicted by the current schedule; ask the school office if the current page is unclear.

The example shows why the source date and the exact scope of a claim matter.

Never invent a citation, fill in missing publication details or say you checked a source you did not open. If a claim remains uncertain, say so clearly.

4. Read beyond the page: lateral checking

Vertical reading means staying on one website and judging it from what it says about itself. Lateral reading means opening other reliable sources in new tabs to find out who is behind the site and how independent sources describe it.

  1. Pause before trusting an unfamiliar website.
  2. Search the organisation or author name outside that website.
  3. Look for independent reporting, recognised reference sources or relevant professional information.
  4. Compare what those sources say with the claim you are checking.
Simple example: A website calls itself “the world's leading science centre”. Do not accept that description just because it appears on the About page. Search for independent information about the organisation, its work and its reputation.

Lateral reading helps investigate the source itself. You must still open and check the evidence for the specific claim.

Common mistakes to avoid

Trusting a generated citation without opening it. A citation can be incomplete, inaccurate or invented.
Treating a source as reliable only because it looks professional or uses a familiar logo. Check who is responsible and what evidence is provided.
Assuming the newest source is always best. Current information matters for changing topics, while older original records may be essential for historical questions.
Using one source to support a broader claim than it actually makes. Check the exact wording, date, place and group covered.
Treating two pages that copy the same report as independent confirmation.
Calling a claim false just because you cannot find it. If evidence is missing, mark it unverified.

Practice exercises

Try each question first, then open the suggested reasoning. Use the fictional examples as practice, not as claims about a real school or organisation.

1. Split a broad claim into checkable parts

Claim: “A new school rule started in 2025 and every school in the country must follow it.”

Suggested response: Check separately whether the rule exists, when it started, which country or region it applies to, which schools are covered and whether exceptions exist. A source confirming the date may not prove the claim about every school.

2. Check whether a citation supports the claim

An AI answer says a report proves that all students learn better with one study method. The linked report only describes a small group at one school.

Suggested response: The source does not support the word “all”. Record the claim as overstated or only partly supported. Check the study's sample, method and limits before describing its findings.

3. Investigate an unfamiliar website

A website says it is an independent expert organisation. What could you do beyond reading its About page?

Suggested response: Search for the organisation and its authors elsewhere. Compare independent descriptions, relevant expertise, published work and any reliable reporting. Then check the evidence for the particular claim separately.

4. Decide whether a date matters

Would you use the same date-checking approach for a current school timetable and a historical photograph?

Suggested response: No. A timetable changes, so the current official version matters. A historical photograph may need an original older record and context from the time. “Newer” does not automatically mean “better” for every question.

5. Handle a citation you cannot find

An AI answer names a journal article, but searches of the journal and library catalogue do not find it.

Suggested response: Do not invent a link or pretend it exists. Mark the citation and related claim unverified, look for another genuine source, and tell the reader what could not be confirmed.

Knowledge check: five questions

Choose one answer for each question. Feedback explains every answer.

1. What should you do with an important AI claim?
2. Best source for the wording of a law?
3. Why check a source date?
4. What if a generated citation cannot be found?
5. What must a source do to support a claim?

Teacher / Parent Suggested Answer Key
  1. Possible parts: whether the app launched; the launch date; whether any approval exists; who approved it; which country/region or schools the approval covers. Other suitable splits accepted.
  2. Who wrote it? What expertise or organisation is responsible? What evidence supports the claims? When was it published or updated? Can independent sources confirm the organisation or key facts?
  3. Open the notice and check its date; find the current official school page or contact the school; compare exact hours and days. Mark the AI claim outdated if it relies on the old notice.
  4. Lateral reading means leaving an unfamiliar website and checking independent sources about who runs it and what others say. For example, search for the organisation's name on trusted independent sites rather than relying only on its own About page.
  5. Example: “I could not find reliable evidence to confirm this claim, so it remains unverified.” Do not call it false without contradictory evidence.

Lesson recap

  • Break an AI response into separate factual claims and check important details one by one.
  • Open each citation and confirm it supports the exact statement.
  • Check the author or organisation, purpose, evidence, date, context and scope.
  • Use lateral reading to investigate unfamiliar websites beyond their own descriptions.
  • Use suitable evidence and independent cross-checks; do not assume repeated copies are independent sources.
  • Report supported, contradicted, partly supported or unverified claims honestly.
  • Follow your teacher's citation rules and disclose AI assistance when required.
