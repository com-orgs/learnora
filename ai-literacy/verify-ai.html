How to Check AI Answers and Sources

AI tools can explain ideas, but they can also invent details, mix up sources or omit context. Treat a generated response as a starting point and verify important claims using reliable evidence.

Learning time: 35–40 minutes · Beginner level · Free study resource

What you will learn

  • Break a long AI answer into separate claims you can check one by one.
  • Use a four-step check: claim, source, date, support.
  • Trace a claim to a primary source and judge whether the source really supports it.
  • Spot invented citations and other signs of an unreliable answer.
  • Explain uncertainty honestly and cite sources the right way.
  • Decide when an AI answer is safe to use in school work — and when it is not.

1. Break an answer into claims

An AI answer is not one thing to believe or disbelieve. It is a bundle of claims, and each claim needs its own check. A claim is a statement that can be true or false: a date, a number, a name, a cause, a quotation. Opinions, advice and examples are different — they are not claims, so you judge them differently.

Start by underlining the factual statements. In the sentence "The Eiffel Tower, completed in 1889, is 330 metres tall and is the most visited paid monument in the world", there are three claims: the year (1889), the height (330 metres), and the "most visited" claim. Each one needs its own evidence. The year and the height are easy to check against a reliable source; the "most visited" claim needs a source that actually counts visitors.

Pay special attention to numbers, dates, names and the word "all" or "always". A claim that says "all penguins live at the South Pole" is stronger — and easier to prove wrong — than "most penguins live in the Southern Hemisphere". Strong words need strong evidence.

Worked example 1 — split the claims. An AI answer says: "Apollo 11 landed on the Moon on 20 July 1969, and Neil Armstrong was the first person to walk on the Moon." Step 1: list the claims — (a) the date, 20 July 1969; (b) Armstrong was first. Step 2: check (a) against a reliable source such as NASA's history pages — confirmed. Step 3: check (b) — confirmed by the same source. Step 4: verdict — both claims check out, so this part of the answer can be used, with NASA cited as the source. Check: notice that neither claim was accepted just because it sounded confident.
Worked example 2 — one wrong claim does not ruin everything. An AI answer about the water cycle says: "Water evaporates from the sea, condenses into clouds, and falls as rain. The Amazon rainforest produces 20% of the world's oxygen." The first two claims are correct textbook science. The third — "20% of the world's oxygen" — is a widely repeated figure that scientists have questioned; most of the oxygen produced by the rainforest is used up there by living things. Step 1: split the claims. Step 2: verify each one separately. Step 3: keep the correct parts, and mark the oxygen claim as doubtful until a reliable source confirms it. Check: a single wrong claim does not make the whole answer useless — but it does mean you cannot copy the whole answer without checking.

2. The four-step check

Use the same four steps for every important claim. Step 1 — Claim: write the claim down in your own words, exactly as stated. Step 2 — Source: find a reliable source that talks about the same thing. Step 3 — Date: check when the source was written or updated, and whether the fact could have changed since. Step 4 — Support: read the source and ask, "Does this actually say what the claim says?" Only when all four steps pass should you use the claim.

Step 4 is where most mistakes hide. A source can be real, recent and from a trusted publisher — and still not support the claim. For example, a page about the Moon's distance from Earth does not support a claim about the Moon's age. The words may look similar, but the evidence is about something else. Always compare the claim and the source side by side.

Worked example 3 — the four steps in action. Claim: "Penguins live at the North Pole." Step 1: the claim is clear — penguins at the North Pole. Step 2: a reliable source, such as a natural history museum page, says penguins live in the Southern Hemisphere. Step 3: the source is current and the fact does not change over time. Step 4: the source directly contradicts the claim. Verdict: false. The claim probably came from mixing up penguins with polar bears, which do live in the Arctic. Check: the check took four steps, and the answer is now certain, not a guess.
Worked example 4 — a claim that partly passes. Claim: "Shakespeare wrote 37 plays." Step 1: the claim is a number — 37. Step 2: reliable sources say Shakespeare is generally credited with around 37 plays, with some plays disputed or written with others. Step 3: the number has not changed recently. Step 4: the source supports "around 37" but not exactly 37 with certainty. Verdict: partly right — write "about 37 plays" and say that a few are disputed. Check: when a source says "around" and the claim says an exact number, the honest version keeps the "around".

3. Trace the claim to its source

Good sources are close to the original facts. For a law, the best source is the official legal text, not a blog post about it. For a scientific study, the best source is the study itself, or a summary by a recognised scientific organisation. For a historical date, a museum or an official archive is better than a random website. These are primary sources: they come from the people or organisations closest to the fact.

When an AI answer gives you a source, open it. Read the page yourself and check three things: who published it, when it was published or updated, and whether the exact claim appears on the page. A search snippet is not enough — snippets are short and can be taken out of context. If you cannot open the source, say so, and treat the claim as unverified.

Watch for a common trick: the answer cites a real source that is about a different topic. A page about "the tallest mountain on Earth" does not support a claim about "the tallest mountain in the Solar System". The citation looks impressive, but it proves nothing. Checking takes a minute; copying an impressive-looking but irrelevant citation takes a second and can cost you marks.

Worked example 5 — opening the source. An AI answer claims: "The Great Wall of China can be seen from space with the naked eye." It cites a space agency page. Step 1: open the cited page. Step 2: the page actually says the Wall is not visible to the naked eye from orbit — it is too narrow and blends in with the landscape. Step 3: the claim is false, and the cited source proves it false. Verdict: do not use the claim; use the source's real statement instead. Check: the citation was real, but the claim was the opposite of what the source said.
Worked example 6 — primary beats secondary. Two sources give the boiling point of water: a cooking blog says 100°C, and a science textbook says 100°C at sea level, lower at high altitude. Both agree, but the textbook is the stronger source because it states the condition (sea level) and is written by experts. For school work, cite the textbook. The blog is fine for a quick check, not as your evidence.

4. Dates and changing facts

Some facts never change: the Moon landing happened in 1969 and will always have happened in 1969. Other facts change often: populations, prices, leaders, records, and anything about current technology. For changing facts, the date of the source matters as much as the source itself.

Ask two questions. First, "When was this source written or updated?" If the source is from 2021 and the claim is about the world's tallest building in 2026, the source may be out of date. Second, "Could this fact have changed since then?" If the answer is yes, find a newer source. AI systems are trained on data from the past, so they are especially likely to give out-of-date answers about current events.

Worked example 7 — an out-of-date claim. An AI answer says: "The largest social media platform has 2 billion users." The cited source is from 2021. Step 1: note the date — 2021. Step 2: ask whether user numbers change — yes, constantly. Step 3: find a newer source before using the number. Verdict: the claim may have been true in 2021, but it cannot be used for today without a current source. Check: the claim is not "wrong", it is "out of date" — a different problem, with the same fix.

5. Spotting invented citations

AI systems can invent citations that look real: a plausible author, a real-sounding journal, a year, even a page number — for a paper that does not exist. This happens because the system predicts what a citation usually looks like, not because it looked one up. Never trust a citation you have not opened.

To check a citation, search for its exact title in quotation marks. A real paper will appear in a library catalogue, a publisher's site, or a search engine for academic work. If nothing appears, or if the title appears only in AI-generated pages, treat the citation as invented. Also check the details: does the author exist? Does the journal exist? Does the year match the topic? A paper about smartphones "published in 1985" is a red flag.

The same caution applies to quotations. If an AI answer quotes a famous person, search for the exact words. Many famous quotations are misattributed — said by someone else, or never said at all. A quotation is only usable when you can find it in a reliable source.

Worked example 8 — the citation that does not exist. An AI answer about homework cites "Smith, J. (2022). The effects of homework on learning. Journal of Educational Research, 45(3), 112–130." Step 1: search the exact title. Step 2: no such paper appears anywhere. Step 3: search the journal and volume — the volume numbers do not match. Verdict: the citation is invented. The claim it was meant to support is now unverified and must be checked another way, or dropped. Check: a perfect-looking citation is not evidence. Only an opened, real source is evidence.

6. Writing it up honestly

Once you have checked the claims, write your answer in your own words. Use the sources you actually opened, and cite them the way your teacher asks. If two reliable sources disagree, say so: "Source A says X, while Source B says Y." That is honest, and it is also good thinking — disagreement between sources is information, not a problem to hide.

If a claim cannot be confirmed, write that plainly: "I could not verify this claim." Do not invent a citation to cover the gap, and do not write the claim as if it were certain. In school work, an honest "unverified" is worth more than a confident-sounding guess.

Finally, if your school allows AI help, say so when you hand work in — for example, "I used an AI tool to find starting points, then checked each claim against the sources listed." That disclosure is part of doing the work honestly. If your school does not allow AI help for a task, do not use it for that task at all.

Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1: Checking the whole answer at once. Wrong: "It sounds right overall, so I will use it." Correct: split it into claims and check each one — one wrong claim does not ruin the rest, and one right claim does not save the rest.
Mistake 2: Trusting a citation without opening it. Wrong: "It has a citation, so it must be true." Correct: open the source and check that it actually says what the claim says.
Mistake 3: Using an old source for a changing fact. Wrong: "The source is from a trusted site." Correct: also check the date — a trusted 2021 source cannot support a 2026 claim about something that changes.
Mistake 4: Accepting a source that mentions a similar word. Wrong: "The source mentions the Moon, so it supports my Moon claim." Correct: the source must support the exact claim, not a nearby topic.
Mistake 5: Inventing a citation to fill a gap. Wrong: writing a plausible-looking reference you never opened. Correct: write "unverified" and either find a real source or drop the claim.
Mistake 6: Hiding disagreement between sources. Wrong: picking the source you like and pretending the other does not exist. Correct: report both, and explain which is stronger and why.

Practice exercises

Try each question before opening the answers.

1. An AI answer says: "The Amazon rainforest covers 5.5 million square kilometres and is home to 10% of the world's species." Split this into two claims.

2. A claim about the population of a city cites a source from 2019. What two questions should you ask before using it?

3. An AI answer cites a real paper, but the paper is about cats and the claim is about dogs. What is wrong, and what should you do?

4. You cannot find a cited paper anywhere. What should you write in your homework instead of the citation?

5. Two reliable sources disagree about a date. Write one honest sentence that reports both.

6. An AI answer quotes a famous scientist. How do you check the quotation?

7. A claim says "all birds can fly". Why is the word "all" a warning sign, and what is the counter-example?

8. A source says the boiling point of water is 100°C. Another says it is 95°C on a mountain. Do they disagree? Explain.

9. An AI answer gives a 2024 statistic about phone use, citing a 2024 report. What else should you check before using it in 2026?

10. Write the four steps of the check as a checklist you could keep next to your computer.

11. A classmate says "I checked it — the AI gave me a citation." What is the one question you should ask them?

12. An AI answer about the Moon says it is 384,400 km from Earth. Your textbook says the same. Is the claim verified? Explain why, using the four steps.

Lesson summary

  • Split an AI answer into separate claims and check each one on its own.
  • Use the four-step check: claim, source, date, support.
  • Open the source yourself. A citation you never opened proves nothing.
  • The source must support the exact claim, not just a nearby topic.
  • Check the date for facts that can change; use current sources.
  • If a claim cannot be confirmed, say so honestly — never invent a citation.
