How Artificial Intelligence Works

Artificial intelligence is a broad field in which computer systems perform tasks such as recognising patterns, processing language or making predictions. Many modern systems learn statistical patterns from examples and use them to produce outputs.

Learning time: 35–40 minutes · Beginner level · Free study resource

What you will learn

  • Describe what artificial intelligence is — and what it is not.
  • Explain how data, training, a model and an output fit together.
  • Distinguish rule-based programs from machine-learning systems.
  • Explain supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning in plain words.
  • Explain why AI outputs need human review before you trust them.
  • Give everyday examples of AI and say what each one can and cannot do.

1. What AI is — and what it is not

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a name for computer systems that do tasks we usually connect with thinking: recognising a face in a photo, translating a sentence, suggesting the next word, or predicting tomorrow's weather. The key word is systems. There is no single "AI brain". There are many different techniques, built by people, for different jobs.

Two ideas are often mixed up. A rule-based program follows rules a person wrote down: "If the temperature is below 18 degrees, switch the heating on." It never does anything its rules do not cover. A machine-learning system is different: instead of being given every rule, it is shown many examples and finds patterns in them. A spam filter is a good example. Nobody writes a rule for every spam message. The system is shown thousands of emails labelled "spam" or "not spam", and it learns which words and patterns usually appear in spam.

Most AI you meet is narrow AI: it is good at one kind of task and useless at others. A system that translates languages cannot drive a car, and a chess program cannot write a poem. When people in films talk about a machine that thinks like a person in every way, they are talking about something that does not exist today.

Worked example 1 — rule-based or learning? A lift button: you press 3, the lift goes to floor 3. That is rule-based — a fixed rule links the button to the action. A photo app that groups your pictures by the people in them is machine learning — it was trained on labelled faces and finds the pattern in new photos. Ask: "Was it given the rule, or did it learn a pattern from examples?"
Worked example 2 — narrow AI. A weather app predicts rain with 70% confidence. It is narrow AI: it is good at weather patterns and nothing else. It cannot explain why it rained, plan your day, or know whether you own an umbrella. One job, done with patterns.

2. Data: the raw material

Every machine-learning system starts with data — examples. For a system that sorts photos of cats and dogs, the data is thousands of photos, each labelled "cat" or "dog". For a system that recommends films, the data is what people watched and how they rated it. The data is the teacher: the system can only learn patterns that are actually in the examples it is shown.

Data comes in two main kinds. Labelled data has the answer attached: a photo labelled "dog", an email labelled "spam". Unlabelled data has no answers: a pile of customer reviews, a library of songs. Labelled data is used to teach a system to make a particular judgement; unlabelled data is used to find groups and structure, such as grouping shoppers with similar tastes.

Three things about data decide how good the result can be. Quantity: a system trained on 50 photos will be weaker than one trained on 50,000. Quality: wrong labels teach wrong patterns — if 100 dog photos are wrongly labelled "cat", the system will make cat mistakes. Coverage: if the data only shows one kind of example, the system will struggle with the kinds it has never seen. A face-recognition system trained only on adult faces will do badly on children's faces.

Worked example 3 — labels matter. A school trains a model to sort drawings into "animal" and "vehicle". The training set has 200 animal drawings and only 20 vehicle drawings. On new drawings, the model calls almost everything an animal — even a bus. Step 1: count the labels (200 vs 20). Step 2: the model saw ten times more animals, so "animal" is its safe guess. Step 3: fix it by adding more vehicle drawings, not by blaming the model. Check: after balancing the data to 200 and 200, the model starts getting buses right.
Worked example 4 — coverage. A voice assistant is trained on recordings of adult voices. A 12-year-old asks it a question and it misunderstands three times in a row. The problem is coverage: children's voices were missing from the training data. The fix is to include them. This is a data problem, not a "the machine is stupid" problem.

3. Training: how a model learns a pattern

Training is the process of showing a model examples and letting it adjust itself until its answers get better. A model is the trained part of the system: a set of numbers (called parameters) that turn an input into an output. Think of it like tuning a radio: at first the sound is fuzzy, and each small adjustment makes it clearer. Training adjusts the parameters, little by little, so the model's answers move closer to the right ones.

Training happens in a loop. Step 1: the model makes a guess on a training example. Step 2: the guess is compared with the correct label, and the size of the error is measured. Step 3: the parameters are adjusted a little to reduce the error. Step 4: repeat, thousands or millions of times. When training ends, the parameters are frozen — the model stops learning and is ready to be used.

One rule makes training honest: the model must be tested on examples it has never seen. If you test a model on the same photos it trained on, a good score proves nothing — it may have memorised them. So data is usually split, for example 80% for training and 20% for testing. A model that scores well on the unseen 20% has really learned a pattern; a model that only scores well on the training data has probably just memorised it.

Worked example 5 — the 80/20 split. A class trains a model on 1,000 labelled photos of cats and dogs. They keep 200 photos aside and train on 800. After training, the model gets 196 of the 200 unseen photos right (98%). Step 1: the test photos were never shown during training. Step 2: 196 out of 200 correct is a real result, not memory. Step 3: the 4 mistakes are worth looking at — two are blurry photos, which tells the class the model struggles with blurry images. Check: if they had tested on the 800 training photos instead, a high score would have told them nothing.
Worked example 6 — a prediction is not a fact. A weather model says there is a 70% chance of rain tomorrow. What does 70% mean? It means that in past days with similar conditions, it rained about 7 times out of 10. It does not mean "it will definitely rain" and it does not mean "it will rain for 70% of the day". A prediction is a statement about a pattern in past data, not a fact about tomorrow. Check: look at what the number is counting — similar past days — before you decide whether to take an umbrella.

4. Three ways machines learn

Supervised learning uses labelled data: each example comes with the right answer, and the model learns to predict that answer for new examples. Spam filters, photo classifiers and exam-marking helpers are supervised. The supervisor is the label.

Unsupervised learning uses unlabelled data: the system finds groups and structure on its own. A shop might use it to group customers who buy similar things, or a music app might group songs that sound alike. Nobody tells the system the groups in advance — it finds them.

Reinforcement learning learns from rewards: the system tries actions, gets a score for each one, and slowly learns which actions earn the best score. It is how some game-playing programs learned to play board games and video games — by playing millions of games against themselves and keeping the moves that won.

Generative AI — the kind that writes text or draws pictures — is usually trained on huge amounts of text or images, learning the patterns of how words and pixels usually go together. When you ask it a question, it does not look up the answer in a book. It produces the most likely continuation, word by word, based on the patterns it learned. That is why its answers can sound confident and still be wrong.

Worked example 7 — which kind of learning? A library app groups its books into "mystery", "sport" and "science" without anyone labelling them first. Step 1: are the books labelled? No. Step 2: is the system finding groups on its own? Yes. Step 3: that is unsupervised learning. Compare: a reading app that predicts whether you will like a book, trained on books you rated 1 to 5 stars — that is supervised learning, because the ratings are labels.
Worked example 8 — predicting the next word. A chatbot is asked "The capital of France is ___". It answers "Paris". Did it "know" that? Step 1: during training it saw the words "capital of France is" followed by "Paris" thousands of times. Step 2: it predicts the most likely next word from that pattern. Step 3: if the training data had contained a mistake repeated often, it would predict the mistake just as confidently. Check: a correct prediction is evidence of a strong pattern in the data, not of understanding.

5. From input to output

Every AI system follows the same shape: input → model → output → human review. The input is what you give it — a photo, a sentence, a set of numbers. The model is the trained pattern-finder. The output is what it produces — a label, a prediction, a paragraph. The last step matters most for school: a person checks the output before using it.

Consider a translation app. Input: a sentence in Urdu. Model: patterns learned from millions of translated sentence pairs. Output: an English sentence. Human review: you read the English sentence and check it makes sense — especially for names, numbers and jokes, which translation models often get wrong.

Outputs can be wrong in different ways. They can be incorrect (the wrong answer), incomplete (missing the important part), out of date (trained on old information), or made up (a confident answer with no real source behind it — sometimes called a hallucination). The model's confidence does not tell you which one you have: a made-up answer can sound just as smooth as a correct one.

6. AI in everyday life

AI is already part of ordinary life, usually invisibly. Search engines rank results with it. Maps predict traffic. Streaming services recommend what to watch next. Phone cameras recognise faces and improve photos in low light. Spellcheckers suggest corrections. In school, AI tools can explain a topic in simpler words, make practice questions, or give feedback on a draft.

Each use has a limit worth knowing. A recommendation system shows you more of what you already like, so it can narrow what you discover. A translation tool can miss tone and jokes. A homework helper can give a wrong answer with total confidence. Knowing the limit is part of using the tool well: you use it for what it is good at, and you check it where it is weak.

People remain in charge. People choose what problem the system should solve, collect and label the data, decide when it is good enough to use, and take responsibility for what happens when it is wrong. When an AI system makes a decision that affects someone — a grade, a job application, a medical suggestion — a person should make the final decision, not the machine.

Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1: "The AI understands what I asked." Wrong: the system matches patterns in words. It does not understand meaning the way you do. Correct: say "the system predicted the most likely answer from patterns in its training data".
Mistake 2: "It sounds fluent, so it must be right." Wrong: fluent output is easy for these systems; correctness is a separate question. Correct: check every important claim against a reliable source.
Mistake 3: "AI is one thing." Wrong: a spam filter, a translator and a game-playing program are different systems with different data and different limits. Correct: ask "which system, trained on what data, for which job?"
Mistake 4: "More data always makes it better." Wrong: more bad data teaches bad patterns faster. Correct: data needs to be accurate, balanced and cover the cases the system will meet.
Mistake 5: "The AI learns on its own." Wrong: people choose the data, the task and the training, and decide when to stop. Correct: the system adjusts its parameters; people do the deciding.
Mistake 6: "A 98% test score means it is always right." Wrong: the score is on a particular test set. New kinds of input can still fail. Correct: ask "tested on what, and what did it get wrong?"

Practice answers

  1. Machine learning (probably): it finds patterns in what food combinations make good recipes, rather than following a fixed list of rules. If it only followed fixed recipes, it would be rule-based.
  2. It will call most cats "dogs" because it saw 50 times more dogs. Fix: add many more labelled cat photos so the two groups are balanced.
  3. A model is the trained part of a system — a set of numbers (parameters) that turn an input into an output, learned from examples.
  4. Testing on training data only shows memory. Unseen examples show whether it really learned a pattern.
  5. 40% means that in past days with similar conditions it rained about 4 times out of 10. It does not mean it will rain for 40% of the day, and it does not mean it will definitely rain.
  6. Supervised: a model that predicts house prices from labelled examples of houses and prices. Unsupervised: grouping songs by sound without labels. Accept other correct examples.
  7. The model produced the most likely words from patterns; it did not look the year up. The output step is where the error shows, but the cause is in training data and pattern-matching.
  8. It needs photos or descriptions of books, labelled with their section (for example "mystery", "sport", "science"). The labels are the sections.
  9. Reinforcement learning. The reward is winning (or the score) — moves that lead to wins are kept and repeated.
  10. The model learned the pattern "raining cats and dogs" as words, not as an idiom meaning heavy rain. It matched word patterns, not meaning.
  11. Filter B. Filter A was tested on emails it had already seen, so its score may be memory. Filter B's score is on unseen emails, so it shows real learning.

Lesson summary

  • AI is a set of techniques, not one machine mind. Most AI is narrow: good at one job.
  • Machine-learning systems learn patterns from data; rule-based programs follow rules people wrote.
  • A model is the trained part of a system. Training adjusts its parameters using labelled examples.
  • Test a model on examples it has never seen — otherwise a good score may only be memory.
  • A prediction is a statement about past patterns, not a fact about the future.
  • Every AI system follows input → model → output → human review. The person checks the output.
