Comparing and Ordering Fractions

Learn how to decide which fraction is greater, which is smaller, and how to arrange several fractions in order. Start with visual models, then use common denominators and helpful benchmarks.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Beginner level

What You Will Learn

  • Compare fractions that have the same denominator or numerator.
  • Compare fractions with different denominators.
  • Use equivalent fractions and common denominators.
  • Use 0, 1/2 and 1 as helpful benchmark values.
  • Arrange a group of fractions from least to greatest or greatest to least.

1. What Does It Mean to Compare Fractions?

Comparing fractions means deciding which fraction represents the greater amount. The symbols help us show the result:

  • > means greater than.
  • < means less than.
  • = means equal to.

The bars show the same-sized whole divided into equal parts.

Important: You can only compare the shaded parts fairly when the wholes are the same size.

2. Fractions with the Same Denominator

When two fractions have the same denominator, their parts are the same size. Compare the numerators: the fraction with the larger numerator is greater.

Example: Compare 3/8 and 5/8. Both fractions are divided into eighths. Since 5 is greater than 3, 5/8 > 3/8.

Try another: 2/7 and 6/7. The denominator is the same, and 6 is greater than 2, so 2/7 < 6/7.

3. Fractions with the Same Numerator

When two positive fractions have the same numerator, the fraction with the smaller denominator is greater. A whole divided into fewer equal pieces gives larger pieces.

Example: Compare 3/4 and 3/8. Three quarters uses larger pieces than three eighths, so 3/4 > 3/8.
Remember: This rule works for positive fractions with the same numerator. Do not assume a larger denominator always makes a fraction larger.

4. Compare Different Denominators Using Equivalent Fractions

For fractions with different denominators, rewrite them as equivalent fractions with a common denominator. Then compare the numerators.

Worked example: Compare 2/3 and 3/4

  1. Find a common denominator for 3 and 4. The lowest common denominator is 12.
  2. Convert 2/3 into twelfths: multiply the top and bottom by 4, giving 8/12.
  3. Convert 3/4 into twelfths: multiply the top and bottom by 3, giving 9/12.
  4. Compare 8/12 and 9/12. Since 8 is less than 9, 2/3 < 3/4.

Both fractions are now expressed in twelfths, so their numerators can be compared directly.

Another example: Compare 5/6 and 7/9

  1. A common denominator for 6 and 9 is 18.
  2. 5/6 = 15/18 and 7/9 = 14/18.
  3. 15/18 is greater than 14/18, so 5/6 > 7/9.

5. Use Benchmarks to Compare Quickly

A benchmark is a familiar value that helps you estimate where a fraction belongs. Useful benchmarks include 0, 1/2 and 1.

  • A fraction with numerator 1 is less than 1, provided its denominator is greater than 1.
  • If the numerator is exactly half the denominator, the fraction equals 1/2.
  • If the numerator is more than half the denominator, the fraction is greater than 1/2.
Example: Compare 3/8 and 5/9. The fraction 3/8 is less than 1/2 because 3 is less than half of 8 (4). The fraction 5/9 is greater than 1/2 because 5 is greater than half of 9 (4.5). Therefore, 3/8 < 5/9.

Benchmarks are useful for quick comparisons. If the result is not clear, use a common denominator to check it exactly.

6. Put Fractions in Order

To order fractions, compare them carefully and place them from smallest to largest or largest to smallest.

Worked example: Order 1/2, 2/3 and 3/4 from least to greatest

  1. Choose a common denominator: 12 works for 2, 3 and 4.
  2. Convert: 1/2 = 6/12, 2/3 = 8/12 and 3/4 = 9/12.
  3. Order the numerators: 6, 8, 9.
  4. Write the original fractions in that order: 1/2, 2/3, 3/4.
Tip: When ordering several fractions, find one common denominator for all of them. Keep each converted fraction beside its original fraction so you do not mix them up.

7. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Comparing denominators only: A bigger denominator does not automatically mean a bigger fraction. Compare equivalent fractions or use a suitable rule.
Changing only the denominator: To make an equivalent fraction, multiply or divide the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.
Comparing different-sized wholes: Fraction comparisons assume the wholes are the same size.
Reversing the order: When a question asks for least to greatest, start with the smallest value.

8. Practice Exercises

Try these questions before revealing the answers.

  1. Use >, < or =: 4/9 ___ 7/9.
  2. Use >, < or =: 3/5 ___ 3/8.
  3. Compare 1/2 and 4/7 using a common denominator.
  4. Compare 5/6 and 7/9 using a common denominator.
  5. Arrange 1/3, 3/4 and 1/2 from least to greatest.
  6. Arrange 5/8, 2/3 and 3/4 from greatest to least.
Show practice answers and explanations
  1. 4/9 < 7/9 because the denominators match and 4 is less than 7.
  2. 3/5 > 3/8 because the numerators match and fifths are larger than eighths.
  3. 1/2 = 7/14 and 4/7 = 8/14, so 1/2 < 4/7.
  4. 5/6 = 15/18 and 7/9 = 14/18, so 5/6 > 7/9.
  5. 1/3, 1/2, 3/4.
  6. 3/4, 2/3, 5/8.

11. Lesson Summary

  • With the same denominator, compare the numerators.
  • With the same numerator, the fraction with the smaller denominator is greater.
  • For different denominators, use equivalent fractions with a common denominator.
  • Benchmarks such as 0, 1/2 and 1 can help you estimate and compare.
  • To order fractions, convert them to a common denominator and compare their numerators.
