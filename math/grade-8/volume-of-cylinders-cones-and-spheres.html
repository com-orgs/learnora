Volume of Cylinders, Cones and Spheres

Volume measures the space inside a three-dimensional object. Cylinders, cones and spheres all use formulas involving π and the radius — learn which formula belongs to which solid, how to use it without mixing up radius and diameter, and how to check your answer.

Learning time: 25–30 minutes · Level: Developing

Learning goals

  • Identify radius, diameter and perpendicular height in cylinders, cones and spheres.
  • Choose and apply the correct volume formula for each solid.
  • Use consistent units, convert diameter to radius and express answers in cubic units.
  • Compare how changes in dimensions affect volume and round only at the final step.
  • Work backwards: find a missing height or radius from a given volume.

1. Radius, diameter and height

The radius r is the distance from the centre of a circular base to its edge. The diameter d is the distance right across the base through the centre, so d = 2r and r = d ÷ 2. The height h is the perpendicular distance between the two bases of a cylinder, or from the apex to the base for a cone. "Perpendicular" matters: for a cone or cylinder that leans, the height is measured at right angles to the base, not along the sloping side.

Worked example: A cylinder has diameter 10 cm. Its radius is 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm. If the question gives a diameter of 14 cm, the radius is 7 cm. Always convert diameter to radius before touching a formula, because every volume formula in this lesson uses r, never d.

Real life: a drinks can is a cylinder, an ice-cream cone is a cone, and a football is a sphere. When a factory fills a can, the amount of drink it holds is the volume of the cylinder; when a baker fills a cone, the amount of ice cream is the volume of the cone.

2. Cylinder: V = πr²h

A cylinder has two equal circular bases. Its volume is base area × height: V = πr²h. The base is a circle of area πr², and stacking that area up through the height h gives the volume. Keep the radius squared and multiply by the perpendicular height.

Worked example: A cylinder has r = 3 cm and h = 5 cm. V = π × 3² × 5 = 45π ≈ 141.4 cm³. Check: the base area is π × 9 ≈ 28.3 cm², and 28.3 × 5 ≈ 141.4 cm³. Real life: a soup tin with r = 4 cm and h = 10 cm holds V = π × 16 × 10 = 160π ≈ 502.7 cm³, which is about 0.5 litres.

3. Cone: V = ⅓πr²h

A cone has one circular base and a vertex (apex). Its volume is one-third of the matching cylinder — the cylinder with the same base radius and the same height: V = ⅓πr²h. The "one-third" is the whole point of the cone formula, and it is also the fact most often forgotten in tests.

Worked example: A cone has r = 3 cm and h = 5 cm. V = ⅓ × π × 3² × 5 = 15π ≈ 47.1 cm³. Check: the matching cylinder would be 45π ≈ 141.4 cm³, and 141.4 ÷ 3 ≈ 47.1 cm³. Real life: an ice-cream cone with r = 4 cm and h = 9 cm holds V = ⅓ × π × 16 × 9 = 48π ≈ 150.8 cm³ of ice cream.

4. Sphere: V = ⁴⁄₃πr³

A sphere is perfectly round in three dimensions: every point on its surface is the same distance r from the centre. Its volume is V = ⁴⁄₃πr³. Remember that the radius is cubed here — r × r × r — not squared. That single difference is what makes sphere volumes grow so fast when the radius grows.

Worked example: A sphere has r = 3 cm. V = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 3³ = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 27 = 36π ≈ 113.1 cm³. Check: 36 × 3.1416 ≈ 113.1. Real life: a football with r ≈ 11 cm has volume ⁴⁄₃ × π × 11³ = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 1,331 ≈ 5,575 cm³, about 5.6 litres of air.

5. Units and rounding

If measurements are in centimetres, volume is in cubic centimetres (cm³). If they are in metres, volume is in cubic metres (m³). Keep π in the calculation until the final step, then round as requested — rounding early makes the final answer drift. Use the same units for all lengths before you start: 1 m = 100 cm, and 1 m³ = 1,000,000 cm³, so mixing metres and centimetres is the fastest way to be wrong by a factor of a million.

Worked example: A water tank is a cylinder with r = 2 m and water depth 3 m. V = π × 2² × 3 = 12π ≈ 37.7 m³. Since 1 m³ = 1,000 litres, that is about 37,699 litres. Check: 12 × 3.1416 ≈ 37.7, and 37.7 × 1,000 ≈ 37,700 litres.

6. How volume changes when dimensions change

Volume does not grow in step with length — it grows faster. If the height of a cylinder doubles and the radius stays the same, the volume doubles, because V = πr²h is directly proportional to h. If the radius doubles, the volume is multiplied by 2² = 4, because the radius is squared. If the radius of a sphere doubles, the volume is multiplied by 2³ = 8, because the radius is cubed. Real life: a cake tin twice as wide holds four times as much mixture, not twice as much.

Worked example: A sphere has r = 5 cm, so V = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 125 = 500π/3 ≈ 523.6 cm³. Doubling the radius to 10 cm gives V = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 1,000 = 4,000π/3 ≈ 4,188.8 cm³. Check: 4,188.8 ÷ 523.6 = 8, exactly as the r³ rule predicts.

7. Working backwards: find a missing length

Sometimes the volume is given and a length is missing. Rearrange the formula before substituting numbers, so the algebra stays clean.

Worked example: A cylinder has volume 200π cm³ and radius 5 cm. Find its height. V = πr²h, so 200π = π × 25 × h. Divide both sides by 25π: h = 200 ÷ 25 = 8 cm. Check: π × 25 × 8 = 200π cm³.

Worked example: A sphere has volume 36π cm³. Find its radius. V = ⁴⁄₃πr³, so 36π = ⁴⁄₃πr³. Divide by π: 36 = ⁴⁄₃r³. Multiply by ¾: r³ = 27. Take the cube root: r = 3 cm. Check: ⁴⁄₃ × π × 27 = 36π cm³.

8. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mistake 1 — Using the diameter as the radius. Wrong: a cylinder with diameter 10 cm used as r = 10. Correct: r = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm, then V = π × 25 × h.
  • Mistake 2 — Forgetting the ⅓ for a cone. Wrong: cone volume = πr²h. Correct: V = ⅓πr²h. A cone holds one-third of its matching cylinder.
  • Mistake 3 — Squaring the radius for a sphere. Wrong: V = ⁴⁄₃πr². Correct: V = ⁴⁄₃πr³. The sphere formula cubes the radius.
  • Mistake 4 — Using the slant height of a cone. Wrong: using the sloping side length as h. Correct: h is the perpendicular height from apex to base. If you are given the slant length, find the perpendicular height first (often with Pythagoras).
  • Mistake 5 — Rounding π too early. Wrong: using π ≈ 3 at the start and rounding at every step. Correct: keep π in the answer (for example 45π) and round only the final decimal.
  • Mistake 6 — Writing square units for a volume. Wrong: "141.4 cm²". Correct: "141.4 cm³". Volume is three-dimensional, so the unit is cubic.

Compare solid volumes and use units

Volume measures the space inside a three-dimensional solid and is written in cubic units. A cylinder has volume V = πr²h. A cone with the same base radius and height has one-third of the cylinder's volume: V = ⅓πr²h. A sphere has volume V = ⁴⁄₃πr³. In each formula, r is the radius, not the diameter.

Worked reasoning: A cone has radius 3 cm and height 4 cm. Its volume is ⅓ × π × 3² × 4 = 12π cm³, approximately 37.7 cm³. If the question gives a diameter of 6 cm, first divide by 2 to get the radius 3 cm.

Keep π in the exact answer when appropriate, then round the decimal only at the end. The final unit must be cubic, such as cm³ or m³, because three dimensions are multiplied.

  • Do not use diameter in place of radius.
  • Square the radius for cylinder and cone formulas; cube it for a sphere.
  • Convert all measurements to the same unit before calculating.

Worked examples

  1. Cylinder r = 3 cm, h = 5 cm: V = π × 3² × 5 = 45π ≈ 141.4 cm³. Check: base area π × 9 ≈ 28.3 cm², times height 5 ≈ 141.4 cm³.
  2. Cone r = 3 cm, h = 5 cm: V = ⅓ × π × 9 × 5 = 15π ≈ 47.1 cm³. Check: one-third of the matching cylinder (45π) is 15π.
  3. Sphere r = 3 cm: V = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 27 = 36π ≈ 113.1 cm³. Check: 36 × 3.1416 ≈ 113.1.
  4. Cylinder from a diameter: d = 10 cm, h = 4 cm. First r = 5 cm. V = π × 25 × 4 = 100π ≈ 314.2 cm³.
  5. Water tank: Cylinder r = 2 m, depth 3 m. V = π × 4 × 3 = 12π ≈ 37.7 m³, about 37,699 litres.
  6. Ice-cream cone: r = 4 cm, h = 9 cm. V = ⅓ × π × 16 × 9 = 48π ≈ 150.8 cm³.
  7. Sphere r = 5 cm: V = ⁴⁄₃ × π × 125 = 500π/3 ≈ 523.6 cm³. Check: 500 ÷ 3 ≈ 166.7, times π ≈ 523.6.
  8. Find the height: Cylinder volume 200π cm³, r = 5 cm. h = 200π ÷ (π × 25) = 8 cm. Check: π × 25 × 8 = 200π cm³.

Independent practice

Attempt each problem before reviewing the worked answer.

  1. Find cylinder volume for r = 2 cm, h = 7 cm. Answer: 28π ≈ 88.0 cm³.
  2. Find cone volume for r = 3 cm, h = 8 cm. Answer: 24π ≈ 75.4 cm³.
  3. Find sphere volume for r = 2 cm. Answer: 32π/3 ≈ 33.5 cm³.
  4. A cylinder has diameter 10 cm and height 4 cm. Answer: r = 5; V = 100π ≈ 314.2 cm³.
  5. What happens to cylinder volume if height doubles? Answer: It doubles.
  6. What happens to sphere volume if radius doubles? Answer: It becomes 8 times as large.
  7. A cone and cylinder have the same base and height. Answer: Cone volume is one-third of cylinder volume.
  8. Why is volume written in cubic units? Answer: It measures three-dimensional space (length × width × height).
  9. Find the volume of a sphere with radius 5 cm. Answer: ⁴⁄₃ × π × 125 = 500π/3 ≈ 523.6 cm³.
  10. Find the volume of a cone with radius 4 cm and height 6 cm. Answer: ⅓ × π × 16 × 6 = 32π ≈ 100.5 cm³.
  11. Find the volume of a cylinder with radius 6 cm and height 2 cm. Answer: π × 36 × 2 = 72π ≈ 226.2 cm³.
  12. Word problem: A cylindrical paint tin has radius 7 cm and height 10 cm. How much paint does it hold, in litres? Answer: V = π × 49 × 10 = 490π ≈ 1,539.4 cm³. Since 1,000 cm³ = 1 litre, that is about 1.54 litres.
