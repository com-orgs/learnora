Scatter Plots and Data Relationships

A scatter plot displays paired numerical data as points on a grid. The pattern of the points can show whether two variables are related, how strong that relationship is, and whether any point does not fit — but it can never prove that one variable causes the other.

Learning time: 25–30 minutes · Level: Developing

Learning goals

  • Plot paired numerical data on correctly labelled axes with units.
  • Describe positive, negative and weak or absent associations.
  • Identify outliers and explain how they may affect a trend.
  • Use a line of best fit for cautious estimates within the observed data range.
  • Explain why correlation does not, by itself, prove causation.

1. Paired data

Each point on a scatter plot represents two measurements from the same item or person, such as hours studied and quiz score, or temperature and ice-cream sales. The two measurements are called a pair, and the pair is plotted as one point (x, y). Put one variable on each axis, choose a sensible scale, and label both axes with units — a plot without labelled axes cannot be read.

Worked example: Five students record hours studied and quiz score: (1, 42), (2, 55), (3, 61), (4, 74), (5, 83). Plot hours on the x-axis and score on the y-axis. The first point sits at x = 1, y = 42. As you move right along the x-axis, the points also move up — the first sign of a positive association, which section 2 explains.

Real life: a shop records the noon temperature and the number of ice creams sold each day. Each day gives one pair (temperature, sales) and one point on the plot. A weather app, a fitness tracker and a school attendance record all produce paired data like this.

2. Correlation: positive, negative or none

Positive correlation means larger x-values tend to occur with larger y-values: the points slope upwards from left to right. Negative correlation means larger x-values tend to occur with smaller y-values: the points slope downwards. No clear pattern means little or no correlation: knowing one value tells you almost nothing about the other. Always use the word "tend": correlation describes a general pattern, not a rule that every single point must follow.

Worked example: As temperature rises, ice-cream sales tend to rise — a positive association. As travel speed rises, journey time for a fixed distance tends to fall — a negative association. Shoe size and test scores show no clear pattern, so there is little or no correlation between them.

3. Strength and outliers

A strong correlation has points close to a clear trend — you could almost draw a straight line through them. A weak correlation has points more spread out, so the trend is harder to see. Strength is about how tightly the points hug the trend, not about how steep the trend is. An outlier is a point far from the general pattern. It should be checked, not automatically removed: it might be a measuring error, or it might be a genuine unusual case that matters.

Worked example: Nine students study 1 to 5 hours and score between 40 and 85, following a clear upward trend. One student studied 1 hour and scored 96. That point sits far above the rest — it is an outlier. Check it first: was the score recorded correctly? Was that student revising for weeks already? Only after checking should you decide whether to keep it in the analysis.

4. Line of best fit

A line of best fit summarises the overall trend. Draw it through the middle of the data, with roughly as many points above the line as below it, and with the line following the direction of the points. It does not have to pass through any actual point, and it must never be drawn to connect the dots one by one.

Worked example: Suppose study time and quiz score generally rise together, and the line of best fit is y = 6x + 40, where x is hours studied and y is the estimated score. For 2 hours of study, the estimate is y = 6 × 2 + 40 = 52. Check: 2 hours is inside the observed data range, so this is an interpolation — an estimate inside the data — and it is reasonable. For 6 hours, y = 6 × 6 + 40 = 76. For 20 hours, the line would give y = 160, which is impossible for a test marked out of 100: that is an extrapolation far outside the data, and it must not be trusted.

5. Correlation is not causation

Two variables can be associated without one causing the other. A third factor may affect both, or the relationship may be coincidental. Avoid claiming cause from a scatter plot alone: the plot shows that two things move together, and it says nothing about why.

Worked example: Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases rise together in summer — a strong positive correlation. Ice cream does not cause sunburn. The third factor is hot, sunny weather: it makes people buy ice cream and it also makes people burn. Whenever you see a strong correlation, ask: "Is there a third factor that could explain both?"

6. Plotting exercise: build a scatter plot

Use this table of eight students. Hours studied (x) and quiz score out of 100 (y): (0, 28), (1, 41), (2, 47), (2, 55), (3, 62), (4, 70), (5, 79), (5, 88). Draw an x-axis labelled "Hours studied" from 0 to 6 and a y-axis labelled "Quiz score" from 0 to 100. Plot each pair as a cross. Then answer: (a) Is the association positive, negative or unclear? (b) Is it strong or weak? (c) Which point, if any, looks like an outlier?

Worked solution: (a) Positive: as hours increase, scores increase — the points rise from bottom-left to top-right. (b) Strong: the points sit close to a rising straight trend; a line through them would be about y = 10x + 30. Check: at x = 3, the line gives y = 60, and the plotted point at (3, 62) is very close. (c) No point is far from the trend, so there is no clear outlier — even the two points at x = 5 (79 and 88) sit near the line.

Now draw the line of best fit through the middle of your points and use it to estimate the score for 4 hours of study. The line y = 10x + 30 gives y = 70 at x = 4, which matches the plotted point (4, 70). That agreement is a good sign your line is in the right place.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mistake 1 — Claiming causation from correlation. Wrong: "More ice cream causes more sunburn." Correct: both rise in hot weather; correlation alone never proves cause.
  • Mistake 2 — Deleting every outlier. Wrong: removing a point just because it looks odd. Correct: check whether it is an error first; a genuine outlier may be the most interesting point in the data.
  • Mistake 3 — Extrapolating far beyond the data. Wrong: using a line fitted to 1–5 hours of study to predict the score for 20 hours. Correct: only estimate inside the observed range; outside it, the pattern may not continue.
  • Mistake 4 — Forgetting axis labels and units. Wrong: a plot with bare numbers on the axes. Correct: label each axis with the variable and its unit, or the plot cannot be read.
  • Mistake 5 — Connecting the dots. Wrong: joining every point with straight segments. Correct: draw one smooth line of best fit through the middle of the pattern.
  • Mistake 6 — Judging strength by steepness. Wrong: "This line is steep, so the correlation is strong." Correct: strength is how closely the points follow the trend; a shallow line can show a strong correlation and a steep line can show a weak one.

Read patterns without overclaiming

A scatter plot displays paired numerical data. A positive association means larger x-values tend to occur with larger y-values; a negative association means larger x-values tend to occur with smaller y-values. A weak or absent association has no clear upward or downward pattern. An outlier is a point far from the overall pattern and may affect a line of best fit.

Worked reasoning: Suppose study time and quiz score generally rise together. A line of best fit can be used to estimate a score for a given study time, but it does not guarantee an individual result. If the line is y = 6x + 40, a study time of 2 hours gives an estimated score of 52.

Correlation describes a relationship, not proof that one variable causes the other. Sleep, prior knowledge or other factors may influence both study time and scores.

  • Describe direction, strength and unusual points.
  • Avoid predictions far outside the data range.
  • Do not claim causation from a scatter plot alone.

Worked examples

  1. Positive association: As temperature rises, ice-cream sales tend to rise. The points slope upwards from left to right.
  2. Negative association: As travel speed rises, journey time for a fixed distance tends to fall. The points slope downwards.
  3. No clear correlation: If points show no upward or downward pattern, there may be no clear correlation — knowing one value tells you almost nothing about the other.
  4. Outlier: Nine points follow a clear upward trend, but one point sits far above the rest. It is an outlier: check it before deciding what to do with it.
  5. Line of best fit: If the line is y = 6x + 40, a study time of 2 hours gives an estimated score of 6 × 2 + 40 = 52. Check: 2 hours is inside the data range, so this interpolation is reasonable.
  6. Interpolation: With the line y = 5x + 20, a value of x = 6 gives y = 5 × 6 + 20 = 50. This is inside the data range, so the estimate is cautious and reasonable.
  7. Extrapolation warning: The same line at x = 20 gives y = 120. If scores are out of 100, that answer is impossible — the line must not be used that far outside the data.
  8. Third factor: Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases are positively correlated, but neither causes the other. Hot, sunny weather is the third factor behind both.

Independent practice

Attempt each problem before reviewing the worked answer.

  1. What does positive correlation mean? Answer: Both variables tend to increase together.
  2. What does negative correlation mean? Answer: One tends to increase as the other decreases.
  3. What is an outlier? Answer: A point far from the general pattern.
  4. Why label axes with units? Answer: So values and measurements can be interpreted correctly.
  5. Can correlation prove causation? Answer: No.
  6. What is a line of best fit used for? Answer: Summarising a trend and making cautious estimates.
  7. Should an outlier always be deleted? Answer: No; investigate it first.
  8. Why avoid predictions far beyond observed data? Answer: The pattern may not continue outside the data range.
  9. A line of best fit is y = 4x + 30, where x is hours studied. Estimate the score for 5 hours. Answer: y = 4 × 5 + 30 = 50.
  10. Four points are (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 7) and (4, 9). Describe the association. Answer: Strong positive association — the points lie close to a rising straight line, y = 2x + 1.
  11. Most points follow an upward trend, but one point is (2, 40) while the others near x = 2 score around 15. What is it, and what should you do? Answer: It is an outlier. Check whether it is a recording error before deciding anything; do not delete it automatically.
  12. Word problem: A shop records daily temperature and umbrella sales for two weeks. On warmer days, umbrella sales are lower; on rainy, cooler days they are higher. Is the association positive or negative? Answer: Negative — as temperature rises, umbrella sales tend to fall.

Printable worksheet and answer key

Worksheet questions

  1. Explain what positive correlation means, in your own words, and give one example.
  2. What is an outlier?
  3. As rainfall increases, umbrella sales increase. Describe the association.
  4. A line of best fit is y = 3x + 10. Estimate y when x = 8.
  5. Data were collected for x from 2 to 12. Why should you not use the line of best fit to predict y at x = 50?
  6. Plot the points (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 5) and (3, 7). What pattern do they show?
  7. A plot shows points scattered with no upward or downward trend. What can you say about the correlation?
  8. Two variables are strongly correlated. Does that prove one causes the other? Explain with an example.

Answer key

  1. Positive correlation means larger values of one variable tend to occur with larger values of the other — for example, hours studied and test score tend to rise together.
  2. An outlier is a point far from the general pattern of the other points.
  3. Positive association: as rainfall increases, umbrella sales tend to increase too.
  4. y = 3 × 8 + 10 = 34.
  5. That is extrapolation far outside the observed range: the pattern may not continue beyond the data, so the prediction is unreliable.
  6. The points lie on a rising straight line, y = 2x + 1, showing a strong positive association.
  7. There is little or no correlation: knowing one value tells you almost nothing about the other.
  8. No. Correlation alone does not prove causation. Example: ice-cream sales and sunburn cases are strongly correlated, but hot sunny weather is the third factor behind both.

Lesson Summary

  • Scatter plots show paired numerical data as points, one point per pair.
  • Positive and negative correlation describe the direction of a relationship; no pattern means little or no correlation.
  • The closeness of points to a trend indicates the strength of a relationship, not the steepness of the line.
  • An outlier is a point far from the pattern: check it, do not delete it automatically.
  • A line of best fit gives cautious estimates inside the data range only — never extrapolate far beyond it.
  • Correlation does not by itself prove causation; always look for a third factor.
