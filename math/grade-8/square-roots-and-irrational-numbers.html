Square Roots and Irrational Numbers

Square roots reverse squaring. Some square roots are rational numbers, while others cannot be written as a fraction of integers.

Learning time: 20–25 minutes · Level: Developing

Learning goals

  • Recognise perfect squares and evaluate their square roots.
  • Estimate square roots by locating numbers between consecutive perfect squares.
  • Distinguish rational numbers, including terminating and repeating decimals, from irrational numbers.
  • Compare positive square roots and use exact or approximate forms appropriately.

1. Perfect squares

A perfect square is the square of an integer: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, and so on. The square root of 49 is 7 because 7² = 49.

2. Estimate square roots

If a number lies between two perfect squares, its square root lies between their roots. Since 25 < 30 < 36, √30 is between 5 and 6.

3. Rational numbers

A rational number can be written as a/b where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. Integers, fractions and terminating or repeating decimals are rational.

4. Irrational numbers

An irrational number cannot be written as a ratio of integers. Its decimal expansion continues without terminating or repeating. Examples include √2 and π.

5. Compare real numbers

Use nearby perfect squares or decimal estimates to compare roots. For example, √50 is greater than 7 because 50 > 49, but less than 8 because 50 < 64.

Estimate and compare square roots

Use nearby perfect squares to estimate a square root. If 36 < 48 < 49, then 6 < √48 < 7. Since 48 is much closer to 49 than to 36, √48 is close to 7. A calculator gives an approximate decimal, but the exact value √48 can be simplified to 4√3.

Worked reasoning: To compare √48 and 6, square the positive numbers: 6² = 36. Since 48 is greater than 36, √48 is greater than 6. This comparison avoids estimating a decimal.

A square root of a perfect square is an integer, so √81 = 9 is rational. The square root of a positive integer that is not a perfect square, such as √11, is irrational. Its decimal continues without ending or repeating.

  • Do not assume every square root is irrational.
  • When comparing positive square roots, compare the numbers inside the roots.
  • Use an approximation sign (≈), not an equals sign, for rounded decimals.

Worked examples

  1. √81 = 9 because 9² = 81.
  2. √20 lies between 4 and 5 because 16 < 20 < 25.
  3. √2 ≈ 1.414… and is irrational.
  4. 0.375 = 3/8, so it is rational.

Independent practice

Attempt each question before reviewing the answer.

  1. Find √121. Answer: 11.
  2. Between which integers is √40? Answer: Between 6 and 7.
  3. Is 0.272727… rational? Answer: Yes; it repeats.
  4. Is √11 rational or irrational? Answer: Irrational.
  5. Which is greater, √48 or 6? Answer: √48 is greater than 6 because 48 is greater than 36.
  6. Write 0.6 as a fraction. Answer: 3/5.
  7. Is 7 an integer and rational? Answer: Yes; 7 = 7/1.
  8. Between which integers is √70? Answer: Between 8 and 9.

Lesson Summary

  • Perfect squares have integer square roots; other roots can be estimated between nearby squares.
  • Rational numbers can be written as a ratio of integers; irrational numbers cannot.
  • Use number-line positions and estimates to compare real numbers.
  • Check approximations by squaring them and rounding as directed.
