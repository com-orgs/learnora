Square Roots and Irrational Numbers

Square roots reverse squaring. Some square roots are rational numbers, while others cannot be written as a fraction of integers.

Learning time: 25–30 minutes · Level: Developing

Learning goals

  • Recognise perfect squares and evaluate their square roots.
  • Estimate square roots by locating numbers between consecutive perfect squares.
  • Distinguish rational numbers, including terminating and repeating decimals, from irrational numbers.
  • Compare positive square roots and use exact or approximate forms appropriately.

1. Perfect squares

A perfect square is the square of an integer: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, and so on. The square root of 49 is 7 because 7² = 49.

Learn the perfect squares up to 15², because they make every estimate faster: 1² = 1, 2² = 4, 3² = 9, 4² = 16, 5² = 25, 6² = 36, 7² = 49, 8² = 64, 9² = 81, 10² = 100, 11² = 121, 12² = 144, 13² = 169, 14² = 196 and 15² = 225. Squaring a negative integer gives the same positive result: (−7)² = 49, just as 7² = 49.

The symbol √ gives the principal (non-negative) square root. So √25 = 5, even though both 5 and −5 square to 25. When a problem asks for the square roots of 25, the answer is 5 and −5; when it asks for √25, the answer is 5 only.

Worked example: Find √144. 12 × 12 = 144, so √144 = 12.

2. Estimate square roots

If a number lies between two perfect squares, its square root lies between their roots. Since 25 < 30 < 36, √30 is between 5 and 6.

To refine an estimate, test a decimal by squaring it. For √30, try 5.5: 5.5² = 30.25, which is slightly above 30, and 5.4² = 29.16, which is below 30. So √30 is between 5.4 and 5.5, and much closer to 5.5. To one decimal place, √30 ≈ 5.5. A calculator gives √30 ≈ 5.477, which agrees with the estimate.

Closeness to an endpoint also helps. 50 is much closer to 49 than to 64, so √50 is much closer to 7 than to 8: in fact √50 ≈ 7.07. But 60 is nearer to 64 than to 49, so √60 is nearer to 8 than to 7: √60 ≈ 7.7. Always check an estimate by squaring it: if the square is close to the original number, the estimate is good.

Worked example: Estimate √90 to one decimal place. 81 < 90 < 100, so √90 is between 9 and 10. Try 9.5: 9.5² = 90.25, slightly above 90. Try 9.4: 9.4² = 88.36. So √90 is between 9.4 and 9.5, closer to 9.5. To one decimal place, √90 ≈ 9.5.

3. Rational numbers

A rational number can be written as a/b where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. Integers, fractions and terminating or repeating decimals are rational.

Terminating decimals stop: 0.25 = 1/4, 0.6 = 3/5 and 1.5 = 3/2. Repeating decimals go on forever in a pattern: 0.333… = 1/3, 0.272727… = 3/11 and 0.8333… = 5/6. Every integer is rational too, because it can be written over 1: 7 = 7/1 and −4 = −4/1. A useful test is: if the decimal stops or repeats, the number is rational.

Worked example: Is 0.125 rational? Yes. It terminates, and 0.125 = 125/1,000 = 1/8.

4. Irrational numbers

An irrational number cannot be written as a ratio of integers. Its decimal expansion continues without terminating or repeating. Examples include √2 and π.

√2 ≈ 1.41421356… and π ≈ 3.14159265… . Their digits continue forever with no repeating block, so no fraction of integers can equal them exactly. The square root of a positive integer is irrational exactly when the integer is not a perfect square: √3, √5, √7 and √11 are all irrational, while √16 = 4 and √121 = 11 are rational. Not every long decimal is irrational: 0.333… repeats, so it is rational, but a decimal such as 0.1010010001… , which never settles into a repeating block, is irrational.

Because irrational numbers cannot be written exactly as decimals, we often keep them in exact form. In a calculation, √2 is exact; 1.41 is only an approximation. Use the exact form until the final step, and round only when the question asks for a decimal.

Worked example: Is √18 rational or irrational? 18 is not a perfect square (16 < 18 < 25), so √18 is irrational. Its value is about 4.24, since 4.24² = 17.9776.

5. Compare real numbers

Use nearby perfect squares or decimal estimates to compare roots. For example, √50 is greater than 7 because 50 > 49, but less than 8 because 50 < 64.

To compare two positive square roots, compare the numbers inside them: if a > b, then √a > √b. So √40 > √36 = 6, and √40 < √49 = 7. To compare a root with an integer, square the integer: is √65 greater than 8? 8² = 64, and 65 > 64, so √65 > 8. Is √65 greater than 8.1? 8.1² = 65.61, and 65 < 65.61, so √65 < 8.1. Together, 8 < √65 < 8.1.

Ordering several numbers is easier after estimating each one. Order 7, √45 and √60: √45 ≈ 6.71 and √60 ≈ 7.75, so √45 < 7 < √60. On a number line, √2 ≈ 1.41 sits between 1 and 2, and √3 ≈ 1.73 sits between √2 and 2.

Worked example: Which is greater, √28 or 5.3? Square 5.3: 5.3² = 28.09. Since 28 < 28.09, √28 < 5.3. Check: √28 ≈ 5.29.

6. Simplifying square roots

A square root can often be written in a simpler exact form by taking out a perfect-square factor: √(a × b) = √a × √b when a and b are non-negative. For √48, 48 = 16 × 3, so √48 = √16 × √3 = 4√3. The number 4√3 is exact, while 6.93 is only an approximation of it.

Look for the largest perfect square that divides the number. For √72, 72 = 36 × 2, so √72 = 6√2. For √75, 75 = 25 × 3, so √75 = 5√3. For √20, 20 = 4 × 5, so √20 = 2√5. If no perfect square greater than 1 divides the number, the root is already in its simplest form: √7, √11 and √15 cannot be simplified.

Worked example: Simplify √200. 200 = 100 × 2, so √200 = √100 × √2 = 10√2. Check: 10√2 ≈ 10 × 1.414 = 14.14, and √200 ≈ 14.14.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1: Adding square roots like ordinary numbers. √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7, but √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5. A square root does not split over addition: √(a + b) is not √a + √b.

Mistake 2: Calling every square root irrational. √64 = 8 and √100 = 10 are rational. Only roots of non-perfect squares, such as √5, are irrational.

Mistake 3: Using = for an approximation. √2 ≈ 1.41, not √2 = 1.41. The equals sign means exactly equal; use ≈ for rounded values.

Mistake 4: Taking the square root of a negative number. There is no real number whose square is negative, so √(−9) is not a real number. (Squaring always gives a non-negative result.)

Mistake 5: Forgetting that √ gives the positive root. √36 = 6, not −6. The equation x² = 36 has two solutions, 6 and −6, but the symbol √36 means 6 only.

Estimate and compare square roots

Use nearby perfect squares to estimate a square root. If 36 < 48 < 49, then 6 < √48 < 7. Since 48 is much closer to 49 than to 36, √48 is close to 7. A calculator gives an approximate decimal, but the exact value √48 can be simplified to 4√3.

Worked reasoning: To compare √48 and 6, square the positive numbers: 6² = 36. Since 48 is greater than 36, √48 is greater than 6. This comparison avoids estimating a decimal.

Refining an estimate: To estimate √30, first bracket it: 25 < 30 < 36, so 5 < √30 < 6. Then test 5.5: 5.5² = 30.25, slightly above 30, and 5.4² = 29.16, below 30. So √30 is between 5.4 and 5.5, about 5.48.

A square root of a perfect square is an integer, so √81 = 9 is rational. The square root of a positive integer that is not a perfect square, such as √11, is irrational. Its decimal continues without ending or repeating.

  • Do not assume every square root is irrational.
  • When comparing positive square roots, compare the numbers inside the roots.
  • Use an approximation sign (≈), not an equals sign, for rounded decimals.

Worked examples

  1. √81 = 9 because 9² = 81.
  2. √20 lies between 4 and 5 because 16 < 20 < 25.
  3. √2 ≈ 1.414… and is irrational.
  4. 0.375 = 3/8, so it is rational.
  5. √169 = 13 because 13² = 169.
  6. Estimate √60. 49 < 60 < 64, so √60 is between 7 and 8. Since 7.7² = 59.29 and 7.8² = 60.84, √60 ≈ 7.7 to one decimal place.
  7. Simplify √75. 75 = 25 × 3, so √75 = √25 × √3 = 5√3.
  8. Order √20, 4 and √30. √20 ≈ 4.47 and √30 ≈ 5.48, so 4 < √20 < √30.

  4. Is √11 rational or irrational? Answer: Irrational.
  5. Which is greater, √48 or 6? Answer: √48 is greater than 6 because 48 is greater than 36.

  10. Between which integers is √55? Answer: Between 7 and 8.
  11. Is √64 rational or irrational? Answer: Rational, because √64 = 8.

Lesson Summary

  • Perfect squares have integer square roots; other roots can be estimated between nearby squares.
  • Rational numbers can be written as a ratio of integers; irrational numbers cannot.
  • Use number-line positions and estimates to compare real numbers.
  • Check approximations by squaring them and rounding as directed.
  • Simplify a root by taking out its largest perfect-square factor, for example √48 = 4√3.
  • Keep an irrational number in exact form until a decimal approximation is asked for.
