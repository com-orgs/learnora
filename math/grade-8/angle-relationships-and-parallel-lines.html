Angle Relationships and Parallel Lines

Parallel lines cut by a transversal make eight angles with only a few possible sizes. Learn to name each angle, find the missing one, and give the reason why it must be that size — without ever measuring it.

Learning time: 25–30 minutes · Level: Developing

Learning goals

  • Name corresponding, alternate interior, alternate exterior, co-interior and vertically opposite angles in a diagram.
  • Use parallel-line rules to find a missing angle and give a one-line reason for each step.
  • Use straight-line, full-turn and vertically opposite angle facts to calculate unknown angles.
  • Solve a two-step missing-angle problem by combining two rules.
  • Use triangle and polygon interior-angle sums to solve problems, and spot the error in a wrong solution.

1. What a transversal makes

When one straight line crosses two other lines it is called a transversal. At each crossing it makes four angles, so two crossings make eight angles in total. Imagine labelling them a to h: at the top crossing, a is top-left, b is top-right, c is bottom-left and d is bottom-right; at the bottom crossing, e is top-left, f is top-right, g is bottom-left and h is bottom-right. The whole lesson is about sorting these eight angles into five families: vertically opposite pairs, corresponding pairs, alternate pairs, co-interior pairs, and pairs on a straight line. Once you can name the family, the rule follows — and the rule tells you the size.

Worked example: Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal and one angle is 72°. Its corresponding angle is also 72°, because corresponding angles on parallel lines are equal. Its alternate angle is 72° too. Its co-interior partner is 180° − 72° = 108°. Check: 72° + 108° = 180°, exactly as co-interior angles must add to 180°.

Before using any of these rules, always check the diagram for the small arrow marks on the lines. Matching arrows mean the lines are parallel, and only then do the corresponding, alternate and co-interior rules apply. If the lines are not marked parallel, the angles might look equal in the sketch but you cannot prove it — and in a test, a sketch is never proof.

2. Angles on a straight line and around a point

Angles on a straight line add to 180°. They are called supplementary angles: two angles that add to 180° are supplements of each other. If one angle is 127°, the adjacent angle on the same straight line is 180° − 127° = 53°. This is the fact you will reach for most often, because every straight edge in a diagram hides a 180° pair.

Angles around a point add to 360°, a full turn. Add the known angles and subtract their total from 360° to find the missing angle.

Worked example: Four angles meet at a point: 90°, 120°, 80° and x. Add the known angles: 90° + 120° + 80° = 290°. Subtract from a full turn: x = 360° − 290° = 70°. Check: 90° + 120° + 80° + 70° = 360°. Real life: a round pizza cut into slices, or the angles around the centre of a clock face, both add to 360°.

Worked example: A straight road meets a side road. One angle at the junction is 127°. The angle next to it on the straight road is 180° − 127° = 53°. Check: 127° + 53° = 180°. The two angles are supplementary, so knowing one tells you the other at once.

3. Vertically opposite angles

When two straight lines cross, they make two pairs of equal angles opposite each other. These are called vertically opposite angles, and they are always equal. Adjacent angles on a straight line are supplementary, which is why the proof works: each angle in the pair shares a straight line with the same neighbour, so both must be 180° minus that neighbour — and therefore equal to each other.

Worked example: Two lines cross and one angle is 118°. The angle opposite it is also 118°, because vertically opposite angles are equal. The angle next to it is 180° − 118° = 62°. Check: 118° + 62° = 180°, as angles on a straight line must add to 180°.

Worked example: Vertically opposite angles are (2x + 10)° and 70°. Set them equal: 2x + 10 = 70. Subtract 10 from both sides: 2x = 60. Divide by 2: x = 30. Check: 2 × 30 + 10 = 70°, which matches the opposite angle.

4. Corresponding angles — the F-shape

When a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding angles are equal. They sit in the same relative position at each crossing — both top-left, or both bottom-right, for example. Trace the two angles with your finger and you will see an F-shape, which is the memory aid: F means corresponding angles are equal.

Worked example: Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. One angle is 65°. Its corresponding angle is also 65°. Reason: corresponding angles on parallel lines are equal. Always write the reason — a number without a reason earns no marks in a test, and the reason is what proves you did not just guess.

Worked example: Corresponding angles are (3x + 10)° and (2x + 35)°. Set them equal: 3x + 10 = 2x + 35. Subtract 2x from both sides: x + 10 = 35. Subtract 10: x = 25. Each angle is 3 × 25 + 10 = 85°. Check: 2 × 25 + 35 = 85° as well, so both expressions give the same angle.

5. Alternate angles — the Z-shape

Alternate angles sit on opposite sides of the transversal and between the parallel lines (interior) or outside them (exterior). They are equal. Trace them and you will see a Z-shape: Z means alternate angles are equal. Alternate interior angles are the pair between the lines; alternate exterior angles are the pair outside the lines. Both pairs are equal when the lines are parallel.

Worked example: Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. An alternate interior angle is 48°. Its alternate partner is also 48°. Reason: alternate interior angles on parallel lines are equal. Check with the straight line: the angle next to it is 180° − 48° = 132°, and its alternate partner is also 132°.

Worked example: An alternate exterior angle is 125°. Its alternate exterior partner is 125°. Reason: alternate exterior angles on parallel lines are equal. The co-interior angle next to it is 180° − 125° = 55°, which is a useful check — 125° + 55° = 180°.

6. Co-interior angles — the C-shape

Co-interior angles sit between the parallel lines on the same side of the transversal. Unlike the other pairs, they are not equal: they add to 180°. Trace them and you will see a C-shape (sometimes called a U-shape): C means co-interior angles are supplementary. This is the pair learners mix up most, so whenever you see a C-shape, think "add to 180°", not "equal".

Worked example: One co-interior angle is 110°. Its partner is 180° − 110° = 70°. Reason: co-interior angles on parallel lines are supplementary. Check: 110° + 70° = 180°.

Worked example: Co-interior angles are 4x° and (2x + 30)°. They add to 180°, so 4x + 2x + 30 = 180. Simplify: 6x + 30 = 180. Subtract 30: 6x = 150. Divide by 6: x = 25. Check: 4 × 25 = 100° and 2 × 25 + 30 = 80°, and 100° + 80° = 180°.

7. Triangle and polygon angle sums

The interior angles of a triangle add to 180°. For an n-sided polygon, the interior-angle sum is (n − 2) × 180°. A regular polygon has equal interior angles, so each one is the total divided by n. Real life: a stop sign is a regular octagon, and a honeycomb is made of regular hexagons — their angles are fixed by this rule, not by measuring.

Worked example: A regular hexagon has interior-angle sum (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°, so each angle is 720° ÷ 6 = 120°. Check: 6 × 120° = 720°. A regular octagon has sum (8 − 2) × 180° = 1,080°, so each angle is 1,080° ÷ 8 = 135°.

Worked example: A pentagon has interior-angle sum (5 − 2) × 180° = 540°. If four of its angles are 110°, 95°, 130° and 105°, the fifth is 540° − (110° + 95° + 130° + 105°) = 540° − 440° = 100°. Check: 110 + 95 + 130 + 105 + 100 = 540.

8. Two-step problems — combining two rules

Most real questions need two rules, not one. The method is: find one angle with the first rule, then use that angle with a second rule to reach the angle you actually want. Write the reason at every step, because the second step depends on the first being right.

Worked example: Corresponding angles are (5x − 20)° and (3x + 40)°. Set them equal: 5x − 20 = 3x + 40. Subtract 3x: 2x − 20 = 40. Add 20: 2x = 60. Divide by 2: x = 30. Each angle is 5 × 30 − 20 = 130°. Check: 3 × 30 + 40 = 130° as well.

Worked example: Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. Angle a is 125°. Angle b is vertically opposite a, so b = 125°. Angle c is co-interior with b, so c = 180° − 125° = 55°. Angle d is corresponding to c, so d = 55°. Check: b + c = 125° + 55° = 180°, as co-interior angles must add to 180°. One fact (a = 125°) gave all four angles.

9. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mistake 1 — Calling co-interior angles equal. Wrong: "The co-interior partner of 118° is 118°." Correct: co-interior angles add to 180°, so the partner is 180° − 118° = 62°. Only corresponding and alternate angles are equal.
  • Mistake 2 — Using parallel-line rules when the lines are not parallel. Wrong: "These two angles look equal, so they are corresponding." Correct: the rules work only when the lines are marked parallel or the question says they are parallel. A sketch is not proof.
  • Mistake 3 — Mixing alternate interior with alternate exterior. Wrong: pairing an angle between the lines with one outside them and calling them alternate interior. Correct: alternate interior angles are both between the lines; alternate exterior angles are both outside them. Both pairs are equal, but they are different pairs.
  • Mistake 4 — Giving an answer with no reason. Wrong: "x = 55°." Correct: "x = 55° because co-interior angles on parallel lines are supplementary." The reason is part of the answer.
  • Mistake 5 — Reading the wrong angle off a diagram. Wrong: taking the angle next to the one you need (its supplement) and treating it as the answer. Correct: check whether the angle you found is the one asked for, or its neighbour on a straight line — if it is the neighbour, subtract from 180°.
  • Mistake 6 — Assuming a diagram is drawn to scale. Wrong: measuring the diagram with a protractor and trusting the measurement. Correct: use the angle rules to calculate. Diagrams in questions are often deliberately not to scale.

Reasoning with angle relationships

When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, corresponding angles are equal and alternate interior angles are equal. Co-interior angles on the same side of the transversal add to 180°. These relationships let you find missing angles even when the diagram is not drawn to scale.

Worked reasoning: Suppose two corresponding angles are (3x + 10)° and (2x + 35)°. Set them equal: 3x + 10 = 2x + 35, so x = 25. Each angle is 85°. Check by substituting 25 into both expressions.

  • Do not assume two angles are equal just because they look equal in a sketch.
  • Use 180° for a straight line and 360° for a full turn.
  • Write the angle relationship before solving for an unknown.

Self-check: If co-interior angles are 4x° and (2x + 30)°, then 4x + 2x + 30 = 180, so x = 25.

Worked examples

  1. Straight line: One angle is 68°. Its supplement is 180° − 68° = 112°. Check: 68° + 112° = 180°.
  2. Around a point: 90° + 120° + 80° + x = 360°, so x = 360° − 290° = 70°. Check: the four angles add to 360°.
  3. Vertically opposite: Two lines cross and one angle is 118°. The opposite angle is 118° (vertically opposite angles are equal) and the adjacent angle is 180° − 118° = 62°.
  4. Corresponding: Parallel lines, one angle 65°. Its corresponding angle is 65°, because corresponding angles on parallel lines are equal.
  5. Alternate interior: Parallel lines, one angle 48°. Its alternate interior partner is 48°. The co-interior angle next to it is 180° − 48° = 132°.
  6. Co-interior: One angle is 110°. Its co-interior partner is 180° − 110° = 70°. Check: 110° + 70° = 180°.
  7. Equation: Corresponding angles (3x + 10)° and (2x + 35)° are equal, so 3x + 10 = 2x + 35, x = 25, and each angle is 85°. Check: 3 × 25 + 10 = 85° and 2 × 25 + 35 = 85°.
  8. Regular octagon: Interior-angle sum is (8 − 2) × 180° = 1,080°, so each angle is 1,080° ÷ 8 = 135°. Check: 8 × 135° = 1,080°.

Independent practice

Attempt each question before reviewing the answer.

  1. Find the supplement of 38°. Answer: 180° − 38° = 142°.
  2. Find the missing angle around a point if the other angles total 275°. Answer: 360° − 275° = 85°.
  3. What angle is vertically opposite to 73°? Answer: 73°, because vertically opposite angles are equal.
  4. A triangle has angles 35° and 85°. Find the third angle. Answer: 180° − 35° − 85° = 60°.
  5. Find the interior-angle sum of a pentagon. Answer: (5 − 2) × 180° = 540°.
  6. What is the co-interior angle to 112° on parallel lines? Answer: 180° − 112° = 68°.
  7. A regular hexagon has interior-angle sum 720°. What is each angle? Answer: 720° ÷ 6 = 120°.
  8. What is the corresponding angle to 64° on parallel lines? Answer: 64°, because corresponding angles are equal.
  9. Find the interior-angle sum of a regular heptagon. Answer: (7 − 2) × 180° = 900°.
  10. Error-spotting: Sara says co-interior angles are equal, so the partner of 118° is 118°. Is she right? Answer: No. Co-interior angles add to 180°, so the partner is 180° − 118° = 62°.
  11. Word problem: A ladder leans against a vertical wall, making 65° with the ground. The wall meets the ground at 90°. What is the angle between the ladder and the wall? Answer: 180° − 90° − 65° = 25°.
  12. Word problem: Two parallel railway lines are crossed by a road. One angle at the crossing is 74°. Find its alternate angle and its co-interior angle. Answer: Alternate angle = 74° (alternate angles are equal); co-interior angle = 180° − 74° = 106°. Check: 74° + 106° = 180°.

4. The interior-angle sum of a pentagon is…

5. A triangle has angles 50° and 60°. The third is…

Printable worksheet and answer key

These questions are new: they use different numbers from the practice section, so try them fresh. Show each step, write the angle rule you use before calculating, and check answers only after attempting the questions.

Worksheet questions

  1. Find the supplement of 52°.
  2. Angles around a point are 110°, 95°, 75° and x. Find x.
  3. What angle is vertically opposite to 129°?
  4. A triangle has angles 58° and 74°. Find the third angle.
  5. Find the interior-angle sum of a hexagon.
  6. What is the co-interior angle to 97° on parallel lines?
  7. A regular pentagon has interior-angle sum 540°. What is each angle?
  8. Corresponding angles are (4x − 15)° and (2x + 35)°. Find x and the size of each angle.

Answer key

  1. 128° because 180° − 52° = 128°.
  2. 80° because 110° + 95° + 75° = 280° and 360° − 280° = 80°.
  3. 129°; vertically opposite angles are equal.
  4. 48° because 180° − 58° − 74° = 48°.
  5. 720° because (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°.
  6. 83° because co-interior angles sum to 180°: 180° − 97° = 83°.
  7. 108° because 540° ÷ 5 = 108°.
  8. x = 25 and each angle is 85°: 4x − 15 = 2x + 35 gives 2x = 50, so x = 25. Check: 4 × 25 − 15 = 85° and 2 × 25 + 35 = 85°.

Lesson Summary

  • Angles on a straight line sum to 180° and angles around a point sum to 360°.
  • Vertically opposite angles are equal.
  • For parallel lines, corresponding and alternate angles are equal while co-interior angles sum to 180°.
  • Use the F-shape for corresponding, the Z-shape for alternate and the C-shape for co-interior angles.
  • Triangle interior angles total 180°; a polygon with n sides has interior-angle sum (n − 2) × 180°.
  • Two-step problems combine two rules: find one angle, then use it to find the next.
  • Write the angle rule used at each step and check the final value.
