Exponents, Powers and Scientific Notation

Exponents are a compact way to write repeated multiplication. They also help describe very large and very small quantities.

Learning time: 25–30 minutes · Level: Developing

Learning goals

  • Interpret powers as repeated multiplication and evaluate powers with positive, zero and negative integer exponents.
  • Apply the product, quotient and power-of-a-power laws when the bases are the same.
  • Write very large and very small values in scientific notation and convert them back to ordinary form.
  • Check that the coefficient in scientific notation has an absolute value from 1 up to, but not including, 10.

1. What an exponent means

In 4³, the base is 4 and the exponent is 3: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. The exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor. It is not multiplication by the exponent: 4³ is 64, while 4 × 3 is only 12.

We read powers in words. 5² is "five squared" and equals 25, because 5 × 5 = 25. 5³ is "five cubed" and equals 125, because 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. For higher powers we say "to the power of": 2⁶ is "two to the power of six" and equals 64, because 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 64.

An exponent of 1 changes nothing: 7¹ = 7. An exponent of 2 always means the number multiplied by itself once, and an exponent of 3 means it appears three times. The base can also be a fraction or a decimal. For example, (1/2)² = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, and 0.3² = 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.09.

Worked example: Evaluate 6³. Write it out as 6 × 6 × 6. First, 6 × 6 = 36. Then 36 × 6 = 216. So 6³ = 216.

2. Zero and negative exponents

For any non-zero number a, a⁰ = 1. A negative exponent means reciprocal: a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ. For example, 2⁻³ = 1/8. Zero to a negative power is undefined.

The zero exponent makes sense if you follow the pattern of dividing by the base each time the exponent falls by one: 2³ = 8, 2² = 4, 2¹ = 2, so 2⁰ = 1. Continuing the same pattern gives 2⁻¹ = 1/2 and 2⁻² = 1/4. Each step divides by 2 again, which is the same as taking the reciprocal of the matching positive power.

Use the reciprocal rule for any base. 3⁻² = 1/3² = 1/9, and 10⁻³ = 1/10³ = 1/1,000 = 0.001. A negative exponent never makes the answer negative: it only moves the power to the denominator. So 5⁻¹ = 1/5 = 0.2, a positive number. Remember that 0⁰ is undefined, and 0 raised to a negative power is also undefined, because it would mean dividing by zero.

Worked example: Evaluate 4⁻². First write the reciprocal: 4⁻² = 1/4². Then 4² = 16, so 4⁻² = 1/16 = 0.0625.

3. Laws of exponents

For the same non-zero base: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (a ≠ 0); (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ. Also (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ and (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ when defined. Add exponents only when multiplying like bases.

Product law: when you multiply powers with the same base, add the exponents. For example, 2³ × 2² = 2⁵ = 32. Check: 8 × 4 = 32. Quotient law: when you divide powers with the same base, subtract the exponents. For example, 3⁵ ÷ 3² = 3³ = 27. Check: 243 ÷ 9 = 27.

Power of a power: multiply the exponents. For example, (4²)³ = 4⁶ = 4,096. Check: 4² = 16 and 16³ = 4,096. Power of a product: apply the exponent to each factor: (2 × 5)³ = 2³ × 5³ = 8 × 125 = 1,000. Power of a quotient: apply the exponent to the numerator and the denominator: (6/2)² = 6²/2² = 36/4 = 9.

These laws only work when their conditions are met. You cannot add exponents when the bases are different: 2³ × 3² cannot be combined into a single power, so evaluate it instead: 8 × 9 = 72. You also cannot use the product law for addition: 2³ + 2⁴ = 8 + 16 = 24, not 2⁷. And (a + b)² is not a² + b².

4. Powers of ten

10⁰ = 1, 10¹ = 10, 10² = 100, and 10⁻² = 0.01. Each step up in a positive exponent multiplies by 10; each step down divides by 10.

Powers of ten are the backbone of our place-value system. 10³ = 1,000, 10⁶ = 1,000,000, and 10⁻³ = 0.001. Multiplying a number by 10² moves its decimal point two places to the right: 3.45 × 100 = 345. Multiplying by 10⁻² moves it two places to the left: 3.45 × 0.01 = 0.0345. This is why powers of ten are so useful for very large and very small numbers: they describe the size of the number, while the other factor describes its digits.

Worked example: Write 7,000,000 as a product of a small number and a power of ten. 7,000,000 = 7 × 1,000,000 = 7 × 10⁶.

5. Scientific notation

Write a number as a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ |a| < 10 and n is an integer. For example, 4,500,000 = 4.5 × 10⁶ and 0.00072 = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴. Move the decimal left for a large number (positive exponent) and right for a small number (negative exponent).

To convert a large number, move the decimal point left until one non-zero digit is before it, and count the moves: that count is the exponent. For 93,000,000, move the point 7 places left to get 9.3, so 93,000,000 = 9.3 × 10⁷. To convert a small number, move the decimal point right until one non-zero digit is before it: 0.00000052 becomes 5.2 after 7 moves, so 0.00000052 = 5.2 × 10⁻⁷.

To convert back, move the decimal point in the direction the exponent tells you: a positive exponent moves it right, a negative exponent moves it left. For example, 2.8 × 10⁴ = 28,000 and 6.1 × 10⁻³ = 0.0061. Scientists use this form for real measurements: the distance from Earth to the Moon is about 3.84 × 10⁸ metres, and the mass of a grain of sand is roughly 1 × 10⁻⁶ kilograms. Comparing two numbers in scientific notation is easy: first compare the exponents, because a larger exponent means a larger number.

Worked example: Write 0.00036 in scientific notation. Move the decimal point right 4 places to get 3.6, so the answer is 3.6 × 10⁻⁴. Check: 3.6 × 0.0001 = 0.00036.

6. Common mistakes to avoid

Mistake 1: Reading 3⁴ as 3 × 4. 3⁴ means 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81, while 3 × 4 = 12. The exponent is a count of factors, not a multiplier.

Mistake 2: Adding exponents with different bases. 2³ × 5³ cannot become 10⁶ or 2⁶. Evaluate each power first: 8 × 125 = 1,000. Only like bases combine.

Mistake 3: Confusing (2³)² with 2³ × 2². A power of a power multiplies exponents: (2³)² = 2⁶ = 64. A product adds them: 2³ × 2² = 2⁵ = 32. They are different answers.

Mistake 4: Thinking a negative exponent gives a negative answer. 2⁻³ = 1/8 = 0.125, which is positive. The minus sign sends the power to the denominator; it does not change the sign of the value.

Mistake 5: Writing scientific notation with a coefficient of 10 or more. 25 × 10⁴ is not scientific notation, because 25 is not less than 10. Move one more place: 2.5 × 10⁵.

Mistake 6: Dropping the brackets on a negative base. (−3)² = 9, but −3² = −9, because the exponent applies to 3 only. Keep the brackets when the base is negative.

Apply exponent laws carefully

For the same non-zero base, multiply powers by adding exponents: am × an = am+n. Divide powers by subtracting exponents, and raise a power to another power by multiplying the exponents. A zero exponent gives 1 for a non-zero base. A negative exponent means take the reciprocal: 10−2 = 1/100 = 0.01.

Worked reasoning: 23 × 24 = 27 = 128. But 23 + 24 = 8 + 16 = 24; the product rule does not apply to addition.

In scientific notation, the first factor must have an absolute value at least 1 but less than 10. For example, 0.00056 = 5.6 × 10−4.

  • Keep brackets when a negative number is the base: (−2)2 = 4, while −22 = −4.
  • Do not add exponents when the bases are different.
  • Check that the decimal point moves in the correct direction.

Worked examples

  1. 3⁴ = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81.
  2. 5² × 5³ = 5⁵ = 3,125.
  3. (2³)² = 2⁶ = 64.
  4. 6.2 × 10⁵ = 620,000; 3.4 × 10⁻³ = 0.0034.
  5. Evaluate 2⁻⁴. Write the reciprocal: 2⁻⁴ = 1/2⁴ = 1/16 = 0.0625.
  6. Simplify 5⁷ ÷ 5³. Subtract the exponents: 5⁷⁻³ = 5⁴ = 625. Check: 78,125 ÷ 125 = 625.
  7. Write 52,000,000 in scientific notation. Move the decimal point 7 places left: 5.2 × 10⁷.
  8. Write 0.00043 in scientific notation. Move the decimal point 4 places right: 4.3 × 10⁻⁴. Check: 4.3 × 0.0001 = 0.00043.

Lesson Summary

  • An exponent tells how many times a base is used as a factor.
  • Use exponent laws only when their conditions are met, especially when bases match.
  • A non-zero number to the zero power equals 1; negative exponents represent reciprocals.
  • Scientific notation writes a number as a value from 1 up to but not including 10 multiplied by a power of ten.
  • Check the position of the decimal point and the sign of the exponent.
  • A power of a power multiplies exponents, while a product of like bases adds them.
  • A negative exponent never makes the answer negative; it takes the reciprocal of the power.
