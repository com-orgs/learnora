Probability Models and Compound Events

Probability measures how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 to 1. A model — a sample space, a tree diagram or a table — helps you list every possible outcome and calculate probabilities consistently, instead of guessing.

Learning time: 25–30 minutes · Level: Developing

Learning goals

  • Describe probability on a scale from 0 to 1 and calculate probabilities for equally likely outcomes.
  • List sample spaces for simple and compound experiments.
  • Use complementary probabilities and distinguish independent from dependent events.
  • Calculate probabilities for compound events using multiplication or addition when the conditions allow.
  • Use tree diagrams and account for changes when sampling without replacement.

1. Probability scale

Probability ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). A probability of ½ means equally likely to happen or not happen. For equally likely outcomes, P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. Probabilities can be written as fractions, decimals or percentages: 1/2 = 0.5 = 50% all mean the same chance.

Worked example: A fair die is rolled. P(rolling a 5) = 1/6, because there is 1 favourable outcome out of 6. P(rolling an even number) = 3/6 = 1/2, because 2, 4 and 6 are three favourable outcomes. Check: the probabilities of all six faces add to 6 × 1/6 = 1, as they must.

Real life: a weather forecast that says "30% chance of rain" means P(rain) = 0.3. It does not mean it will rain for 30% of the day — it means that in similar conditions, rain happens about 3 times out of 10.

2. Sample spaces

A sample space lists every possible outcome. For a fair six-sided die it is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Listing outcomes prevents omissions and double counting — most probability mistakes come from a sample space that is missing an outcome or counting one twice.

Worked example: Two fair coins are tossed. The sample space is {HH, HT, TH, TT} — four equally likely outcomes. Note that HT and TH are different outcomes: the first coin shows heads and the second tails, or the other way round. Treating them as one outcome is the classic mistake that makes people say "three outcomes" instead of four.

Worked example: Two fair dice are rolled. The sample space has 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely pairs: (1,1), (1,2), …, (6,6). The pairs that give a total of 7 are (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2) and (6,1) — six of them — so P(total 7) = 6/36 = 1/6. Check: 7 is the most likely total, because it has the most pairs.

3. Complementary events

An event either happens or does not happen. The event "not A" is called the complement of A, and P(not A) = 1 − P(A). For example, if P(rain) = 0.3, P(no rain) = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7. Complements are useful when "not happening" is easier to count than happening — for example, "at least one head in three tosses" is much easier as 1 − P(no heads at all).

Worked example: If P(A) = 0.65, P(not A) = 1 − 0.65 = 0.35. Check: 0.65 + 0.35 = 1. Worked example: Three coins are tossed. P(no heads) = P(TTT) = 1/8, so P(at least one head) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8. Check: the outcomes with at least one head are HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH — seven out of eight.

4. Independent events

Two events are independent when the result of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Two separate fair coin tosses are independent: the first toss does not remember what happened, and neither does the second. This is why "a tail is due after five heads" is wrong — the next toss still has P(heads) = 1/2. That wrong idea is called the gambler's fallacy.

Worked example: Two independent fair coin tosses. P(two heads) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Check against the sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}: only HH is two heads, so 1 out of 4. Worked example: A coin is tossed and a die is rolled. P(heads and a 6) = 1/2 × 1/6 = 1/12. Check: the combined sample space has 2 × 6 = 12 equally likely outcomes, and only (H, 6) is what we want.

5. Tree diagrams and caution

A tree diagram shows stages and their probabilities as branches. Multiply along a path for an "and" outcome; add probabilities for separate non-overlapping paths. Without replacement, probabilities change after the first selection, so the second-stage branches carry different probabilities from the first. Always check at the end that all the end probabilities add to 1 — if they do not, a branch is wrong.

Worked example: A bag contains 3 red and 2 blue counters. Two counters are drawn without replacement. The probability of drawing red twice is 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10. The second fraction is 2/4 because one red counter has already been removed, leaving 2 red out of 4 total. The events are dependent, so you may not use 3/5 twice.

6. Tree diagram worked example: two coin tosses

Draw a tree for two fair coin tosses. The first toss branches into Heads (1/2) and Tails (1/2). From each of those, the second toss branches again into Heads (1/2) and Tails (1/2). There are four paths, one for each outcome in the sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}.

Multiply along each path: P(HH) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4; P(HT) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4; P(TH) = 1/4; P(TT) = 1/4. Check: 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 1. Now add paths to answer questions: P(exactly one head) = P(HT) + P(TH) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. P(at least one head) = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 3/4, which also equals 1 − P(TT) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. Both methods agree, which is a good check.

Now the two-dice table. Write the 36 pairs in a 6 × 6 table, with the first die across the top and the second down the side, and fill in the totals. Count along the diagonals: total 2 has 1 pair, 3 has 2, 4 has 3, 5 has 4, 6 has 5, 7 has 6, 8 has 5, 9 has 4, 10 has 3, 11 has 2 and 12 has 1. Check: 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 36. From the table: P(total 8) = 5/36, P(a double) = 6/36 = 1/6, and P(total more than 9) = (3 + 2 + 1)/36 = 6/36 = 1/6.

7. Mutually exclusive events and "or"

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time — for one die roll, "rolling a 2" and "rolling a 5" cannot both happen. For mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). But be careful: if the events can overlap, adding double-counts the overlap. For one die roll, P(even or greater than 4) is not 3/6 + 2/6, because 6 is both even and greater than 4. Count the outcomes instead: {2, 4, 5, 6} is 4 outcomes, so the probability is 4/6 = 2/3.

Worked example: A card is drawn from a normal pack of 52. P(a king or a queen) = 4/52 + 4/52 = 8/52 = 2/13, because a card cannot be both a king and a queen. Check: 8 favourable cards out of 52.

8. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mistake 1 — Counting HT and TH as one outcome. Wrong: "Two coin tosses have three outcomes: two heads, two tails, one of each." Correct: the sample space is {HH, HT, TH, TT} — four equally likely outcomes, so P(exactly one head) = 2/4 = 1/2, not 1/3.
  • Mistake 2 — Adding probabilities for "and". Wrong: P(two heads) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Correct: for independent events, multiply: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Add only for "or" with mutually exclusive events.
  • Mistake 3 — Using the same probability after removing an item. Wrong: drawing two counters without replacement and using 3/5 twice. Correct: after one red is removed from 3 red and 2 blue, the second probability is 2/4, not 3/5.
  • Mistake 4 — Forgetting the complement shortcut. Wrong: listing all seven outcomes for "at least one head in three tosses". Correct: P(at least one head) = 1 − P(no heads) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.
  • Mistake 5 — Treating dependent events as independent. Wrong: "The first card drawn does not matter." Correct: drawing without replacement changes the sample space, so the events are dependent and the probabilities change.
  • Mistake 6 — Believing in "due" outcomes. Wrong: "Five heads in a row, so a tail is due." Correct: each toss is independent, so P(tails) is still 1/2. Past results do not change the next probability.

Understand compound events

For independent events, the outcome of the first event does not change the probability of the second, so multiply the probabilities to find the chance that both happen. For mutually exclusive events, which cannot happen together, add their probabilities to find the chance that either happens. When an item is not replaced, the total number of possible outcomes changes and the events are dependent.

Worked reasoning: A bag contains 3 red and 2 blue counters. Two counters are drawn without replacement. The probability of drawing red twice is 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10. The second fraction is 2/4 because one red counter has already been removed.

For two independent coin tosses, the probability of two heads is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. A tree diagram helps show each possible path and its probability.

  • Check whether the first event changes the sample space.
  • Use multiplication for “and” when the events are independent.
  • Use addition for “either” only after checking whether the events overlap.

Worked examples

  1. A fair die: P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2, because 2, 4 and 6 are three favourable outcomes out of six.
  2. Two coin tosses: P(two heads) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Check: only HH out of {HH, HT, TH, TT}.
  3. Complement: If P(A) = 0.65, P(not A) = 1 − 0.65 = 0.35. Check: 0.65 + 0.35 = 1.
  4. Two dice, total 7: Six pairs give 7 out of 36, so P(total 7) = 6/36 = 1/6.
  5. At least one head: In two tosses, P(at least one head) = 3/4. Check: 1 − P(TT) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4.
  6. Without replacement: A bag has 3 red and 2 blue counters. P(red then red) = 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10.
  7. Dependent draw: A bag has 4 red and 3 blue counters. P(red then blue, without replacement) = 4/7 × 3/6 = 12/42 = 2/7. Check: after a red is removed, 3 blue remain out of 6 total.
  8. Mutually exclusive "or": One card from a pack: P(king or queen) = 4/52 + 4/52 = 8/52 = 2/13.

Independent practice

Attempt each problem before reviewing the worked answer.

  1. Find P(rolling a 5) on a fair die. Answer: 1/6.
  2. Find P(rolling an odd number). Answer: 3/6 = 1/2.
  3. If P(A) = 0.2, find P(not A). Answer: 0.8.
  4. Find P(two heads) in two independent fair coin tosses. Answer: 1/4.
  5. How many outcomes are in the sample space for two coin tosses? Answer: 4: HH, HT, TH, TT.
  6. Find P(sum is 7) when rolling two fair dice. Answer: 6/36 = 1/6.
  7. What does mutually exclusive mean? Answer: Events cannot happen at the same time.
  8. Why do probabilities change without replacement? Answer: The total and favourable items remaining may change.
  9. Find P(rolling a 6, then rolling a 6 again) with a fair die. Answer: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36.
  10. A spinner has P(red) = 3/8. What is P(not red)? Answer: 1 − 3/8 = 5/8.
  11. Two fair coins are tossed. Find P(exactly one head). Answer: 2/4 = 1/2, from HT and TH.
  12. Word problem: A bag has 5 green and 5 yellow counters. Two are drawn without replacement. Find P(both green). Answer: 5/10 × 4/9 = 20/90 = 2/9.

Printable worksheet and answer key

These questions are new: they use different numbers from the practice section, so try them fresh. Write each probability as a fraction or decimal and show the sample space where useful, and check answers only after attempting the questions.

Worksheet questions

  1. A fair die is rolled. Find P(rolling a prime number).
  2. A coin is tossed three times. Find P(three heads).
  3. If P(A) = 0.45, find P(not A).
  4. Two fair dice are rolled. Find P(the total is 8).
  5. A bag has 6 white and 4 black counters. One counter is drawn. Find P(black).
  6. A spinner has P(red) = 1/4. It is spun twice. Find P(red twice).
  7. A bag has 3 red and 7 blue counters. Two are drawn without replacement. Find P(red then blue).
  8. Explain why P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) only works for independent events, giving an example where it fails.

Answer key

  1. Prime numbers on a die are 2, 3 and 5 — three outcomes out of six, so 3/6 = 1/2.
  2. 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8. The sample space has 8 equally likely outcomes and only HHH is three heads.
  3. 1 − 0.45 = 0.55.
  4. Five pairs give a total of 8 — (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2) — so 5/36.
  5. 4 black out of 10 total, so 4/10 = 2/5.
  6. 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16.
  7. 3/10 × 7/9 = 21/90 = 7/30. The second probability is 7/9 because one red has been removed, leaving 9 counters.
  8. The multiplication rule needs the second probability to be unchanged by the first event. Without replacement it changes: for example, drawing two red counters from 3 red and 2 blue gives 3/5 × 2/4 = 3/10, not 3/5 × 3/5.

Lesson Summary

  • Probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • A sample space lists possible outcomes; a complement is everything outside the event, and P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • For independent events, multiply probabilities to find the chance that both occur; for mutually exclusive events, add them for "either".
  • A tree diagram shows every path: multiply along a path, add across separate paths, and check the ends add to 1.
  • Without replacement, probabilities change after the first selection — the events are dependent.
  • Past results never change the next probability: a tail is never "due".
