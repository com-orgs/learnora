Paragraph Writing: Topic Sentences and Supporting Details

A strong paragraph develops one main idea. Learn to plan, organise and connect your sentences so readers can follow your thinking.

Study time: 20–25 minutes · Includes: worked example, practice, five-question quiz and printable worksheet.

What You Will Learn

  • Identify the purpose of a paragraph and keep it focused on one main idea.
  • Write a topic sentence that states the controlling idea.
  • Choose specific supporting details, examples or evidence.
  • Use transitions to show how ideas connect.
  • Write a conclusion that reinforces the main idea without simply repeating every word.

1. Understand the paragraph structure

A paragraph is a group of related sentences that develop one main point. In many school paragraphs, you will find three useful parts:

PartPurposeQuestion to ask
Topic sentenceIntroduces the main idea and narrows the focus.What is this paragraph mainly about?
Supporting sentencesExplain the idea with reasons, facts, examples or details.How can I explain or prove this point?
Concluding sentenceBrings the paragraph to a natural close.What should the reader remember?

Not every paragraph must follow exactly the same pattern. A narrative or creative paragraph may use a different structure, but unity and clear development still matter.

2. Write a focused topic sentence

A topic sentence usually names the subject and expresses the specific point you will develop. Avoid a topic that is too broad or a statement that gives no direction.

Too broad: Exercise is important.
More focused: A short daily walk can improve a student's energy and concentration.

The focused sentence gives the paragraph a clear direction: daily walking and two possible benefits. Supporting sentences should explain those benefits rather than suddenly changing to an unrelated topic.

3. Develop the idea with supporting details

Supporting details make the topic sentence understandable and convincing. Depending on the task, you can use:

  • Reasons: explain why a point is true or important.
  • Examples: show what the idea looks like in a real situation.
  • Facts or evidence: use accurate information from a reliable source when research is required.
  • Descriptions: add precise details that help the reader picture an event or object.

After writing a supporting sentence, ask: “Does this help explain my main point?” Remove or move details that do not belong.

4. Connect sentences with transitions

Transitions guide readers through your ideas. Choose a word or phrase that expresses the relationship; do not add transitions mechanically to every sentence.

RelationshipUseful transitions
Adding an ideaalso, in addition, furthermore
Giving an examplefor example, for instance
Showing a resulttherefore, as a result, consequently
Showing contrasthowever, although, on the other hand
Showing sequencefirst, next, finally

5. Build a clear conclusion

A concluding sentence gives the paragraph a sense of completion. It may restate the main point in fresh words or show why the details matter. Do not introduce a completely new argument at the end.

Topic sentence: Preparing a school bag the night before can make mornings calmer.
Supporting details: Students can check their timetable, pack books and find homework before going to sleep.
Conclusion: A few minutes of preparation can help the school day begin in a more organised way.

6. Worked example: a complete paragraph

Topic: Why a class garden can benefit students

A class garden can make science lessons more practical and engaging. Students can observe how seeds germinate, compare the growth of plants and record changes over time. They also learn to share responsibilities such as watering and removing weeds. These activities connect classroom ideas with real observations while encouraging teamwork. For these reasons, a small class garden can become a useful learning space.

Notice: The opening sentence gives one main point. The middle sentences provide examples and explanations. The final sentence closes the paragraph by reinforcing its value.

7. A simple planning method

  1. Read the writing prompt and underline its key instruction.
  2. Write your main point in one sentence.
  3. List two or three details that directly support it.
  4. Put the details in a logical order.
  5. Add transitions only where they improve the connection.
  6. Write a concluding sentence and check that the paragraph stays focused.

8. Independent practice

  1. Write a topic sentence about one benefit of reading for pleasure.
  2. List three details that support your sentence.
  3. Choose a transition that introduces an example.
  4. Write a concluding sentence in different words from your topic sentence.
  5. Read the paragraph aloud and remove any sentence that does not support its main idea.

9. Test Your Understanding

Choose one answer for each question, then select Check Answers. Feedback explains each answer.

1. What is the main job of a topic sentence?

2. Which detail best supports the topic sentence “School libraries help students learn”?

3. Which transition most clearly introduces an example?

4. What should a concluding sentence usually do?

5. Which sentence does NOT belong in a paragraph about the benefits of cycling to school?

10. Printable Worksheet

Write a paragraph of 5–7 sentences on the topic: One change that would make our school more welcoming.

  1. Write a focused topic sentence.
  2. Add at least three relevant supporting details.
  3. Use one or two suitable transitions.
  4. Finish with a concluding sentence.
  5. Underline the topic sentence and circle the transition words.

Suggested answer features

  1. A clear main point about one practical improvement.
  2. Specific details that explain how the change helps students.
  3. Transitions used where they make relationships clearer.
  4. A conclusion that reinforces the same main point.

11. Key Takeaways

  • Keep each paragraph focused on one main idea.
  • Use a topic sentence to guide the reader.
  • Support the idea with relevant reasons, examples or evidence.
  • Use transitions to show relationships between ideas.
  • Conclude clearly and edit for unity, order and sentence accuracy.
