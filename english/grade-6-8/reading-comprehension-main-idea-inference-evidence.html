GRADES 6–8 · ENGLISH · READING

Reading Comprehension: Main Idea, Inference and Evidence

Strong readers do more than recognise words. They identify what a text is mostly about, notice details that develop its ideas, make reasonable inferences and support their answers with evidence.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Beginner to intermediate

What You Will Learn

  • Identify the main idea of a paragraph or passage.
  • Distinguish a main idea from a supporting detail.
  • Make an inference using clues and logical reasoning.
  • Choose relevant evidence from the text to support an answer.
  • Summarise a passage without adding unsupported opinions.

1. Main idea and topic

The topic is the subject a text discusses. The main idea is the most important point the writer makes about that topic. A topic may be expressed in one or two words, while a main idea is usually a complete statement.

Topic: School gardens.
Main idea: School gardens can help students learn science while encouraging teamwork and responsibility.

A useful question is: “What does the writer most want me to understand about this topic?” Avoid choosing a detail that is true but too narrow to represent the whole paragraph.

2. Supporting details

Supporting details explain, illustrate or provide evidence for the main idea. They may include facts, examples, descriptions, reasons or events.

  • A main idea is the central point.
  • Supporting details answer questions such as how, why, when or what happened.
  • A detail should connect clearly to the main idea.
A sentence can be important without being the main idea. Check whether it explains the whole passage or only one part of it.

3. Making inferences

An inference is a reasonable conclusion based on clues in a text and what the reader already knows. Writers do not always state every fact directly.

Text clue: “Mina glanced at the dark clouds, folded her picnic blanket and hurried towards the house.”
Reasonable inference: Mina thinks it may rain soon. The dark clouds and her decision to go indoors support this conclusion.

An inference must be supported by clues. It is not a guess based only on imagination or personal preference.

4. Using text evidence

Evidence is a word, phrase or sentence from the text that supports an answer. A strong response makes a point and then explains the clue that supports it.

Question: Why did Mina go indoors?
Answer: Mina probably expected rain. The text says she noticed dark clouds and hurried towards the house.

When quoting, use only the words needed and keep the original meaning. If you paraphrase, express the idea in your own words without changing it.

5. Read the passage

The Library on the Corner

For years, the small library on Willow Street was quiet after school. Many students walked past its windows without stopping. When the librarian, Mr. Hale, noticed this, he asked a group of students what would make the library more welcoming.

The students suggested a weekly reading club, a table for puzzles and a shelf where people could exchange books. Mr. Hale helped them organise the space. At first, only six students came to the reading club. By the fourth week, the group had grown so much that they needed to move the chairs into a larger room.

Not every idea worked immediately. The puzzle table was often untidy, so the students made a simple schedule for putting the pieces away. They also created short book reviews to help visitors choose what to read next. Soon, younger children began visiting with their families.

The library had not become larger, but it had become more active. The students learned that a welcoming place is not created by one person alone; it grows when people notice a need and work together to meet it.

6. Guided reading analysis

What is the main idea?

The passage shows that students and a librarian worked together to make a quiet library more welcoming and useful to the community.

Which details support it?

  • Students suggested a reading club, puzzle table and book-exchange shelf.
  • The reading club grew from six students to a larger group.
  • Students created a schedule and wrote short book reviews.
  • Younger children began visiting with their families.

What can we infer?

The students became more responsible and involved because they helped organise and improve the library. This is supported by their schedule for tidying the puzzle table and their book reviews.

How can we support an answer?

Use a short, relevant detail from the passage. For example: “The club grew so much that they needed a larger room” supports the idea that more students became interested.

7. A simple summary method

  1. Identify the topic.
  2. Find the main idea that connects the whole passage.
  3. Select two or three important supporting details.
  4. Leave out minor details and repeated information.
  5. Write the summary in your own words without adding new opinions.
Sample summary: Students worked with a librarian to improve their local library. Their ideas attracted more visitors, and they learned to share responsibility for the space.

8. Practice Exercises

Answer using information from the passage.

  1. What was the main problem with the library at the beginning?
  2. Name two ideas the students suggested.
  3. What evidence shows that the reading club became more popular?
  4. What can you infer about the students by the end of the passage?
  5. Write a one- or two-sentence summary of the passage.
Show suggested answers
  1. It was quiet after school and many students walked past without stopping.
  2. Any two: a weekly reading club, a puzzle table or a book-exchange shelf.
  3. By the fourth week, so many students attended that the group needed a larger room.
  4. They became more involved and responsible because they helped organise the space and solve problems.
  5. Students and a librarian worked together to make a quiet library more welcoming. Their ideas brought in more visitors and taught them to share responsibility.

Key Takeaways

  • The topic is the subject; the main idea is the central point about that subject.
  • Supporting details explain or develop the main idea.
  • Inferences combine text clues with logical reasoning.
  • Text evidence supports an answer and should be relevant.
  • A summary gives the main idea and key details in your own words.
