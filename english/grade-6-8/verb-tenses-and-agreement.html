Verb Tenses and Subject–Verb Agreement

Verbs tell what happens and when it happens. Correct verb forms help readers understand time, while subject–verb agreement keeps sentences grammatically consistent.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Choose present, past and future verb forms to match the time of an event.
  • Form simple tenses with regular and common irregular verbs.
  • Make verbs agree with singular and plural subjects.
  • Recognise common agreement traps involving words between the subject and verb.
  • Keep verb tense consistent unless the time changes.

1. What tense tells us

Verb tense places an action or state in time. The simple present describes habits, facts and regular events; the simple past describes completed past events; the simple future describes events expected later.

TenseExampleUse
PresentShe walks to school.Habit or general fact
PastShe walked yesterday.Completed past action
FutureShe will walk tomorrow.Expected future action

Time words are your best clue. Yesterday, last week, ago and last night point to the past. Every day, always, usually and now point to the present. Tomorrow, next week and soon point to the future. When you are unsure, look for the time word first, then choose the verb form that matches it.

The present tense is also used for facts that are always true, even if they are not happening right now. “Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level.” and “The sun rises in the east.” are present-tense sentences about facts, not about what is happening at this moment. In the same way, timetables and habits use the present: “The train leaves at eight every morning.”

2. Regular and irregular verbs

Many regular verbs form the past tense by adding -ed: talk → talked, clean → cleaned. Some verbs are irregular and change form: go → went, write → wrote, take → took, see → saw.

Examples: Today I write a note. Yesterday I wrote a note. Tomorrow I will write a note.

Learn common irregular forms and check unfamiliar verbs in a reliable dictionary.

Regular verbs follow spelling rules when you add -ed. If the verb ends in a single vowel and a single consonant, double the consonant: stop → stopped, plan → planned. If it ends in a consonant plus y, change the y to i: study → studied, carry → carried. If it ends in a silent e, drop the e: like → liked, hope → hoped.

Common irregular verbs to learn by heart include: go → went, see → saw, take → took, write → wrote, eat → ate, run → ran, buy → bought, bring → brought, teach → taught, catch → caught, swim → swam, give → gave. The verb be is the most irregular of all: in the present it is am, is or are, and in the past it is was or were. There is no shortcut — these forms must be memorised, and reading a lot is the best way to meet them again and again.

3. Subject–verb agreement

A verb must agree with its subject in number. In the simple present, a singular third-person subject usually takes a verb ending in -s or -es: “The bird sings.” A plural subject uses the base form: “The birds sing.”

Singular: The player runs. Plural: The players run. Be verb: She is ready; they are ready.

Spelling matters for the third-person singular. Most verbs add -s: play → plays. Verbs ending in -s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x or -o add -es: watch → watches, pass → passes, go → goes. Verbs ending in a consonant plus -y change y to i and add -es: study → studies, fly → flies. But if a vowel comes before the y, just add -s: play → plays, enjoy → enjoys.

Some subjects look plural but are singular. Each, everyone, everybody, somebody, anyone, nobody and no one are always singular, even though they seem to mean many people: “Everyone is welcome.” “Each of the students has a book.” Collective nouns such as team, class and family usually take a singular verb when the group acts as one unit: “The team is ready.”

4. Find the real subject

Words between the subject and verb do not usually change the agreement. Find the main subject, not the nearest noun.

Incorrect: The box of pencils are on the desk.
Correct: The box of pencils is on the desk. The subject is box, which is singular.

With and, two subjects usually form a plural subject: “Mina and Ali are ready.” With or or nor, the verb generally agrees with the nearer subject in standard school grammar: “Either the teacher or the students are presenting.”

Sentences beginning with there follow the same rule, but the subject comes after the verb. “There is a book on the table.” (one book) but “There are three books on the table.” (three books). Do not let the word there fool you — find the noun that follows the verb and match it.

Another trap is a phrase that starts with of. In “The bowl of apples is on the table”, the subject is bowl, not apples, so the verb is singular: is. Cross out the of-phrase in your head, and the real subject becomes clear.

5. Keep tense consistent

Use the same tense for actions that happen in the same time frame. Change tense when the time changes, not randomly.

Inconsistent: Yesterday we visited the museum and see the fossils.
Consistent: Yesterday we visited the museum and saw the fossils.

Time words such as yesterday, now, every day, last week and tomorrow help you choose the correct tense.

Stories are usually told in the past tense from start to finish: “We arrived at the beach, built a sandcastle and watched the sunset.” If you switch to the present in the middle — “We arrived at the beach and watch the sunset” — the reader stumbles, because the time has jumped without a reason.

A tense change is correct when the time really does change, or when you state a fact inside a past-tense story. “Yesterday we visited the museum, which holds old fossils.” is correct: the visit was in the past, but the museum still holds the fossils now. The test is always the same: does the time in the sentence match the time the verb describes?

6. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Adding -ed to an irregular verb. “He goed to school.” is wrong. Correct: “He went to school.” Irregular past forms must be memorised.

Mistake 2: Matching the verb to the nearest noun instead of the subject. “The box of pencils are on the desk.” is wrong — the subject is box. Correct: “The box of pencils is on the desk.”

Mistake 3: Using a plural verb with everyone, each or nobody. “Everyone are welcome.” is wrong. Correct: “Everyone is welcome.” These words are always singular.

Mistake 4: Switching tense in the middle of a sentence. “Yesterday we visited the museum and see the fossils.” is wrong. Correct: “Yesterday we visited the museum and saw the fossils.”

Mistake 5: Forgetting the -s on a third-person singular present verb. “He go to school every day.” is wrong. Correct: “He goes to school every day.”

Mistake 6: “There is” with a plural noun. “There is three books on the table.” is wrong. Correct: “There are three books on the table.”

Worked Examples

  1. Present: The class studies English every day.
  2. Past: The class studied English yesterday.
  3. Future: The class will study English tomorrow.
  4. Agreement: The list of names is on the table. The subject is list, not names.
  5. Present tense, plural subject: “The children play in the park every day.” Children is plural, so the verb stays in its base form, play, with no -s.
  6. Irregular past: the past of bring is brought. “She brought her lunch yesterday.” (Not “bringed”.)
  7. Find the real subject: “The team of players is ready.” The subject is team (singular), not players, so the verb is is.
  8. Fix the tense: “Tomorrow we visit the zoo and saw the lions.” Both actions are in the future, so both verbs must be future: “Tomorrow we will visit the zoo and see the lions.”

Independent Practice

Try each question before revealing the answers.

  1. Change to past tense: They visit the museum.
  2. Change to future tense: I finish my homework.
  3. Choose: The dog (bark / barks) at strangers.
  4. Choose: The players (is / are) ready.
  5. Correct: The basket of apples are heavy.
  6. Correct the tense: Last night we eat dinner and watched a film.
  7. Give the past tense of catch.
  8. Choose: She (watch / watches) TV every evening.
  9. Choose: Each of the students (has / have) a book.
  10. Change to the present tense: They walked to school.
  11. Choose: Neither the boys nor the girl (were / was) late.
  12. Correct the tense: Yesterday she reads a magazine and watched TV.
Show practice answers and explanations
  1. They visited the museum.
  2. I will finish my homework.
  3. barks — dog is singular.
  4. are — players is plural.
  5. The basket of apples is heavy. The subject is basket.
  6. Last night we ate dinner and watched a film.
  7. caught — catch is irregular; it does not take -ed.
  8. watches — she is third-person singular, and watch adds -es.
  9. has — each is always singular.
  10. They walk to school.
  11. was — with neither … nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, girl, which is singular.
  12. Yesterday she read a magazine and watched TV. Both actions are in the past.

Lesson Summary

  • Verb tense places an action or state in time; keep tense consistent unless the meaning changes.
  • Regular verbs usually form the past with -ed, while irregular verbs have different forms.
  • A verb must agree with its subject in number and person.
  • Find the true subject before choosing a verb, especially when phrases come between them.
  • Proofread for tense shifts and subject–verb agreement.
  • Irregular verbs do not take -ed; their past forms must be learnt (go/went, teach/taught, catch/caught).
  • Words like each, everyone and nobody are always singular and take a singular verb.
