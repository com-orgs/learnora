Grades 6–8 · READING

Text Structure and Author’s Purpose

Readers understand a text more deeply when they notice how ideas are organised and why the author chose to present them. Structure is the pattern connecting ideas; purpose is the reason for writing.

Learning time: approximately 30–35 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Recognise description, sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, and problem and solution.
  • Use signal words as clues without relying on them alone.
  • Identify purposes such as informing, explaining, persuading and entertaining.
  • Explain how structure helps readers follow ideas.
  • Support interpretations with details from a passage.

1. Description and sequence

Description groups details about a subject, place or idea. Sequence presents steps or events in order. A wetland description may group details about shallow water, plants and habitats. A recipe uses sequence: first gather ingredients, next mix them, then bake. “First”, “next” and “finally” can signal sequence.

2. Cause and effect

Cause and effect explains why something happens and what follows. Example: Heavy rain increases runoff; runoff may carry soil into streams and make water cloudy. Words such as because, therefore and as a result can help, but check that the relationship is supported. Events happening together do not automatically prove causation.

3. Compare and contrast

Compare means identify similarities; contrast means identify differences. Example: Solar and wind power are renewable sources. Solar panels use sunlight, whereas wind turbines use moving air. Clues include both, similarly, however, whereas and unlike. Identify the features being compared.

4. Problem and solution

A problem-and-solution text identifies a challenge, explains why it matters and presents possible responses. For a playground with little shade, options might include planting suitable trees or installing shade structures. Evaluate cost, safety, maintenance and time.

5. Identify author's purpose

Authors may write to inform with facts, explain how or why something works, persuade readers to accept a position, or entertain through story or humour. A text can have more than one purpose. Ask what the author wants readers to know, understand, feel or do, then support your answer with a detail.

Worked Example

A school compared two ways to reduce lunchtime waste. Reusable trays reduce disposable items, while composting diverts suitable food scraps. Both approaches can help, but trays need washing facilities and composting needs correct sorting. The school should test both options and measure waste collected.

Explain the main idea in your own words before moving to the quiz.

Knowledge Check

Choose one answer for each question, then select Check answers. Read the feedback even when your answer is correct.

1. A recipe uses “first”, “next” and “finally”. Which structure?

2. Strong winds knock down branches and block a road. Which relationship?

3. Which phrase most clearly signals contrast?

4. An article argues that a town should add cycle lanes and gives reasons. Its purpose?

5. How best support an answer about author's purpose?
