Grades 6–8 · WRITING

Argument Writing: Claims, Reasons and Evidence

A strong argument does more than state an opinion. It makes a clear claim, supports that claim with reasons and relevant evidence, considers another point of view and explains why the conclusion follows.

Learning time: approximately 30–35 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Distinguish a topic, opinion and arguable claim.
  • Build reasons that logically support a claim.
  • Choose specific facts, examples or observations as evidence.
  • Acknowledge a counterargument respectfully and respond to it.
  • Organise an introduction, body paragraphs and conclusion.

1. From topic to focused claim

A topic names a subject; a claim states a position that readers could reasonably question. A useful claim is specific and can be supported. Example: “Schools should maintain small gardens because they give students practical science experience and encourage responsibility.” The claim is more useful than “Gardens are great” because it states a position and reasons.

2. Reasons and evidence

A reason explains why a claim makes sense. Evidence supports that reason: a reliable fact, relevant example, investigation result or detail from a text. Example: Claim—schools should provide quiet reading time. Reason—regular reading gives learners time to practise concentration. Evidence—a class reading log can record how consistently students read. Evidence must be relevant and represented accurately.

3. Build a supporting paragraph

Use claim → reason → evidence → explanation. Start with one clear point, give a reason, add a specific example or fact, then explain how it supports the claim. A paragraph should develop one main reason. Use transitions when moving to another reason.

4. Consider another point of view

A counterargument is a reasonable alternative view. Acknowledge it fairly, then respond with evidence or explain a limit. Example: Some students may prefer free time for clubs. A fair response could suggest a short, flexible reading period that does not replace other activities. Do not mock people who disagree.

5. Conclude and revise

Return to the claim in fresh words, bring together the strongest reasons and leave a clear final thought. Do not add an unsupported new argument. Revise for precise vocabulary, varied sentences, accurate punctuation and traceable evidence.

Worked Example

Students should have access to a quiet reading period because it gives them regular time to practise comprehension. A class reading log can help learners record books and reflect on unfamiliar vocabulary. Although some students may prefer more free-choice time, a short flexible period can support reading without replacing clubs or sport.

Explain the main idea in your own words before moving to the quiz.

Independent Practice

Complete these tasks before the quiz. Support your choices with reasons and evidence.

  1. Turn the topic “school gardens” into a focused, arguable claim and give two relevant reasons.
  2. Suggest one reliable source or observation that could support one of your reasons. Explain how it would help a reader evaluate the claim.
  3. Write a fair counterargument to the claim that schools should have a weekly outdoor learning session, then respond respectfully.
Show suggested responses
  1. Example: Schools should maintain small gardens because they provide practical science learning and shared responsibility.
  2. Examples include a recorded class investigation, a school observation log or a relevant report from a reliable education or science organisation. Explain what the evidence shows and how it supports the reason.
  3. Some may worry about weather or lost classroom time. A response could suggest a short session, safe clothing and an indoor alternative.

Knowledge Check

Choose one answer for each question, then select Check answers. Read the feedback even when your answer is correct.

1. Which is the most focused, arguable claim?

2. What is the purpose of evidence?

3. Which is a useful supporting-paragraph structure?

4. What is a counterargument?

5. Which revision is most responsible?
