Sentence Types and Structure

A clear sentence communicates a complete idea. Understanding subjects, predicates and clauses helps you write sentences that are accurate, varied and easy to follow.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Identify the subject and predicate in a sentence.
  • Recognise statements, questions, commands and exclamations by their purpose and punctuation.
  • Distinguish independent clauses from dependent clauses.
  • Classify simple, compound and complex sentences.
  • Correct sentence fragments and run-on sentences.

1. A complete sentence

A complete sentence expresses a complete thought. It normally contains a subject (who or what the sentence is about) and a predicate (what the subject does or is).

Example: The young bird (subject) built a nest (predicate). The sentence communicates a complete idea.

A command can have an understood subject: “Close the door.” The subject you is understood.

2. Four sentence purposes

Sentences can be grouped by what they do:

  • Declarative: makes a statement and usually ends with a full stop. Example: The library opens at nine.
  • Interrogative: asks a question and ends with a question mark. Example: When does the library open?
  • Imperative: gives a command or request. Example: Please return the book.
  • Exclamatory: expresses strong feeling and ends with an exclamation mark. Example: What an exciting match!

The purpose and punctuation should agree. Do not use an exclamation mark for every ordinary statement.

3. Clauses: independent and dependent

A clause contains a subject and a verb. An independent clause expresses a complete thought and can stand alone. A dependent clause does not express a complete thought by itself.

Independent: We stayed indoors. Dependent: because the rain was heavy. Together: We stayed indoors because the rain was heavy.

Words such as because, although, when, if and while often introduce dependent clauses.

4. Simple, compound and complex sentences

  • Simple: one independent clause. Example: The dog barked.
  • Compound: two or more independent clauses joined correctly. Example: The dog barked, and the gate opened.
  • Complex: one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. Example: The dog barked when the gate opened.

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) when it joins two independent clauses. A dependent clause at the beginning is usually followed by a comma: “Although it was late, we continued reading.”

5. Avoid fragments and run-ons

A fragment is an incomplete sentence presented as if it were complete. A run-on joins independent clauses without correct punctuation or a joining word.

Fragment: Because the bus was late.
Complete: Because the bus was late, we arrived after the bell.
Run-on: I finished my work I checked it.
Correct: I finished my work, and I checked it.
Or: I finished my work. I checked it.

Worked Examples

  1. “The team practised.” — one independent clause, so it is simple.
  2. “The team practised, and the coach watched.” — two independent clauses joined by and, so it is compound.
  3. “The team practised because the final was near.” — an independent clause plus a dependent clause, so it is complex.

Independent Practice

Try each question before revealing the answers.

  1. Identify the subject in: The bright moon lit the path.
  2. Classify: Please switch off the lights.
  3. Is “Although the shop was closed” a complete sentence?
  4. Classify: We packed our bags, and we left early.
  5. Classify: When the bell rang, the students entered.
  6. Correct this run-on: I like science it explains the world.
Show practice answers and explanations
  1. The bright moon.
  2. Imperative (a request or command).
  3. No. It is a dependent clause and needs an independent clause.
  4. Compound sentence.
  5. Complex sentence.
  6. I like science because it explains the world. (A full stop between the clauses is also correct.)

Knowledge Check

Choose one answer for each question, then check your answers. Each question is worth one point.

1. Which part tells what the subject does or is?

2. Which sentence is interrogative?

3. Which is a dependent clause?

4. Two independent clauses joined with a comma and a coordinating conjunction make a…

5. Which sentence is complex?
