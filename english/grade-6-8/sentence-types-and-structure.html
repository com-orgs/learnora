Sentence Types and Structure

A clear sentence communicates a complete idea. Understanding subjects, predicates and clauses helps you write sentences that are accurate, varied and easy to follow.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Curriculum-neutral learning support

What You Will Learn

  • Identify the subject and predicate in a sentence.
  • Recognise statements, questions, commands and exclamations by their purpose and punctuation.
  • Distinguish independent clauses from dependent clauses.
  • Classify simple, compound and complex sentences.
  • Correct sentence fragments and run-on sentences.

1. A complete sentence

A complete sentence expresses a complete thought. It normally contains a subject (who or what the sentence is about) and a predicate (what the subject does or is).

Example: The young bird (subject) built a nest (predicate). The sentence communicates a complete idea.

A command can have an understood subject: “Close the door.” The subject you is understood.

To find the subject, ask: “Who or what is the sentence about?” Then ask: “What about it?” The answer to the first question is the subject; the answer to the second is the predicate. In “The old lighthouse warned the ships”, the sentence is about the old lighthouse, so that is the subject, and “warned the ships” is the predicate.

Subjects and predicates can be compound. In “Mina and Ali walked home”, two subjects share one predicate. In “The dog barked and wagged its tail”, one subject has two actions in its predicate. A complete sentence can be short — “Birds sing.” has a subject (birds) and a predicate (sing) — and it can be long, as long as it still expresses one complete thought.

2. Four sentence purposes

Sentences can be grouped by what they do:

  • Declarative: makes a statement and usually ends with a full stop. Example: The library opens at nine.
  • Interrogative: asks a question and ends with a question mark. Example: When does the library open?
  • Imperative: gives a command or request. Example: Please return the book.
  • Exclamatory: expresses strong feeling and ends with an exclamation mark. Example: What an exciting match!

The purpose and punctuation should agree. Do not use an exclamation mark for every ordinary statement.

The same words can have different purposes. “You are coming.” states a fact. “You are coming?” asks a question. “You are coming!” shows surprise. The words are the same; the punctuation and the purpose change the meaning, so always read a sentence and ask what the writer is doing with it.

Imperative sentences are often polite. “Please return the book.” is still a command, even though it begins with “please”. A request is not a question just because it sounds gentle — it only becomes interrogative if it asks something and ends with a question mark, such as “Could you return the book?”

3. Clauses: independent and dependent

A clause contains a subject and a verb. An independent clause expresses a complete thought and can stand alone. A dependent clause does not express a complete thought by itself.

Independent: We stayed indoors. Dependent: because the rain was heavy. Together: We stayed indoors because the rain was heavy.

Words such as because, although, when, if and while often introduce dependent clauses.

Other subordinating words include since, unless, before, after and as. Relative clauses are also dependent: in “The book that I borrowed was interesting”, the clause “that I borrowed” cannot stand alone, even though it contains a subject (I) and a verb (borrowed). Words such as who, which and that often begin relative clauses.

Use the stand-alone test whenever you are unsure. Cover the rest of the sentence and read the clause on its own. If it makes sense by itself — “We stayed indoors.” — it is independent. If it leaves you waiting for more — “Because the rain was heavy…” — it is dependent and needs a main clause to finish the thought.

4. Simple, compound and complex sentences

  • Simple: one independent clause. Example: The dog barked.
  • Compound: two or more independent clauses joined correctly. Example: The dog barked, and the gate opened.
  • Complex: one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. Example: The dog barked when the gate opened.

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) when it joins two independent clauses. A dependent clause at the beginning is usually followed by a comma: “Although it was late, we continued reading.”

There is one more structure: the compound-complex sentence. It has two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause. Example: “The dog barked, and the gate opened when the wind blew.” The two main clauses are “The dog barked” and “the gate opened”, joined by and; “when the wind blew” is the dependent clause.

To remember the coordinating conjunctions, use the word FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. A semicolon can also join two independent clauses without a conjunction: “The dog barked; the gate opened.” Both versions are correct; the semicolon simply makes the link tighter.

5. Avoid fragments and run-ons

A fragment is an incomplete sentence presented as if it were complete. A run-on joins independent clauses without correct punctuation or a joining word.

Fragment: Because the bus was late.
Complete: Because the bus was late, we arrived after the bell.
Run-on: I finished my work I checked it.
Correct: I finished my work, and I checked it.
Or: I finished my work. I checked it.

A comma splice is a common kind of run-on: two independent clauses joined by only a comma. “The bus was late, we missed the start.” is a comma splice. Fix it in one of three ways: use a full stop (“The bus was late. We missed the start.”), add a conjunction (“The bus was late, so we missed the start.”), or use a semicolon (“The bus was late; we missed the start.”).

Fragments come in several forms. A fragment may be missing a subject (“Ran to the shop.” — who ran?), missing a verb (“The tall tree in the garden.” — what about it?), or be a dependent clause on its own (“After the match ended.”). In each case, add the missing part or join the fragment to a complete sentence.

6. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Treating a dependent clause as a sentence. “Because it rained.” is not a complete sentence. Correct: “Because it rained, the match was cancelled.”

Mistake 2: Joining two sentences with only a comma. “The bus was late, we missed the start.” is a comma splice. Correct: “The bus was late, so we missed the start.”

Mistake 3: Calling a sentence compound when “and” joins a dependent clause. “She stayed because she was tired.” is complex, not compound — “because she was tired” cannot stand alone.

Mistake 4: Forgetting the comma after a fronted dependent clause. Wrong: “When the bell rang the students stood up.” Correct: “When the bell rang, the students stood up.”

Mistake 5: Using exclamation marks for ordinary statements. “The shop opens at nine!” should be “The shop opens at nine.” Save exclamation marks for real surprise or strong feeling.

Mistake 6: Thinking a polite request is a question. “Please close the window.” is imperative, not interrogative. It only becomes a question if it asks something: “Could you close the window?”

Worked Examples

  1. “The team practised.” — one independent clause, so it is simple.
  2. “The team practised, and the coach watched.” — two independent clauses joined by and, so it is compound.
  3. “The team practised because the final was near.” — an independent clause plus a dependent clause, so it is complex.
  4. “The rain stopped, but the streets stayed wet.” — two independent clauses (“The rain stopped” and “the streets stayed wet”) joined by but, so it is compound.
  5. “Although the rain stopped, the streets stayed wet.” — the dependent clause “Although the rain stopped” plus the independent clause “the streets stayed wet”, so it is complex. Note the comma after the fronted dependent clause.
  6. Find the subject and predicate in “The old lighthouse warned the ships.” Ask: “Who or what is the sentence about?” — the old lighthouse. Ask: “What about it?” — warned the ships. Subject: “The old lighthouse”; predicate: “warned the ships”.
  7. Fix the fragment “After the match ended.” It is a dependent clause, so add a main clause: “After the match ended, the players shook hands.”
  8. Fix the comma splice “The shop was closed, we went home.” Two independent clauses need more than a comma: “The shop was closed, so we went home.” A full stop between the clauses is also correct.

Try each question before revealing the answers.

  1. Identify the subject in: The bright moon lit the path.
  2. Classify: Please switch off the lights.
  3. Is “Although the shop was closed” a complete sentence?
  4. Classify: We packed our bags, and we left early.
  5. Classify: When the bell rang, the students entered.
  6. Correct this run-on: I like science it explains the world.
  7. Identify the predicate in: The river flooded the fields.
  8. Classify: Did the train arrive on time?
  9. Is “Running through the park” a complete sentence? Explain your answer.
  10. Classify: The baker opened the shop, and customers came in.
  11. Join these two sentences with because: “The match was cancelled.” “It rained all day.”
  12. Correct the comma splice: The library is quiet, we must whisper.
Show practice answers and explanations
  1. The bright moon.
  2. Imperative (a request or command).
  3. No. It is a dependent clause and needs an independent clause.
  4. Compound sentence.
  5. Complex sentence.
  6. I like science because it explains the world. (A full stop between the clauses is also correct.)
  7. “flooded the fields” — the predicate says what the river did.
  8. Interrogative — it asks a question and ends with a question mark.
  9. No. It is a fragment: there is no subject and no complete verb (running is a participle, not a main verb).
  10. Compound sentence — two independent clauses joined by and.
  11. “The match was cancelled because it rained all day.” The second sentence becomes a dependent clause, so the result is complex.
  12. “The library is quiet, so we must whisper.” A full stop between the clauses is also correct.

Lesson Summary

  • A complete sentence has a subject and a predicate and expresses a complete thought.
  • Sentence purpose may be declarative, interrogative, imperative or exclamatory.
  • Independent clauses can stand alone; dependent clauses need a main clause.
  • Simple, compound and complex structures help writers vary sentence patterns.
  • Edit fragments and run-ons so every sentence is clear.
  • A compound-complex sentence has two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.
  • The FANBOYS words (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) join independent clauses in compound sentences.
