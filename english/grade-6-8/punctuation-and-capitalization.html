GRADES 6–8 · ENGLISH

Punctuation and Capitalization

Punctuation helps readers understand how ideas fit together. Capital letters show where sentences begin and identify names and titles. Learn the main rules, study examples, and practise editing sentences.

Learning time: approximately 25–30 minutes · Beginner to intermediate

What You Will Learn

  • Use full stops, question marks and exclamation marks.
  • Place commas in lists and after introductory words or phrases.
  • Use apostrophes for contractions and possession.
  • Use quotation marks for direct speech and quotations.
  • Recognise when colons and semicolons are appropriate.
  • Capitalise sentence beginnings, proper nouns, days, months and titles.

1. End punctuation

Choose an end mark that matches the purpose of the sentence.

  • Full stop (.) ends a statement: The library closes at six.
  • Question mark (?) ends a direct question: When does the library close?
  • Exclamation mark (!) shows strong feeling or emphasis: What an amazing discovery!
Compare: “You finished the project.” gives information. “Did you finish the project?” asks a question. “You finished the project!” expresses excitement or surprise.
Do not add a question mark to an indirect question: “She asked when the library closes.”

2. Commas

Commas create pauses and separate parts of a sentence so the meaning is clear.

Commas in a list

Use commas to separate items: We packed pencils, notebooks, rulers and glue. A comma before “and” in a list is called the Oxford comma. Style guides differ on whether it is required; use the style your school or publication follows.

After introductory words or phrases

Use a comma after an introductory phrase when it helps readers see where the main sentence begins.

After the match, the players thanked their coach.
However, the weather changed quickly.

To separate extra information

Commas can set off information that is not essential to identify the noun.

My aunt, who lives in Toronto, is visiting next week.
A comma alone should not join two complete sentences. This error is called a comma splice. Use a full stop, a semicolon where suitable, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction.

3. Apostrophes

Apostrophes have two common jobs: showing missing letters in contractions and showing possession.

UseExampleMeaning
Contractiondo not → don'tLetters have been omitted.
Singular possessionthe student's bookThe book belongs to one student.
Plural possession ending in sthe students' booksThe books belong to multiple students.
Irregular plural possessionthe children's gamesChildren is plural but does not end in s.
Its usually shows possession (“The dog wagged its tail”). It's means “it is” or “it has”. Do not use an apostrophe to make an ordinary plural.

4. Quotation marks

Quotation marks show a person's exact words or a quotation from a source. In many styles, commas and full stops go inside closing quotation marks, but conventions vary between regions and style guides.

Maya said, “I have finished my assignment.”
“Are you ready?” asked Leo.

Start a new paragraph when a different speaker begins in a longer passage of dialogue. Use quotation marks consistently and do not put them around words merely for decoration.

5. Colons and semicolons

A colon (:) can introduce an explanation, example or list after a complete introductory clause.

Bring three things: a notebook, a pen and a ruler.

A semicolon (;) can connect two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction.

The rain stopped; the match continued.

A semicolon can also separate complex list items that already contain commas.

Do not place a colon directly between a verb or preposition and its object: “The items are: pens and books” is usually better written “The items are pens and books.”

6. Capitalization

Use a capital letter in these situations:

  • At the beginning of a sentence: The lesson starts now.
  • For the pronoun I: Sam and I walked home.
  • For proper nouns: Amira, London, Lake Victoria.
  • For days, months and languages: Monday, October, English.
  • For titles and names of organisations, following the relevant style: World Health Organization.

Common nouns such as “city”, “teacher” and “river” are not normally capitalised unless they begin a sentence or form part of a proper name.

7. Editing a sentence

Read this sentence with missing punctuation and capitals:

on saturday maya asked can we visit the science museum

A corrected version is:

On Saturday, Maya asked, “Can we visit the science museum?”

Check the sentence in stages: capitalise the first word and proper names, identify the direct speech, add quotation marks, and place the question mark inside the quotation marks because the spoken words are a question.

Key Takeaways

  • Choose end punctuation to match the sentence's purpose.
  • Use commas to separate items and make sentence structure clear.
  • Use apostrophes for contractions and possession, not ordinary plurals.
  • Use quotation marks consistently for exact speech and quotations.
  • Capitalise sentence beginnings and proper nouns.
  • Read your writing aloud and proofread it before sharing.
