FINANCIAL LITERACY · GRADES 6–8

Money Skills for Everyday Life

Financial literacy means understanding everyday money concepts and using them to make informed decisions. Students can begin with simple skills: planning how money is used, setting savings goals, comparing prices, understanding percentages and recognising unsafe requests for personal information.

Examples on this page use currency-neutral units so students in different countries can practise the same skills. This is general education, not personal financial advice.

Six money skills to build

1. Needs and wants

A need is something essential for health or safety; a want is something desirable but not essential. Context matters, so explain the reason for each choice.

2. Make a simple budget

List the money available, planned spending and savings. Check that planned spending does not exceed the amount available.

3. Set a savings goal

Choose a goal, estimate its cost, decide how much to save each week and track progress. Small regular amounts can add up.

4. Compare prices

Compare the amount, quantity, quality and any extra costs. Unit price can help compare different package sizes.

5. Understand percentages

Use percentage skills to understand discounts, tax examples and simple interest calculations in classroom problems.

6. Protect your money and identity

Recognise suspicious messages, protect passwords, avoid sharing one-time codes and ask a trusted adult before responding to unusual payment requests.

Worked example: build a weekly budget

Suppose a student has 20 units available for a week. They plan to use 8 units for transport, 5 for food and school supplies, and save 4 units.

Planned total: 8 + 5 + 4 = 17 units.
Amount remaining: 20 − 17 = 3 units.
The plan fits within the available amount, with 3 units not yet allocated. The student can keep it as a buffer or assign it to another planned purpose.

This is a classroom example only. Real budgets depend on a person's circumstances and local costs.

Questions students often ask about money

What is a budget?

A budget is a plan for how available money will be used. It helps compare money coming in with planned spending and saving.

Why is saving important?

Saving helps people prepare for a goal or an unexpected cost. A useful first step is to set a realistic target and track progress over time.

How do I compare two offers?

Check the quantity, unit price, quality, delivery or other extra costs, and whether you actually need the item. A lower headline price is not always the better value.
